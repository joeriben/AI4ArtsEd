## 2. What the controls did

- Prompt field: the example or free text; a picture dropped on it is described into text (not tried).
- "+": the round chooser; one perspective at a time on this surface; the bubble can be replaced by pressing "+" again.
- Writing models chips: a set of language models that each rewrite the prompt under the perspective; Open Weights gave Mistral Large 4 and GLM-5.3, Closed Weights gave Claude Sonnet 5 and GPT-5.6 Terra; "model family" and "Custom" not tried. Choosing another set puts its rows on top and moves what has run down; the old rows keep their text and their picture and are not run again.
- Image model chips: the model that draws every row with the same seed.
- Run the series: rewrites, then draws the reference (the prompt unchanged) and each rewrite.
- Cells: the rewritten prompt in full (scrolling inside the cell), the picture, and a waiting text where no picture is.
- Interpretation help, Hide column: not used. Help: opens by itself on the first arrival with the page's text quoted in 1; every later click shows that text again.

## 6. What the help text should tell a user

The Help panel's own text, which opens on arrival, is quoted in 1: it says that Prompt Interception is the step in which the platform writes a prompt over again through a perspective, that the step runs here once per writing model on the same prompt, that one image model puts a picture beside each text, that the top row holds the prompt as written with the step left out, and that between two rows the writing model is the only difference. It should say what there is to read in that: the perspective is a way of speaking about the same thing, and four models speak it four ways. The one used here, "Sensitive", instructs the writer to perceive "the INPUT in and from the in-between", to attend to "relations, transitions, thresholds, and subtle shifts", to translate this "into an aesthetic form of expression of your own choosing, which you clearly name", and to avoid "Fixations, clear centers, rigid hierarchies, or final resolutions"; the four texts in 3 are four answers to that instruction, a platinum-palladium print, a watercolour, two 16 mm films, and the pictures beside them are what one image model made of each answer. The reading is of texts as texts: the aesthetic form each model chose and named, what of the sentence it kept (the house, the people, the animals), what it dropped, what it added, and the register it wrote in; and of the pictures as the trace of that text in an image model. The help should also say: the chips' tooltips name the lineup's members, and the rows show which of them this installation runs; choosing another lineup moves what has run down and keeps it; the perspective's own words, where they reached a text, are marked in it (the view carries this marking; I did not notice it in use); where a rewrite is not in English the platform translates it before drawing; Flux 2 is preselected because it reads a long prompt to its end, while SD 3.5 is anchored at CLIP's 77 tokens and shows what stands at the beginning (the view's own reasoning for the default); a picture cell that still says "Wait for this run to finish" after the series has ended belongs to a lost run, and running the series again fills it.

## 8. Teaching scenarios

Prompt Interception is the platform's own step: the idea stays, and a perspective gives the way of speaking about it. This surface holds the idea and the perspective still and lets the writer vary, so what a language model adds to a way of speaking becomes readable as its share. The judgement is an aesthetic and a cultural one: which of four texts carries the perspective, which the sentence, which neither; and where a perspective names a tradition, whether the model knows it or writes its cliché. The pictures are the simplification that makes the texts comparable at a glance, and material for the next step.

**S1 — Four writers, one perspective** (formal, art, grades 9–13, 90 min). Pairs choose one perspective from the arts or aesthetics group and run the example prompt under Open and Closed Weights; they print the four texts and mark in each what the prompt said, what the perspective asked for, and what the model added; then they name the aesthetic form each model chose (a platinum-palladium print, a watercolour, two 16 mm films) and judge which text they would call written through the perspective and which merely decorated. The pictures are compared last, for what of each form the image model kept. Sequence: 10 min choice; 15 min runs; 30 min marking and naming; 25 min judgement; 10 min pictures. Subject link: perspective and voice, paraphrase, style against manner. Uncertainty: the closed models are paid cloud services; a lesson needs the installation's keys.

**S2 — Does the model know the Bauhaus?** (formal, art, grades 10–13, 90 min). The class researches one tradition the arts group names (Bauhaus, the Literati painters, the Renaissance) from a textbook or a museum's page before the run, and writes down three things that belong to it and three that a cliché of it would add. Then the example prompt runs under that perspective on four models. The pairs read each text against their list: which model wrote the tradition's own forms and which wrote the cliché; the pictures show what of either reached the image model. The judgement rests on what the class knows about the tradition, and the model has no say in it. Sequence: 20 min research and lists; 15 min runs; 35 min reading; 20 min plenary. Subject link: art history, the cliché as a form; knowing a tradition as the basis of judgement. Uncertainty: not run; a lineup may be partly unavailable.

**S3 — Your own perspective** (formal, art or language teaching, grades 10–13, 2 × 45 min). Pairs write a perspective of their own with "Your Call!" from the freestyle group: a way of speaking they want, in their own words (a manner, a mood, a tradition they know). The example prompt runs under it on four models; the pairs read which model did what they meant, where the instruction was too vague to bind and where too narrow, and rewrite it; second run, same seed. The two runs are compared for what the rewriting of the instruction changed in four writers at once. Sequence: 15 min writing; 15 min run and reading; 15 min rewriting; 15 min second run; 30 min comparison. Subject link: writing an instruction for a way of speaking; the author of the rule. Uncertainty: I saw "Your Call!" in the chooser and did not run it.

**S4 — Who appears in the rewrite?** (formal, ethics or social studies, grades 10–13, 45 min). The class reads the note's figures (Fang et al.), then runs a prompt with people in it under "Sensitive" and "Forceful" and lists who the rewrites name, with what age, gender and work, across four models, and who disappears (the people were "soft vertical streaks" and a reserved sliver of paper). Subject link: representation in text, the note's own measure.

**S5 — Open against closed** (formal, social studies, grades 10–13, 45 min). One perspective, the two lineups; the class reads the four texts for register, length, medium and for what each set leaves out, and puts beside each row what the register says about the model (maker, country, year, size, open or closed weights). The question for the plenary is what the two kinds of model have in common in their writing and what separates them, and whether the difference lies with the makers or with the sizes. Subject link: who makes the models, open and closed weights. Uncertainty: the two open models wrote still media and the two closed models wrote film; four texts carry no generalisation.

**S6 — The lost run** (non-formal, maker space or teacher workshop, 30 min add-on). Participants run a series, and the facilitator shows what the cells say when a picture does not arrive and how to tell a queue from a lost run. Subject link: reading an instrument's error states. Uncertainty: cannot be staged on demand.

**(b) Mutatis mutandis**

--

**Beyond the framework**: the framework's Create 2 example compares tools for varied interpretations and stops at the variety. Here the comparison is a judgement: whether a model carried a perspective, and whether it knows a tradition or writes its cliché, and that judgement rests on what the class knows about the Bauhaus or the Literati and on its ear for a manner against a decoration. The instrument puts the platform's own step, Prompt Interception, under observation and lets the pupils write the rule a model has to speak by (S3). The framework has no competence for knowing a tradition as the basis of judging a text, and none for authoring the way of speaking a model follows.
