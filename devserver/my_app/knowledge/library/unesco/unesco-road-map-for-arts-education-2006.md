# Road Map for Arts Education

## PROFIL

The "Road Map for Arts Education" was written by UNESCO as an outcome of the UNESCO World Conference on Arts Education: Building Creative Capacities for the 21st Century, held in Lisbon from 6 to 9 March 2006, and is addressed to all stakeholder groups in arts education — educators, parents, artists, school directors, government ministries, policy makers, and intergovernmental and non-governmental organizations. Its subject is the contribution of Arts Education to creativity and cultural awareness in the twenty-first century, and its position is that creative and cultural development ought to be a foundational function of education: the arts are valuable both as disciplines in themselves and as vehicles for learning across the curriculum, and they belong to every learner rather than a talented few. The document identifies four aims — upholding the human right to education and cultural participation, developing individual capabilities, improving the quality of education, and promoting the expression of cultural diversity — then defines concepts (arts fields, approaches including Arts in Education, and three pedagogical dimensions), sets out essential strategies for teacher and artist education and for partnerships at ministerial, school and teacher levels, and addresses research and knowledge-sharing through clearinghouses or "Observatories". It closes with a concluding vision, recommendations grouped by stakeholder, and an annex of case studies from Papua New Guinea, Canada, the United Kingdom, Lithuania, Colombia, Norway, the Republic of Korea, the USA and Spain, Australia, Finland, and UNESCO's own Young Digital Creators project. It builds on the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the Convention on the Rights of the Child, the UNESCO Universal Declaration on Cultural Diversity (2001), the Dakar Framework for Action and the EFA Global Monitoring Report 2006, and it invokes Antonio Damasio on emotional processing and Sir Ken Robinson on imagination, creativity and innovation; it also refers to existing US standards for art teacher preparation. It is usable across performing arts, literature and poetry, craft, design, digital arts, storytelling, heritage, visual arts and film, media and photography, in both formal and non-formal education, and in situations ranging from primary and secondary schooling to work with marginalized groups, indigenous peoples, disabled children, and post-disaster and post-conflict contexts.

## WISSEN

### Background (p. 2–3)

The "Road Map for Arts Education" grew out of the UNESCO World Conference on Arts Education: Building Creative Capacities for the 21st Century, held in Lisbon from 6 to 9 March 2006. It examines how Arts Education can answer the twenty-first century's demand for creativity and cultural awareness, and it sets out the strategies required to introduce or strengthen it within formal and non-formal learning environments.

The document is intended to build a shared understanding, across every stakeholder group, of why Arts Education matters and of its essential contribution to better-quality education; to define concepts and pinpoint good practice; and to work as a living reference that spells out the concrete changes and steps needed to embed the arts in schools and education systems. It further seeks to articulate a vision, forge consensus on Arts Education's value for a creative and culturally aware society, stimulate joint reflection and action, and mobilize the funding and personnel required for fuller integration.

The Road Map confronts long-standing debates in the field: whether the arts are taught purely for appreciation or should also strengthen learning in other subjects; whether art is a discipline valued in itself or a source of knowledge, skills and values (or both); and whether it serves a talented few or everyone. Its comprehensive answer stresses that creative and cultural development ought to be a foundational function of education.

### The Aims of Arts Education (p. 3–7)

Four aims are identified:

1. **Uphold the human right to education and cultural participation.** International declarations and conventions guarantee every child and adult education and opportunities for full, harmonious development and for taking part in artistic and cultural life; these rights underpin the case for making Arts Education a compulsory element of every country's educational programme. It is a universal human right belonging to every learner, including groups commonly excluded from education — immigrants, cultural minorities, people with disabilities. The document quotes the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Article 22: economic, social and cultural rights indispensable for dignity and free development of personality; Article 26: education directed to full development of the human personality, understanding, tolerance and peace; Article 27: the right freely to take part in the cultural life of the community and enjoy the arts) and the Convention on the Rights of the Child (Article 29: developing the child's personality, talents and abilities to their fullest potential; Article 31: the child's right to full participation in cultural and artistic life, with appropriate and equal opportunities for recreation, leisure, and cultural and artistic activity).

2. **Develop individual capabilities.** Everyone possesses creative potential, and the arts offer a setting in which learners actively engage in creative experiences and processes. Research shows that introducing learners to artistic processes while drawing on their own culture nurtures creativity and initiative, a vivid imagination, emotional intelligence and a moral "compass", critical reflection, autonomy, and independence of thought and action. Learning in and through the arts also stimulates cognitive development and makes what and how learners learn more relevant to modern societies, while offering unique perspectives on a wide range of subjects that no other educational route can provide. Because the goal is equal opportunity for cultural and artistic activity for all people, artistic education must be compulsory for all, systematic, and sustained across several years, given its long-term nature. Arts Education integrates physical, intellectual and creative faculties and enables more dynamic, fruitful relations among education, culture and the arts. The document invokes Professor Antonio Damasio: modern learning environments privilege cognitive skill-building over emotional processes, producing a growing divide between cognitive and emotional processing, and this imbalance, he argues, helps explain declining moral behavior in modern society; since emotional processing is integral to decision-making and acts as a vector for actions and ideas, establishing reflection and judgement, and since sound moral behavior — the solid grounding of the citizen — requires emotional participation, Arts Education, by fostering emotional development, can rebalance the two and support a culture of peace. Further arguments: twenty-first-century economies increasingly need workforces that are creative, flexible, adaptable and innovative, and education systems must evolve accordingly; Arts Education supplies these capacities, letting learners express themselves, critically evaluate the world and engage actively with human existence. It also enables nations to build the human resources needed to exploit their cultural capital and sustain strong cultural (creative) industries and enterprises — publishing, music, film, television and other media — which in many less-developed countries can drive socio-economic development and which, together with cultural institutions (museums, music venues, cultural centres, galleries, theatres), are most people's main gateways to culture. Arts Education helps people discover and critically respond to what these industries and institutions offer, while the industries themselves become a resource for educators.

3. **Improve the quality of education.** The EFA Global Monitoring Report 2006 shows access to education expanding while quality stays low in most countries; access alone is not enough — students need education of good quality too. "Quality education" is learner-centred and rests on three principles: relevance to the learner combined with promotion of universal values; equity of access and outcomes, guaranteeing inclusion rather than exclusion; and reflection and fulfilment of individual rights. In general terms, it equips all young people and other learners with locally relevant abilities to function successfully in their societies; fits students' lives, aspirations and interests and those of their families and societies; and is inclusive and rights-based. Under the Dakar Framework for Action, learning in and through the arts strengthens at least four prerequisites of quality: active learning; a locally-relevant curriculum that captures learners' interest and enthusiasm; engagement with and respect for local communities and cultures; and well-trained, motivated teachers.

4. **Promote the expression of cultural diversity.** Art both manifests culture and communicates cultural knowledge; each culture owns unique artistic expressions, and this diversity — contemporary and traditional forms of human creativity — contributes uniquely to the nobility, beauty, heritage and integrity of civilizations. Arts Education strengthens personal and collective identities and values, safeguards and promotes diversity, and passes appreciation of arts and culture from one generation to the next. Because many countries are losing tangible and intangible culture that schools neither value nor transmit to future generations, education systems must incorporate it — achievable through Arts Education in formal and non-formal settings. Main Lines of Action from the UNESCO Universal Declaration on Cultural Diversity (2001) reinforce this: Action Line 6 — encouraging, wherever possible, linguistic diversity at all education levels while respecting the mother tongue, and fostering the learning of several languages from the earliest age; Action Line 7 — using education to build awareness of cultural diversity's positive value, improving curriculum design and teacher education accordingly; Action Line 8 — incorporating traditional pedagogies, where appropriate, to preserve and fully use culturally appropriate ways of communicating and transmitting knowledge.

### Concepts Related to Arts Education (p. 7–8)

**Arts fields.** All cultures seek answers to questions of existence and share the resulting insights through the basic elements of communication — words, movement, touch, sound, rhythm and image; the expressions that convey insight and provoke reflection are called "art". History has attached labels to various types of art expression, and although words like "dance", "music", "drama" and "poetry" are used worldwide, their deeper meanings differ from culture to culture, so any inventory of arts fields is pragmatic, evolving and never exclusive. A tentative list comprises:

- performing arts (dance, drama, music, etc.)
- literature and poetry
- craft
- design
- digital arts
- storytelling
- heritage
- visual arts and film
- media
- photography

The arts should reach learners gradually through practice and experience, valuing the process as much as the product; because many art forms cross disciplines, their interdisciplinary character and shared features deserve greater emphasis.

**Approaches to Arts Education.** Imagination, creativity and innovation exist in every person and can be nurtured; the three processes are tightly linked. Following Sir Ken Robinson: imagination is the defining feature of human intelligence, creativity applies imagination, and innovation completes the process by bringing critical judgement to bear on an idea's application. Any approach must start from the learner's own culture(s): confidence grounded in deep appreciation of one's own culture is the ideal springboard for exploring, then respecting, the cultures of others, while acknowledging culture's perpetual evolution in both historical and contemporary contexts. Content and structure should both reflect each art form's characteristics and provide artistic means for communicating and interacting across cultural, social and historical contexts. Two main (compatible, non-exclusive) approaches exist: (1) teaching the arts as separate subjects, building students' skills, sensitivity and appreciation; (2) treating the arts as a teaching-and-learning method in which artistic and cultural dimensions enter all curriculum subjects. The **Arts in Education (AiE)** approach treats the arts — and their associated practices and cultural traditions — as a vehicle for teaching and deepening general curriculum subjects (for instance, colours, forms and objects from visual arts and architecture to teach physics, biology or geometry; drama or music as a way into languages). Grounded in the theory of "multiple intelligences", AiE extends the benefits of Arts Education to every student and subject and contextualizes theory through practical application of artistic disciplines; it demands changes in teaching methods and teacher training.

**Dimensions of Arts Education.** Three complementary pedagogical streams structure the field: studying artistic works; practising the arts; and direct encounters with artistic works (concerts, exhibitions, books, films). The student thus gains knowledge (1) through interaction with the artwork or performance, the artist and the teacher; (2) through his or her own practice of art; and (3) through study and research — of an art form and of its relation to history.

### Essential Strategies for Effective Arts Education (p. 8–12)

Effective Arts Education of high quality depends on professionally skilled art teachers and generalist teachers, reinforced by partnerships with accomplished artists. Two objectives follow: equipping teachers, artists and others with the materials and education they need ("Creative learning needs creative teaching"), and fostering creative partnerships at every level linking Ministries, schools and teachers with arts, science and community organizations. Partnerships succeed when the partners share an understanding of their goals and respect each other's competencies; educators and artists should therefore enter their professions with insight into the other's field, including a shared interest in pedagogy. Teacher and artist education programmes need revision so that both groups can share responsibility for facilitating learning and exploit the outcomes of cross-professional cooperation — specific arrangements that challenge most societies. Two essential strategies follow: relevant, effective education of teachers and artists, and partnership-building between education and cultural systems and actors.

#### 1. Education of teachers and artists (p. 9–11)

- **Teachers of general subjects**: in the best case, teachers and school administrators appreciate artists' values and qualities and are equipped to work with them in schools; this lets them realize their own potential and use the arts in teaching. Ideally they can also produce or perform art, analyse, interpret and evaluate artworks, and appreciate works from other periods and cultures. Primary teachers frequently adopt the AiE approach — songs for memorizing vocabulary, scientific definitions or mathematical formulas — which can relieve curriculum overload, but integration fails unless the arts are also specifically taught alongside it.
- **Arts teachers**: teaching the arts reaches beyond transmitting skills, practices and bodies of knowledge. Beyond studio competency, programmes should broaden teacher preparation; art teachers should tap the skills of artists, including those from other disciplines, and learn to work with artists and with subject teachers in schools. Comprehensive arts teacher education may build knowledge and skills in:
  - one or more arts disciplines
  - interdisciplinary arts expression
  - methodologies for teaching the arts
  - methodologies for interdisciplinary teaching in and through the arts
  - curriculum design
  - assessment and evaluation appropriate for arts education
  - formal (school-based) arts education
  - informal (community-based) arts education

  Schools alone are never sufficient: partnerships with community individuals and organizations — museum and gallery visits, live performance attendance, Artists in School (AIS) programmes, and Environmental Education through Arts Education — enrich learning for teachers and students alike. New technologies (artistic creation with new technologies, electronic music, new media, online teaching) have widened Arts Education's scope and created new roles for art teachers in the 21st century, and can serve as a collaboration platform linking art teachers with artists, scientists and other educators. Yet computer art, though now accepted as an art form, a legitimate mode of art production and a teaching method, is rarely taught in schools, because motivated teachers often lack experience, pedagogical preparation and resources. Secondary subject teachers can set tasks requiring collaboration across specializations — business and technology applied to the commercial side of the arts, or projects linking the arts with history or social studies — which presupposes that subject teachers value Arts Education. Finally, guidelines and standards for pre-service art teacher preparation should be established locally and perhaps nationally; existing standards — developed mainly in the USA, such as the NBPTS Early Adolescence through Young Adulthood Art standards, Standards for Art Teacher Preparation, Purposes, Principles, and Standards for School Art Programmes, and The National Visual Arts Standards — can serve as reference points for planning, implementing and evaluating national programmes.
- **Artists**: artists of every discipline, and cultural professionals, need opportunities to build pedagogical capability and the skills to work with educators in schools and learning centres and to communicate directly with learners; joint projects pairing artists-in-training with teachers-in-training can secure future cooperation.

Progress is hampered by scarce funding and, particularly outside urban areas, by the absence of cultural resources — libraries, theatres, museums.

#### 2. Partnerships (p. 11–12)

Although creativity ranks high in most policy documents, its dependence on quality education as the principal means of facilitating creativity is rarely acknowledged at a fundamental level. Implementation becomes affordable and feasible when built on partnership. Joint responsibility between the Ministries handling Culture and/or Education — and among the mechanisms that implement and evaluate programmes — is required, with each entity clear about its role. Partnership types:

- **Ministerial/municipality level**: joint policies and budgets across Ministries of Culture, Education, and higher education and research for curricular and extracurricular class projects; policy-level union of arts and education among Ministries and municipalities (which often run both educational and cultural institutions) to connect schools with the cultural world through cooperation projects — the aim being art and culture at the heart of education, not at the curriculum's margins.
- **School level**: most cities, towns and villages worldwide have some cultural facility, and learning is no longer confined to schools; partnerships between schools and cultural institutions have opened new pedagogical possibilities. Where collaboration is long-standing, its extent and effectiveness still vary widely, and genuine commitment from both sides is vital. Close partnerships — chiefly visits to cultural institutions — give students information, artistic encounters and immersion in artistic processes, with great potential for integrated teaching, especially in primary education, where visual learning resonates strongly with young children.
- **Teacher level**: teachers gain by inviting artists experienced in movement, words, sound and rhythm, and images to develop in-school and extra-curricular projects matched to participants' age, teaching methods and duration; some cultural institutions also offer extensive online teaching resources for teachers, arts educators, families and students.

Obstacles include: Arts Education budgets, where they exist, may sit in one ministry or department with little willingness to share; government bureaucracies can be narrow in outlook and disinclined to cooperate; and the educational and cultural fields differ in both individual and structural "cultures".

### Research on Arts Education, and Knowledge Sharing (p. 12–13)

Evidence-based decision-making is a precondition for building creative capacities: policy makers need proof of effectiveness before endorsing Arts Education and Arts-in-Education. Creativity expressed through culture may be humanity's most evenly spread resource, and research shows some education systems suppress it while others foster it; yet the mechanisms are poorly documented, so the claim that Arts Education is among the best media for nurturing creativity (when teaching methods support it) finds little traction with policy makers, and more research in this area is needed. Existing evidence is thin, anecdotal and hard to obtain in many countries; successful programmes seldom articulate their theoretical assumptions or document outcomes, leaving few accessible best-practice case studies for advocacy — a major setback for practice, policy and integration.

Because Arts Education involves creating art as well as appreciating, observing, interpreting, critiquing and philosophising about it, researchers must look, think and observe through both artistic and pedagogical lenses. Research may be global, national, institutional or discipline-based, and should address:

- describing current Arts Education programmes — their nature and extent;
- links between Arts Education and creativity;
- links between Arts Education and social abilities, active citizenship and empowerment;
- evaluations of programmes and methods, especially the social and individual value they add;
- the diversity of delivery methods;
- how effective Arts Education policies are;
- the nature and impact of education–culture partnerships in implementation;
- developing and using standards for teacher education;
- assessment of students' learning in Arts Education (identifying best practice in assessment techniques);
- the influence of cultural industries (television, film) on children's and other learners' arts education, and ways to ensure these industries deliver responsible Arts Education to citizens.

Implementation involves: setting a research agenda and securing funding; running research seminars; surveying arts educators' research interests; and promoting interdisciplinary collaboration on methodologies. Universities and other institutions can work with a clearinghouse (or "Observatory") that gathers, analyses, reworks and distributes Arts Education knowledge — a dependable data source for advocacy and lobbying, focused either thematically (e.g., performing arts education) or geographically (e.g., arts education in India).

### Concluding Vision (p. 14)

Building creative capacity and cultural awareness is hard but indispensable work: every force in society must help new generations acquire not only knowledge and skills but, above all, the values, attitudes, ethical principles and moral direction needed to act as responsible world citizens and guarantors of a sustainable future. Universal education must be of good quality — and it reaches that quality only if, through Arts Education, it develops the insights and perspectives, creativity and initiative, and critical reflection and occupational capacities the new century demands. The Road Map is offered as a template — a set of overall guidelines to be adapted, changed and expanded to fit national and societal contexts.

### Recommendations (p. 15–21)

The recommendations draw on declarations from preparatory conferences held during 2005 — in Australia (September), Lithuania (September), Colombia and the Republic of Korea (November), and Trinidad and Tobago (June) — and on the African and Arab States regional discussion groups at the Lisbon conference (6–9 March 2006). Considerations framing them include: developing aesthetic sensibility, creativity and the capacities for critical thought and reflection inherent to being human is a right belonging to every child and young person; children and young people need greater awareness both of themselves and of the natural and cultural environment around them, and educational and cultural systems should aim at universal access to cultural goods, services and practices; Arts Education readies audiences and the wider public to appreciate artistic manifestations; globalization challenges cultural diversity while knowledge-based societies increasingly demand imagination, creativity and collaboration; in many societies art traditionally was, and often remains, woven into everyday life, transmitting culture and transforming communities and individuals; young people need dedicated spaces for artistic activity — community-cultural centres and art museums; multicultural societies face a growing need for creativity and imagination that Arts Education can efficiently meet; contemporary societies need strategies and policies — educational and cultural alike — that sustain and transmit cultural and aesthetic values and identity, thereby enhancing diversity and building peaceful, prosperous, sustainable societies; most nations are multicultural — a confluence of cultures, communities, nationalities and languages — whose cultural complexity has generated creative energy, indigenous educational perspectives and practices, and a rich tangible and intangible heritage now threatened by multiple socio-cultural, economic and environmental changes; the arts are valuable and applicable in learning, building cognitive and social skills, innovative thinking and creativity, and encouraging the behaviours and values of social tolerance and celebration of diversity; Arts Education improves learning and skills through flexible structures (related time, discipline and roles), relevance to learners' lives and social and cultural environment, and cooperation between formal and informal learning systems; this aligns with how societies have traditionally conceived the arts and with the newer understanding that learning through the arts improves learning and skills; by generating cross-cutting skills and abilities and raising motivation and active class participation, Arts Education can raise educational quality, helping achieve one of the six EFA goals set by the Dakar World Conference on Education for All (2000); it can serve therapy with disabled children and support post-disaster and post-conflict contexts; like all education it must be of high quality to work; and, as ethical and civic construction, it is a basic instrument of social integration that can help tackle many societies' critical issues — crime and violence, illiteracy that persists, gender inequalities (including male underachievement), child abuse and neglect, unemployment and political corruption. The development of information and communication technologies (ICT) across society and the economy also holds potential for Arts Education.

Challenges identified: many countries' education policies undervalue Arts Education, leaving it isolated and devalued as an area of knowledge; cultural and educational systems, and their concerns, are frequently dissociated, with two separate agendas running in parallel or even in opposite directions; specialized teacher training programmes are too few, and general teacher education does too little to promote the arts' role in teaching and learning; artists and their participation in Arts Education processes are under-recognized; a vast body of Arts Education experience remains unresearched and unsystematized; and budgets are absent or inadequate for routine and development needs.

#### 1. Recommendations for Educators, Parents, Artists, and Directors of Schools and Educational Institutions (p. 17)

*Advocacy, Support and Education*: build public awareness of Arts Education's value and social impact, generating demand for it and for skilled arts educators; lead, support and assist teaching in and through the arts; make active participation in, and access to, the arts a core educational component for every child; encourage local, contextualized human and material resources to serve as both providers and content of quality education; supply resources and learning materials that help educators develop, use and share new arts-rich pedagogy; help practitioners exploit technology so Arts Education can reach marginalized groups and support innovative knowledge products and knowledge-sharing; back continuous professional development for teachers, artists and community workers so professionals value cultural diversity and can grow students' abilities to create, critique and innovate; promote artistic practice through digital media; where absent, establish youth cultural centres and other Arts Education spaces and facilities.

*Partnerships and Cooperation*: cultivate active, durable partnerships linking educational contexts (formal and non-formal) with the wider community; bring local arts practitioners into learning contexts and include local art forms and techniques, reinforcing local cultures and identity; foster cooperation among schools, parents, community organizations and institutions, and mobilize community resources for Arts Education programmes so that communities share in transmitting cultural values and local art forms.

*Implementation, Evaluation and Knowledge-sharing*: carry out and evaluate collaborative school-community projects grounded in inclusive cooperation, integration and relevance; promote effective documentation and knowledge-sharing among teachers; share information and evidence with stakeholders — governments, communities, media, NGOs and the private sector.

#### 2. Recommendations for Government Ministries and Policy Makers (p. 17–19)

*Recognition*: acknowledge Arts Education's role in readying audiences and the wider public to appreciate artistic manifestations; recognize the need for an Arts Education policy linking communities, social and educational institutions, and the world of work; value successful locally-developed, culturally-relevant practices and projects and replicate them in future work; prioritize generating better public understanding and deeper recognition of Arts Education's essential contributions to individuals and society.

*Policy Development*: convert growing recognition of Arts Education's importance into resources sufficient to turn principles into action, raising awareness of what arts and creativity offer everyone and backing a new vision of arts and learning; design national and regional research policies attentive to ancestral cultures and vulnerable population groups; develop implementation and monitoring strategies to safeguard quality; secure Arts Education a permanent, central, adequately funded place in the curriculum, staffed by teachers of suitable quality and skill; ground funding and programme decisions in research and establish new norms for assessing Arts Education's impact, since it can be demonstrated that Arts Education can contribute significantly to improving student performance in areas such as literacy and numeracy, as well as providing human and social benefits; guarantee continuity of public Arts Education policies beyond individual government programmes; adopt regional arts education policies covering all countries of a region (e.g., African Union); include Arts Education in the Cultural Charters that all Member States adopt.

*Education, Implementation and Support*: provide professional education for artists and teachers to raise delivery quality, and found arts-education departments in universities where none exist; make the education of arts teachers a new system priority so they contribute more effectively to learning and cultural development, and embed arts sensitization in the training of all teachers and education actors; deploy trained teachers and artists in educational institutions and non-formal settings so that Arts Education can grow and be promoted; carry the arts through the whole school curriculum and into non-formal education; open Arts Education, inside and outside schools, to every individual regardless of abilities, needs or social, physical, mental or geographical situation; produce the material resources needed for effective delivery of the arts — space, media, books, tools and art materials — and distribute them to all schools and libraries; deliver Arts Education to indigenous peoples in forms suited to their own cultural ways of teaching and learning, accessible in their own languages, recalling the principles of the UNESCO Declaration on Cultural Diversity; examine how to build localized Arts Education programmes on local values and traditions.

*Partnerships and Cooperation*: foster partnerships across all concerned ministries and governmental organizations to build coherent, sustainable policies and strategies; urge officials at every level to unite with educators, artists, NGOs, lobby groups, business, the labour movement and civil society in advocacy action plans and messages; draw arts and cultural institutions, together with foundations, media, industry and the private sector, actively into education; weave partnerships linking schools, artists and cultural institutions into the core educational process; advance sub-regional and regional cooperation in arts education to strengthen regional integration.

*Research and Knowledge-sharing*: compile a comprehensive databank of Arts Education resources, human and material, open to all educational institutions including via the Internet; ensure Ministries of Education and Culture disseminate information on Arts Education, implementation and follow-up; stimulate collections and inventories of artworks that enrich Arts Education; document the oral culture of societies-in-crisis.

#### 3. Recommendations for UNESCO and Other Intergovernmental and Non-governmental Organizations (p. 19–21)

*Advocacy and Support*: recognize Arts Education's contributions across all areas of society and treat it as a major cross-sectoral strategy; connect it with appropriate resources and related areas such as Education for All and Education for Sustainable Development; stress bottom-up strategies empowering and validating practical, grassroots initiatives; use Arts Education programmes to teach socio-cultural and environmental problems, cultivating pupils' environmental values, sense of belonging and commitment to sustainable development; persuade communication media to back Arts Education objectives and cultivate aesthetic sensitivity and artistic values in the public; keep Arts Education within international programmes; earmark budget provisions to foster it and promote its curricular inclusion; advance its development and implementation at different levels and modalities of education programmes from interdisciplinary and trans-disciplinary perspectives, opening new aesthetic channels; promote investments furnishing the cultural goods, material resources and funding to: create specialized areas within schools and cultural spaces that offer varied forms of Arts Education; supply specialized didactic materials, including mother-tongue publications; and ensure Arts Education's development together with fair pay and working conditions for the teaching professionals building this field; press governments and other agencies to facilitate collaboration among ministries, departments, NGOs, cultural institutions and arts professionals; convene further Arts Education conferences to enable regular reflection and continuous improvement — the Ministers and participants endorsed the Republic of Korea's proposal to host the second World Conference in Seoul.

*Partnerships and Cooperation*: help each country's cultural and educational institutions coordinate and agree on policies and activities for developing Arts Education; encourage definition of the abilities and mechanisms for linking formal and non-formal Arts Education across educational and cultural institutions; build cooperative networks among Member States and within their education and cultural systems, founding successful development on cooperative activities and alliances. Referring to the African Union–UNESCO partnership agreement concluded after the Summit of African Heads of State and Government (Khartoum, January 2006):

1. support UN Member States in adopting and proclaiming a "Decade for Arts Education for All" (2006–2016);
2. revisit the objectives of the Education-for-all strategy so that arts education is included;
3. with the African Union, strengthen support to national institutions promoting culture and the arts in Africa (e.g., CRAC in Togo, CELTHO in Niger), to Arts Education institutions (public or private) and to civil-society initiatives reinforcing endogenous artistic abilities;
4. jointly with the African Union and the intergovernmental sub-regional bodies (CEDEAO, SADDEC, CEMAC, etc.), support an African Regional Conference on Arts Education.

*Research, Evaluation and Knowledge-sharing*: press for continuous evaluation of Arts Education's emotional, cultural, social, cognitive and creative impacts; institute a regional system for gathering and spreading Arts Education information; advance knowledge-sharing and networking via Arts in Education Observatories (clearinghouses), together with UNESCO Chairs and the UNITWIN Network; promote arts research to guide future initiatives in this expanding field; create an international research database offering scientifically sound evidence of what Arts Education and creative involvement mean for individuals and society — including (among other areas) the integrated human being's development, public health, social cohesion, conflict resolution, and new technologies' use in creative expression in schools; commission case studies and research as guides to more participatory, practice-led research, potentially yielding an international network of researchers sharing methodologies and building better assessment models with students, artists, parents and teachers as active participants — building future capacity and informing lifelong learning and assessment; encourage research into, and rediscovery of, the traditional uses of arts in learning and everyday life; record and evaluate bibliographical and other information sources on Arts Education for analysis, re-packaging and dissemination; systematize significant experiences as a basis for quality indicators and exchange; facilitate regional and international education and research projects; establish international networks enabling regional cooperation and exchange of best practice in implementing Arts Education policies.

*Training and Support for Teachers, Schools and Artists*: support teacher training in Arts Education theory and practice; mobilize international support for teacher training and curriculum development to widen coverage and quality, especially in resource-challenged countries; bring artists, tradition-bearers and cultural promoters into primary and secondary education to enrich pupils' creative use of diverse forms of artistic expression; create programmes for research and lifelong training for professionals connected with Arts Education (artists, teachers, managers, planners, etc.); foster the national and international organization of arts teachers for stronger social representation and professional capacity; stimulate the creation of texts, materials, methodologies and teaching-learning guides for Arts Education; embed new ICT in teacher training and in formal and non-formal education alike, as instruments of creation, artistic expression, reflection and critical thinking.

### Annex: Case Studies (p. 22–26)

**Education for arts teachers**:
- *Teacher Education Partnerships for Secondary Education in Papua New Guinea*: singing, dancing, mime, sculpture, painting and storytelling are integral to how indigenous communities in Papua New Guinea (PNG) live; the arts mark every stage — birth, adulthood, old age, death and what follows — helping people make sense of the world, so arts teaching and learning, and arts knowledge and skills, matter greatly there. The project pairs teacher educators with community artists to prepare future art teachers: trainees from the University of Goroka's Expressive Arts Department work with the principal artist, George Sari of Okiufa village on the campus fringe, who learned his clan's history and stories from his grandfather and father and absorbed its land, flora and fauna. Working alongside him, students reconnect with their past and build skills and knowledge in ways described as magical and "mesmerizing". The collaboration among the students, George and the department is cited as good practice in arts teacher education.
- *The Artist in Community Education Programme, Canada*: a specialized stream of Queen's University's Bachelor of Education brings together artists from dance, music, theatre, the visual arts and creative writing for a nine-month course that satisfies teacher certification while keeping the arts and creativity firmly at its centre; applicants need strength in an artistic discipline plus an undergraduate degree. Practitioners with extensive experience as both artists and educators teach the curriculum; candidates acquire pedagogical practices suited to teaching the arts, learn to build and sustain partnerships with colleagues and with arts and education organizations, undertake collaborative, interdisciplinary projects with practitioners of other art forms, and learn to apply their artistry in educational settings — schools, community arts centres and the outreach programmes of professional arts organizations.

**Education for artists**:
- *The Artist Teacher Scheme in the United Kingdom*: part of an expanding national effort to develop art and design teachers professionally, operating through fifteen centres — twelve of them in England, with a single centre in Scotland and two in Wales — each pairing a leading gallery or museum of contemporary art with a university art school or college of art; the National Society for Education in Art Design manages the scheme, with core funding from the Arts Councils of England, Scotland and Wales. The centres' varied offerings let artist teachers deepen their awareness of how rich and complex contemporary fine art practice is, and of the range of thinking and influences informing it; artist teachers can reappraise or re-engage with their own development as artists and join a strong professional community, while the schemes also aim to lift standards of art and design teaching and learning in schools and colleges through artist teachers' individual practice. Provision ranges from Introductory Courses of up to five days combining intensive practical and theoretical workshops with seminars, through an Intermediate Programme co-ordinating seminars, studio or gallery visits and workshops, to courses leading to a Masters degree (http://www.nsead.org/cpd/ats.aspx).

**Partnerships — ministerial/municipality level**:
- *Methods for Partnership, Lithuania*: to bind the culture and education sectors more closely, the Ministry of Education and Science has launched national initiatives providing extra-curricular arts activities for children, most projects formulated at government level and backed organizationally by municipalities, NGOs and national centres for arts, youth and tourism. Goals include keeping children productively occupied after school, nurturing creativity and self-expression, supporting artistically gifted children, and building cultural awareness and local environmental and community knowledge.
- *Laboratories of Investigation-Creation, Colombia*: established under the Ministry of Culture of Colombia's 'National Plan for the Arts' to advance the visual arts and knit together cultural, academic and artistic institutions. Working regionally, they create meeting spaces where artists and teachers exchange practices — artistic and pedagogical — with an eye to future configurations of arts training and Arts Education; they also build an inter-regional perspective on artistic and pedagogic practice and circulate pedagogic models to regions less advanced in this area.
- *Norwegian Cultural Rucksack*: launched roughly five years earlier by the Norwegian government so that every student in grades one to ten would regularly meet high-quality artists and artistic expressions within the regular school curriculum. Built on cooperation between school and cultural authorities nationally, regionally and locally, the scheme has linked arts organizations and institutions with the school system in partnership: each school's annual programme now includes visits from performing artists and trips to museums and other cultural venues, plus art workshops and performances in which students — and sometimes staff — work with professional artists. It is generally well received locally, though challenges remain in building artists' and teachers' competencies to deepen its educational effects and in forging mutual understanding among all actors about its potential.

**Partnerships — school level**:
- *Pilot Project for School Level Partnership in the Republic of Korea (2004–2006)*: the initiative set out to model cooperation and institutionalize a community network to underpin long-term Arts Education planning in schools. In 2005 the Korea Culture and Arts Education Service (KACES) funded 64 projects across the country, varying in how they partnered with practitioners, local artist groups and arts organizations, and using arts centres, museums, galleries and similar venues as classrooms. Combined with the 'Artist-in-School' initiative, which gives artists pre-service education to engage them in education, it met schools' demand for Arts Education by supplying professional instructors in newly popular fields — drama, dance, film and media — dispatching about 1500 artist-turned-instructors to 3000 schools.
- *Learning through Art (LTA), Guggenheim Museum (USA, Spain 2006)*: an educational programme reinforcing curriculum subjects by placing artists in state schools for close work with teachers and their pupils. Children are encouraged to learn in many ways — talking, exploring, acting and creating; because they take readily to learning through artistic activity, the workshops effectively reinforce curriculum areas and develop reading, writing and language skills, while taking part in the creative process builds skills in planning and conducting projects, teamwork and critical thinking. LTA reaches children who might otherwise struggle with traditional teaching and, above all, generally raises children's self-esteem and personal growth. Each programme is unique and individualized, tailored to each class's interests, shortcomings and abilities, and can cover any curriculum area or theme, from natural sciences to mathematics: once teachers identify a curriculum subject needing extra support and define their targets and the skills and attitudes to encourage, the artist and a Museum educator design a sequence of workshops organized into teaching units — hour-and-a-half workshops spanning painting, photography, sculpture, video, music and digital art, taking place at the school weekly over twenty weeks. With the teachers, the programme's artists play a critical role in stimulating the children's work and encouraging them to apply to curriculum subjects the conceptual thinking proper to artistic creativity.

**Partnerships — teacher level**:
- *Windmill Performing Arts, Australia*: develops collaboration among artists, teachers, companies and institutions to commission new work and to build partnerships, co-presentations, touring and research. Since 2002 the company has created children's performances spanning opera, theatre, dance, music, ballet and puppetry, nationally and internationally. Children's cognitive and holistic development underpins its activities: strategic programmes run with universities and the education sector include professional training for educators and artists, arts-based workshops for families, and arts education research. One university partnership produced 'Children's Voices', a longitudinal research project exploring and recording how performance affects children's learning; the findings inform the creation of future Windmill performances and provide formal documentation and assessment of arts education in an Australian context.
- *The Oak of Finland Cultural Heritage Project*: in Finland, teachers commonly bring artists into their learning environments or arrange visits to cultural institutions and events; rare, however, is teacher collaboration with on-line programmes. A successful example here is 'Oak of Finland Plus', a joint undertaking of the National Board of Antiquities, the National Board of Education and the Ministry of the Environment to develop heritage education through partnerships; Cultural Heritage education is regarded in Finland as the new core curriculum. The project teaches cultural literacy, builds understanding of global cultures and develops methods of cultural heritage education that draw on schoolteachers, museums, regional environmental centres and the two national boards; schools and museums first joined via the internet, then ran the project with the aid of its homepage, journals and CD-ROMs. In all, 400 schools and 500 teachers took part, along with 65 museums and 15 organizations spread over 70 municipalities.
- *Young Digital Creators (YDC)*: a further on-line partnership is UNESCO's DigiArts 'Young Digital Creators' (YDC) project, created in 2004 — a web-based international programme through which young people gradually build, working collaboratively with digital creative tools, a deeper understanding of one another's cultural values and shared perspectives on today's global issues. It aims to promote innovative uses of arts and creativity for expression and communication, foster international cultural communication, familiarize young people with visual literacy and visual communication, and mobilize youth communities through creative online learning. Each session brings together, on average, 15 schools or youth centres via the internet; a teacher's kit outlining the programme's phases guides implementation, and an on-line moderator appointed by UNESCO provides the pedagogical assistance students need. Four YDC programmes exist, addressing water, peace, HIV/AIDS, and life in the city; across the 2005–6 training sessions, participation exceeded 120 schools and youth centres from a range of geo-cultural backgrounds.

## GLOSSAR

- **Arts Education** — learning in and through the arts; framed as a universal human right and a compulsory element of educational programmes for every learner, including those commonly excluded.
- **Arts in Education (AiE)** — an approach that uses the arts and their associated practices and cultural traditions as a vehicle for teaching and deepening general curriculum subjects, grounded in the theory of "multiple intelligences".
- **Arts fields** — a pragmatic, evolving, never-exclusive categorization of art forms (performing arts; literature and poetry; craft; design; digital arts; storytelling; heritage; visual arts and film; media; photography).
- **Dimensions of Arts Education** — the three complementary pedagogical streams — studying artistic works, practising the arts, and direct encounters with artistic works.
- **Quality education** — learner-centred education defined by three principles — relevance to the learner together with universal values, equity of access and outcomes guaranteeing inclusion, and reflection and fulfilment of individual rights.
- **Artists in School (AIS)** — programmes that bring artists into educational settings, cited as valuable learning opportunities for teachers and students.
- **Clearinghouse / Observatory** — a body that gathers, analyses, reworks and distributes information and knowledge about Arts Education, serving as a dependable data source for advocacy and lobbying.
- **Cultural (creative) industries** — industries such as publishing, music, film, television and other media that serve as most people's chief gateways to culture and the arts and as resources for educators.
- **Decade for Arts Education for All (2006–2016)** — a decade proposed for adoption and proclamation by UN Member States, linked to the African Union–UNESCO partnership agreement.
- **Cultural capital** — the valuable cultural resources of a nation, whose exploitation requires human resources that Arts Education helps develop.
- **Environmental Education through Arts Education** — arts-based environmental learning, cited among valuable educational opportunities in all learning contexts.
