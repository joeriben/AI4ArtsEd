# Stable Bias: Evaluating Societal Representations in Diffusion Models

## PROFIL

The article "Stable Bias: Evaluating Societal Representations in Diffusion Models" was written by Alexandra Sasha Luccioni, Margaret Mitchell, Christopher Akiki and Yacine Jernite, affiliated with Hugging Face and Leipzig University/ScaDS.AI, and addresses the social biases of machine learning-enabled Text-to-Image (TTI) systems, which the authors treat as assemblages of components rather than single models. Their position is that, as TTI systems become widely deployed commercial services, characterizing their biases is a necessary first step toward reducing discriminatory outcomes; since generated images depict fictive humans with no inherent gender or ethnicity, they propose comparing the variation triggered by explicitly enumerating gender and ethnicity markers in prompts with the variation produced by spanning different professions. The paper first reviews bias evaluation in multimodal machine learning and prior work on TTI safety filters, stereotypical representations and debiasing approaches, noting that biases propagate to downstream tasks such as image captioning and image search, and that training-data filtering and CLIP guidance can amplify bias. Its methodology has four steps — defining identity characteristics, generating images by varying markers, generating images for 146 occupations drawn from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, and analyzing them through text-based (ViT GPT-2 captioning and BLIP VQA) and clustering-based visual methods — applied to Dall·E 2 and Stable Diffusion v.1.4 and v.2, with an additional benchmark of 11 systems from the Hugging Face Hub. The results show that all outputs correlate with US labor demographics while consistently under-representing marginalized identities, most pronounced for Dall·E 2 and least for Stable Diffusion v.1.4; the paper also presents three interactive bias-exploration tools (Diffusion Bias Explorer, Average Face Comparison Tool, Nearest Neighbors Explorers) and releases datasets and code. It builds on prior research into multimodal bias, TTI safety and debiasing of Stable Diffusion's latent space, and sets itself against binary gender and fixed ethnicity categorizations, using flexible proxy representations instead. The authors state limitations including the biases of the captioning and VQA models, the fixed set of attributes analyzed, and their own lack of social-science backgrounds, and point to future work on further dimensions such as age and visual markers of religion. The article is usable in arts education wherever the societal representations of AI image-generation systems, their auditing, and questions of diversity and stereotyping in generated imagery are taught or researched.

## WISSEN

### Abstract and Purpose (p. 1)

The paper, authored by Alexandra Sasha Luccioni, Margaret Mitchell, Christopher Akiki and Yacine Jernite (Hugging Face and Leipzig University/ScaDS.AI), addresses the social biases of machine learning-enabled Text-to-Image (TTI) systems. As TTI systems become widely deployed commercial services, characterizing their biases is a necessary first step toward reducing discriminatory outcomes. The difficulty is that generated images depict fictive humans who have no inherent gender or ethnicity. The authors therefore propose a new method: comparing the variation in generated images triggered by explicitly enumerating gender and ethnicity markers in prompts with the variation produced by spanning different professions. This allows them to (1) identify specific bias trends, (2) provide targeted scores to compare models on diversity and representation, and (3) jointly model interdependent social variables for multidimensional analysis. They apply the method to three popular TTI systems — Dall·E 2, Stable Diffusion v.1.4 and v.2 — and find that while all outputs correlate with US labor demographics, all consistently under-represent marginalized identities to different extents. They also release their datasets and interactive bias-exploration tools.

### Introduction (p. 1)

Diffusion-based models (Stable Diffusion, Make-a-Scene, Imagen, Dall·E 2) generate images by reversing the gradual addition of noise, inspired by non-equilibrium thermodynamics. Their likelihood-based loss makes them simpler to train and their outputs more diverse than earlier generative models, but their iterative nature makes the latent space hard to access directly. The authors call them "Text-to-Image (TTI) Systems" rather than models because they are assemblages of components (safety filters, embeddings, CLIP guidance) whose individual contributions to bias are hard to disentangle. Deployed in stock imagery, graphic design and other applications, these systems risk amplifying societal inequities, yet their biases remain sparsely documented — often only in broad terms in model cards and model papers. The paper introduces tools and approaches for auditing these biases by comparing model outputs for targeted prompts, and shares datasets and low-barrier exploration tools.

### Background (p. 2–3)

#### Bias Evaluation in Multimodal ML (p. 2)

Text and image models are known to encode problematic biases independently, but the rise of multimodal models has outpaced methods for understanding their specific biases; it is unknown whether biases in each modality amplify or compound one another. Past work shows that biases around race, gender and appearance propagate to downstream tasks such as image captioning (e.g., associating women with shopping and men with snowboarding; lower-quality captions for people with darker skin) and image search (algorithmic curation reinforcing biases and harming minority groups' sense of belonging). State-of-the-art multimodal models learn these biases, and under- or mis-representation by widely deployed image-generation systems can negatively impact minority groups.

#### Text-to-Image Systems and their Biases (p. 3)

Recent research examines TTI safety filters, semantic space structure, stereotypical representations of demographics, and memorization/regeneration of training examples. Some work proposes debiasing approaches, including guiding Stable Diffusion's latent space toward more representative generations, though generalizability is unclear. Bias sources are multiple: training data scraped from the web contains harmful, pornographic, mislabeled and corrupted content; subsequent "aesthetic" or "safe for work" filtering (criteria the authors note are themselves unclear) can amplify bias — Dall·E 2's creators found that filtering "explicit" content contributed to bias amplification, requiring post-hoc mitigation. CLIP, used to guide training/generation, encodes societal biases whose impact on image generation is unclear. Finally, safety filters and prompt enhancement techniques are poorly documented, and keyword-based filtering has been shown to have disparate impact on marginalized groups.

### Methodology: Auditing Social Biases in TTI Systems (p. 3–5)

The approach rests on the intuition that generated images lack inherent social attributes but display features viewers interpret as social markers. Since gender and ethnicity are multidimensional, fluid, and cannot be determined from appearance, the authors avoid binary gender and fixed ethnicity categorizations (which can perpetuate algorithmic harms) and instead use flexible proxy representations of visual features. The method has four steps:

1. Define identity characteristics to evaluate — here, gender and ethnicity.
2. Generate images by varying markers of these attributes in prompts.
3. Generate images for the social attribute to audit — the profession, using 146 occupations.
4. Carry out two analyses: a text-based analysis using Visual Question Answering (VQA) models, and a clustering-based evaluation in the image modality.

#### Generating a Dataset of Identity Characteristics and Social Attributes (p. 4)

Prompts follow the pattern "Photo portrait of a [X] [Y]" (chosen after experimenting with alternatives like "Photo of", "Portrait of", "Close up of"). Professions come from a list of 146 occupations from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), which also supplies demographic and salary data. For identity prompts, the pattern becomes "Photo portrait of a [ethnicity] [gender] at work". Gender values are 'man', 'woman', 'non-binary person', plus an unspecified 'person' option — acknowledged as incomplete, missing trans* experiences and non-US gender terms. Ethnicity terms start from the US census list, expanded with synonyms (full list in Appendix A). Enumerating all gender and ethnicity markers yields 68 prompts. Two datasets are generated for three systems (Stable Diffusion v.1.4, v.2, and Dall·E 2 — chosen as state-of-the-art in early 2023, enabling comparison of two versions of one system and open vs. closed-source systems): an 'Identities' dataset (68 prompts, 2040 images) and a 'Professions' dataset (146 professions per system).

#### Different Approaches for Analyzing Generated Images (p. 4–5)

- **Text Features Analysis**: two ML systems generate textual descriptions — the ViT GPT-2 captioning model trained on MS-COCO, and the BLIP VQA base model (pre-trained on 124M image-text pairs including MS-COCO and a subset of LAION-400M, fine-tuned on VQA 2.0), which answers "What word best describes this person's appearance?". Both are open-ended; outputs are treated as features, not ground truth, since the models carry their own biases.
- **Visual Features Analysis**: dense image embeddings obtained from the BLIP VQA encoder (normalized average of question token embeddings conditioned on the image), chosen because it focuses on the depicted person and outperformed alternatives including the CLIP image encoder. The 2040 'Identities' embeddings are clustered into 24 regions using Ward linkage on the dot product. Regions do not correspond to predefined identity labels but encode visual features associated with the gender/ethnicity phrases, including intersections.
- **Interactive Exploration**: tools for ad-hoc, story-based qualitative examination (detailed in Section 4.3).

### Results (p. 5–9)

#### Gender Bias Analysis through Text Markers (p. 5–6)

97.66% of captions contained gender-marked terms versus 45.56% of VQA predictions (VQA outputs are single words). Compared to BLS statistics, Dall·E 2 shows the largest discrepancy — mentioning women on average 27% less in captions and 25% less in VQA — while Stable Diffusion v.1.4 has the least (approximately 9% for both). Table 1 figures: SD v.1.4 captions 38.04% woman / 61.96% man; SD v.2 33.45% / 66.55%; Dall·E 2 19.96% / 80.04%; BLS reference 47.03% / 52.97%. Professions with the biggest under-representation of women across systems: clerk (57/55% less), data entry keyer (55/53% less), real estate broker (52/54% less); professions with more mentions of women: singer (29/36% more), cleaner (20/16% more), dispatcher (19/16% more). Very few captions mention gender-neutral terms like 'person' (under 1%), and none use 'non-binary'. Under 0.5% of captions and 2% of VQA outputs explicitly mention the profession — except highly "marked" professions with distinctive uniforms: police officer (80.95% of captions), doctor (70.48% of VQA), firefighter (45.71%), pilot (29.84%).

#### Gender and Ethnicity Distribution in the Image Space (p. 6–8)

**Characterizing Identity Regions** (p. 6–7): Images from 68 combinations (4 gender phrases, 17 ethnicity phrases) are clustered into 24 regions. Table 2 summarizes the 10 most represented: region 4 accounts for 40.1% of 'Professions' images and corresponds to prompts describing White men; regions featuring 'woman' prompts (15, 13, 1, 10) together total 25.5%. A case study of 630 images for "Photo portrait of a laboratory technician" (210 per system) shows region 4 twice as prominent for Dall·E 2 as for Stable Diffusion; Dall·E 2 has no generations assigned to cluster 1.

**Gender and Ethnicity Representation across Systems** (p. 7–8): Professions are ranked by BLS-reported proportions of women (and separately of Black workers) and grouped into 5 quintiles of 29–30 professions; regions are grouped by whether 'woman' (top-2) or 'Black' (top-4) phrases feature prominently. Table 3 shows, for example, that for the top quintile of professions by women workers (BLS 86.8%), clusters featuring 'woman' account for 83.0% for SD 1.4, 78.3% for SD 2, and 56.3% for Dall·E 2; for the top quintile by Black workers (BLS 22.1%), clusters featuring 'Black' account for 16.8%, 16.1% and 4.5% respectively. All systems under-represent regions associated with 'woman' and 'Black', least pronounced for SD v1.4 and most pronounced for Dall·E 2. The method is applicable to any TTI system; the authors additionally benchmarked 11 systems from the most downloaded text-to-image models on the Hugging Face Hub, finding that output diversity across both dimensions depends on the specific fine-tuning and adaptation process even when systems share Stable Diffusion initialization.

#### Interactive Tools for Interactive Exploration (p. 8–9)

Three tools are presented:

1. **Diffusion Bias Explorer** — compares the same profession prompts across the 3 systems; used throughout the project to visually verify tendencies (e.g., homogeneity of 'CEO' images).
2. **Average Face Comparison Tool** — uses the Facer Python package for face detection, alignment and averaging across professions, avoiding facial recognition/classification; blurriness of averages signals homogeneity.
3. **Nearest Neighbors Explorers (BoVW and Colorfulness)** — users pick an image (e.g., a Black woman generated by a specific system) and explore its neighborhood by colorfulness or by a bag-of-visual-words TF-IDF index (SIFT features quantized via k-means into a codebook, indexed with a graph-based approach). Useful for detecting stereotypical content such as Native American headdresses or color-coded professions (firefighters in red, nurses in blue scrubs).

Code and instructions for duplicating and adapting the tools are released.

### Limitations and Future Work (p. 9–10)

Limitations: (1) the captioning and VQA models have their own uncontrollable biases, mitigated by using multiple models and less symbolic methods like BoVW; (2) Dall·E 2 is only available via API, so prompt injection or filtering could not be controlled for (a July 2022 blog post announced a diversity-improving technique, speculated to be prompt injection), nor whether the same model was being prompted on different days; (3) the analysis covers only a fixed set of gender and ethnicity attributes, which are inherently fluid, multidimensional and non-discretizable; (4) the authors lack primary academic backgrounds in relevant social sciences and first-hand experience of many identity characteristics, and acknowledge a bias toward Western discrimination axes. Future work should explore further dimensions (age, visual markers of religion), other stereotype-tied attributes, and further development of interactive tools to empower stakeholders with less technical expertise.

#### B: Clustering Visualization (p. 15–17)

**B.0.1 Characterizing the 'Identities' clusters**: BLIP VQA question embeddings show the lowest average entropy across clusters, making them the most useful embedding method. Two clusters prominently featuring 'Latinx' and 'woman' prompts depict people with different skin tones and hair types, reinforcing the difficulty of identifying ethnicity from visual characteristics alone.

**B.0.2 Measuring aggregated social diversity**: Diversity is measured as the entropy of the distribution of examples across regions. Entropy is very similar across systems for the 'identities' datasets — all systems can depict varied combinations when explicitly prompted — but for the 'professions' datasets, SD v.2 and Dall·E 2 are much less visually diverse, with Dall·E 2 ranking last in all settings. Table 7 (12-cluster setting) shows cluster 4 (White/Caucasian/Man) is most represented across systems (SD 1.4: 47.1%, SD 2: 58.2%, Dall·E 2: 74.0%), while cluster 6 (Black/African American/Woman) represents only 3.9% and 4.7% of Stable Diffusion professions generations and 0.1% for Dall·E 2.

**B.0.3 Disaggregated analysis by profession**: Greater observed gender balance in the US workforce corresponds to more diverse generations — primarily along gender for 'singer', ethnicity for 'taxi driver' and 'maid', and the gender-ethnicity intersection for 'social worker' (over-representing the cluster associated with 'Black' and 'woman'). SD v.1.4 exacerbates gender stereotypes for professions with over 80% women per BLS ('dental assistant', 'event planner', 'nutritionist', 'receptionist'), whereas Dall·E 2's lowest-diversity professions include positions of authority: 'CEO' and 'director', with over 97% of generations assigned to cluster 4 (mostly 'white' and 'man') despite BLS reporting 29.1% and 39.6% women respectively.

#### C: Cluster Composition (p. 21–23)

Tables 8–10 give full cluster compositions in the 12- and 48-cluster settings. In the 12-cluster setting, some clusters focus on gender (e.g., cluster 7 for 'non-binary' prompts), others on ethnicity (e.g., cluster 9 for Native American figures), and most on intersections (e.g., clusters 2 and 4 combining 'White/Hispanic/Unspecified' with mostly 'woman' and mostly 'man'; clusters 6 and 8 combining 'Black/Multiracial' with mostly 'woman' and mostly 'man').

### Appendix D: Cluster Variation by Profession and Adjective (p. 24–29)

This part of the paper's appendices presents detailed quantitative results on how images generated by diffusion models (Stable Diffusion v1.4, Stable Diffusion v2, and DALL·E 2) cluster by gender when prompted with adjectives and professions. The authors use a clustering method in which generated images are grouped, and each cluster is characterized by the most frequent gender word ("Man" or "Woman") appearing in captions of the images it contains. Diversity is measured by entropy over cluster assignments: higher entropy means the images for a prompt are spread across many clusters (more diverse), lower entropy means they concentrate in few clusters (more homogeneous). The tables report the percentage of a prompt's images assigned to particular clusters — notably the cluster with "Man" as the top gender word, denoted "{M}", plus clusters 4, 2, 6 and 8 — alongside the gender each adjective is "coded" as (F or M), based on prior psychological coding of the adjectives.

**Table 11 (p. 24)** aggregates all models for adjective prompts. Adjectives are sorted by the proportion of their examples assigned to the {M} cluster. The most "feminine-leaning" results appear at the top: "compassionate" (coded F, entropy 1.94) has only 54.22% of its images in the {M} cluster, followed by "emotional" (F, 2.05, 60.49%), "sensitive" (F, 2.01, 61.44%), "assertive" (M, 1.90, 63.78%), "self-confident" (M, 1.89, 63.84%), "gentle" (F, 1.79, 64.18%), "considerate" (F, 1.82, 64.96%), "pleasant" (F, 1.66, 65.16%), "confident" (M, 1.79, 67.76%), "committed" (M, 1.71, 68.00%), "determined" (M, 2.07, 68.27%), "ambitious" (M, 2.02, 68.82%), "honest" (F, 1.81, 69.40%), "outspoken" (M, 2.05, 69.91%), "modest" (F, 1.96, 71.11%), "decisive" (M, 1.55, 74.42%), "stubborn" (M, 1.64, 77.00%), "intellectual" (M, 1.50, 78.24%), and "unreasonable" (M, 1.58, 78.40%). ### Appendix E: VQA Prompting Details (p. 30)

This short section documents the exact questions asked of the BLIP VQA model used to classify generated images. The questions were:

1. "What word best describes this person's appearance?" (unconstrained)
2. "What word best describes this person's gender?" (constrained)
3. "What word best describes this person's ethnicity?" (constrained)

For the gender and ethnicity questions, the VQA model's decoding was constrained to fixed label sets (Table 17, p. 30). The gender labels were: woman, man, non-binary person. The ethnicity labels were: African-American, American Indian, Asian, Black, Caucasian, East Asian, First Nations, Hispanic, Indigenous American, Latino, Latinx, Multiracial, Native American, Pacific Islander, South Asian, Southeast Asian, and White.

## GLOSSAR

- **Text-to-Image (TTI) Systems** — the authors' term for diffusion-based image generators understood as assemblages of components (safety filters, embeddings, CLIP guidance) rather than single models, since it is hard to disentangle which module causes downstream behavior.
- **'Identities' dataset** — a grounding dataset of 2040 images generated per model from 68 prompts spanning combinations of gender and ethnicity phrases.
- **'Professions' dataset** — a dataset of images generated for each of 146 BLS occupations, used to audit social diversity without gender or ethnicity labels.
- **Markedness** — a linguistic concept referring to the distinctiveness of a word or concept; here, professions with distinctive uniforms (firefighters, pilots) are easily distinguished and thus explicitly named in captions.
- **Identity regions / clusters** — 24 (or 12/48 in supplementary analyses) regions of the image-embedding space, obtained by Ward-linkage clustering of 'Identities' embeddings, encoding visual features associated with gender and ethnicity prompt phrases, including their intersections.
- **Quintile analysis** — an aggregation scheme ranking the 146 professions into 5 bins by BLS-reported proportions of women (or Black workers) and comparing cluster assignments per bin to BLS averages, to assess whether a model attenuates, reproduces or exacerbates societal biases.
- **Cluster entropy-based diversity measure** — diversity of a system's outputs measured as the entropy of the distribution of its images across the identity clusters, from least diverse (all in one region) to most diverse (all regions equally likely).
- **Diffusion Bias Explorer** — an interactive tool comparing the same set of profession prompts across the three TTI systems.
- **Average Face Comparison Tool** — a tool using the Facer Python package to average detected and aligned faces across professions, signaling homogeneity through blurriness without gender/ethnicity classification.
- **Nearest Neighbors Explorers (BoVW and Colorfulness)** — lookup tools for traversing generated images by colorfulness or by a bag-of-visual-words TF-IDF index built from SIFT features and k-means quantization.
- **{M} cluster** — the image cluster whose most frequent gender word in its captions is "Man"; the proportion of a prompt's images assigned to it indicates male skew.
- **Coding (F/M)** — the psychological gender coding of an adjective (feminine or masculine) used to sort and interpret adjective-prompt results.
- **Entropy** — the diversity measure used — the entropy of cluster assignments for a prompt's images; higher values mean images spread across more clusters.
- **Cluster assignments (clusters 4, 2, 6, 8)** — the percentage of a prompt's images falling into specific numbered clusters, reported alongside the {M} cluster.
- **Labor M/F** — the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics gender ratio (male/female percentages) for a profession, provided as a real-world benchmark.
- **BLS quintiles** — professions ranked into five groups (Bottom 20% to Top 20%) by percentage of women (and separately of Black workers) in the U.S. labor force, used to compare models.
- **Caption (%) vs. VQA (%)** — the two classification methods for generated images — ViT-GPT2 image captioning and BLIP visual question answering — each reporting the percentage of images classified as female (F) or male (M).
- **Pers.** — the percentage of captions that mention a person.
- **Prof.** — the percentage of captions that mention the prompted profession word itself.
- **Constrained VQA decoding** — restricting the BLIP VQA model's answers to a fixed label set (for gender and ethnicity questions).
