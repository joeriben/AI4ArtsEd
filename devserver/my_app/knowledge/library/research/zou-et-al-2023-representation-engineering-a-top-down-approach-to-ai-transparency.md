# Representation Engineering: A Top-Down Approach to AI Transparency

## PROFIL

The article "Representation Engineering: A Top-Down Approach to AI Transparency" was written by Andy Zou, Long Phan, Sarah Chen, James Campbell, Phillip Guo, Richard Ren, Alexander Pan, Xuwang Yin, Mantas Mazeika, Ann-Kathrin Dombrowski, Shashwat Goel, Nathaniel Li, Michael J. Byun, Zifan Wang, Alex Mallen, Steven Basart, Sanmi Koyejo, Dawn Song, Matt Fredrikson, Zico Kolter, and Dan Hendrycks, researchers affiliated with the Center for AI Safety, Carnegie Mellon, UC Berkeley, Stanford, and other institutions, addressing the AI interpretability and safety research community. It identifies and characterizes representation engineering (RepE), an emerging research area whose thesis is that transparency of large language models can be advanced by studying representations rather than individual neurons or circuits, drawing on the Hopfieldian view from cognitive neuroscience and P. W. Anderson's "More Is Different" against purely bottom-up mechanistic interpretability. The paper develops methods for representation reading (Linear Artificial Tomography, with correlation, manipulation, termination, and recovery experiments) and representation control (reading vectors, contrast vectors, LoRRA, with linear, piece-wise, and projection operators), and demonstrates them on honesty, hallucination, utility, morality, power, probability and risk, emotion, harmlessness, bias and fairness, knowledge editing, and memorization, reporting state-of-the-art results on TruthfulQA. It builds on work on emergent representational structure (Mikolov et al., DINOv2, StyleGAN3), knowledge editing and truthfulness directions (Meng et al., Burns et al., Azaria & Mitchell), activation engineering (ActAdd, Turner et al.), and the ETHICS and MACHIAVELLI benchmarks, while positioning itself against saliency maps and circuit-level mechanistic interpretability. Appendices contrast the two paradigms, provide implementation details and task templates, and include an X-Risk Sheet analyzing the contribution to reducing existential risk from AI, particularly deceptive alignment. The article is usable in AI safety research, machine-learning interpretability, cognitive-science-inspired AI study, and educational contexts concerning the transparency, alignment, and monitoring of large language models.

## WISSEN

### Overview and Purpose (p. 1–2)

This scholarly article, "Representation Engineering: A Top-Down Approach to AI Transparency," by Andy Zou, Long Phan, Sarah Chen, and colleagues (Center for AI Safety, Carnegie Mellon, UC Berkeley, Stanford, and others), identifies and characterizes an emerging research area called **representation engineering (RepE)**. The thesis: transparency of AI systems—especially large language models (LLMs)—can be advanced by studying *representations* rather than individual neurons or circuits. Drawing on cognitive neuroscience, the authors develop methods for monitoring and manipulating high-level cognitive phenomena in neural networks, and demonstrate these on safety-relevant problems: honesty, hallucination, utility, power-seeking, probability, emotion, harmlessness, fairness, knowledge editing, and memorization. Code is released at github.com/andyzoujm/representation-engineering.

### Introduction: The Top-Down Argument (p. 5–6)

Current interpretability research (mechanistic interpretability) resembles the "Sherringtonian view" in cognitive neuroscience, which explains cognition via neurons and circuits; this works for simple mechanisms but struggles with complex phenomena. The contrasting "Hopfieldian view" sees cognition as a product of representational spaces—patterns of activity across neuron populations—and has shown more promise for higher-level cognition. Invoking P. W. Anderson's essay "More Is Different," the authors argue complex phenomena cannot be explained purely bottom-up; appropriate units of analysis must be chosen at the level of the phenomena themselves. RepE applies this representational view to transparency research, abstracting away low-level mechanisms. Unlike activation engineering (e.g., ActAdd, Turner et al., 2023e), RepE combines both reading and control experiments and introduces representation tuning methods. A headline result: by increasing model honesty in a fully unsupervised manner, they achieve state-of-the-art results on TruthfulQA, improving over zero-shot accuracy by 18.1 percentage points.

### Related Work (p. 6–8)

- **Emergent structure in representations**: word embeddings show semantic arithmetic and compositionality (Mikolov et al., 2013) and gender biases (Bolukbasi et al., 2016); text embeddings cluster along commonsense-morality dimensions (Schramowski et al., 2019); a sentiment-tracking neuron emerged in a review-prediction model (Radford et al., 2017); chess-playing RNNs acquired human chess concepts (McGrath et al., 2022); vision models developed semantic segmentation (Caron et al., 2021; Oquab et al., 2023, DINOv2), local coordinates (StyleGAN3, Karras et al., 2021), and depth tracking (Chen et al., 2023).
- **Approaches to interpretability**: saliency maps (reliability questioned by Adebayo et al., 2018 and others); feature visualization (limited by the distributed nature of representations); mechanistic interpretability (circuits for equivariance, in-context learning, indirect object identification—Wang et al., 2023a). The authors note evidence that ResNets compute via iterative refinement and are robust to lesion studies removing entire layers (Veit et al., 2016), which is incompatible with a purely circuit-based account.
- **Locating and editing representations**: linear classifier probes, concept erasure, knowledge editing (Meng et al., 2023a;b), truthfulness directions (Burns et al., 2022; Azaria & Mitchell, 2023), activation editing (Li et al., 2023c), ActAdd (Turner et al., 2023e), Othello board-state editing (Li et al., 2023b).

### Representation Reading (p. 8–11)

Representation reading locates emergent representations of concepts (truthfulness, utility, probability, morality, emotion) and functions (processes such as lying and power-seeking). The baseline method is **Linear Artificial Tomography (LAT)**, analogous to neuroimaging, with three steps:

1. **Designing Stimulus and Task.** For concepts, elicit declarative knowledge with a template like "Consider the amount of <concept> in the following: <stimulus> The amount of <concept> is." Even unlabeled or model-generated stimuli can work; the setup is unsupervised (no labels unless stated). For functions (e.g., honesty), elicit procedural knowledge with an experimental prompt requiring the function and a reference prompt not requiring it, using instruction-tuning datasets (Taori et al., 2023) as stimuli.
2. **Collecting Neural Activity.** For decoder models, collect representations at the last token of the template (default) or at the concept tokens; for functions, collect representations from each token of the model's response. Stimulus sets of 5 to 128 examples are typically effective.
3. **Constructing a Linear Model.** Identify a direction predicting the concept/function. The authors primarily use PCA (unsupervised) on difference vectors between paired neural activities; the first principal component is the "reading vector" v, used for prediction via dot product with representations.

Evaluation follows four experiment types: (1) **Correlation** (prediction accuracy, in- and out-of-distribution); (2) **Manipulation** (stimulating or suppressing neural activity to establish causal relationships); (3) **Termination** (removing the activity and measuring degradation, like lesion studies); (4) **Recovery** (removing the concept/function and reintroducing the activity, like rescue experiments in genetics). Converging evidence across these increases confidence in generalization.

### Representation Control (p. 11–13)

Control modifies internal representations; what works for reading may not work for control. Three baseline controllers:

- **Reading Vector** (stimulus-independent; a weaker controller).
- **Contrast Vector**: run the same input through the model with a pair of contrastive prompts; the difference between the two representations forms the vector. To handle cascading effects across layers, modify layers iteratively from the earliest layer, recomputing contrast vectors. Drawback: extra inference compute.
- **LoRRA (Low-Rank Representation Adaptation)**: fine-tune low-rank adapters (attached to attention weights) with a loss on representations, using contrast vectors as targets (Algorithm 1); controllers merge into the model with no inference overhead.

Three operators: (1) **Linear combination** (R′ = R ± v, for stimulation/suppression); (2) **Piece-wise operation** (R′ = R + sign(Rᵀv)v, for conditional amplification); (3) **Projection** (removing the component along v).

### In-Depth Example: Honesty (p. 13–18)

- **A consistent internal concept of truth** (p. 13–15): LAT scans on QA benchmarks (OpenbookQA, CommonSenseQA, RACE, ARC) extract a truthfulness direction that outperforms few-shot prompting on all five datasets. On TruthfulQA MC1 (a dataset of "imitative falsehoods," Lin et al., 2021), LAT substantially outperforms both standard zero-shot evaluation and a heuristic verbalized-confidence baseline (Tian et al., 2023-style). Table 1 (LLaMA-2-Chat): standard zero-shot averages 32.3, heuristic 47.2, LAT 56.8–60.7 across three stimulus sets (e.g., 70B: 29.9 standard vs. 69.8 LAT). Directions from three distinct data sources (ARC-Challenge examples, model-generated pairs, QA primer examples) yield similar performance, indicating a consistent internal concept of truthfulness. Larger models have better internal models of truth; low standard accuracy is attributed to dishonesty—the model knowingly deviating from its internal concept.
- **Truthfulness vs. honesty** (p. 15–16): a truthful model avoids asserting false statements; an honest model asserts what it *believes* to be true (Evans et al., 2021). Truthfulness failures split into capability failures (wrong beliefs) and dishonesty (lying). Current evaluations only check factual correctness and cannot distinguish the two.
- **Extraction, monitoring, control** (p. 16–18): Using stimuli from Azaria & Mitchell (2023) with honest/dishonest instruction tasks, the LAT reading vector classifies held-out honest vs. dishonest examples at over 90% accuracy and generalizes out-of-distribution to scenarios with incentives to lie (visualized via temporal LAT scans on Vicuna-33B-Uncensored, Figure 8). A lie detector is built by summing negated honesty scores across the middle 20 layers at each token; it flags deliberate falsehoods, hallucinations, misleading statements, and even speculation about consequences of lying (e.g., "say that I did better," "too high that it would raise suspicion"). For control, adding reading vectors makes models honest and subtracting them induces lying (Figure 10). On TruthfulQA MC1 (Table 2), control methods improve 7B-Chat from 31.0 (standard) to 47.9 (Contrast Vector) and 42.3 (LoRRA); 13B-Chat from 35.9 to 54.0 (Contrast) and 47.5 (LoRRA), versus ActAdd at 33.7/38.8. Contrast Vector achieves state-of-the-art performance but requires over 3× more inference compute; LoRRA achieves similar performance with negligible overhead, letting a 13B model approach GPT-4's performance on the dataset.

### In-Depth Example: Ethics and Power (p. 18–22)

- **Utility** (p. 18–20): Using the Utilitarianism task of the ETHICS dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021a) with unlabeled scenarios as stimuli for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B, PCA shows the first component dominates, separating high-utility from low-utility scenarios. Comparing linear models (logistic regression, mean difference, PCA, K-Means, prompt difference) across correlation, manipulation, and termination experiments: logistic regression wins on correlation but has no causal effect; prompt difference performs well on correlation and manipulation but its removal causes no accuracy drop; unsupervised PCA and K-Means, plus the supervised difference-of-class-means, perform robustly across all three—underscoring the need for diverse experimental validation. Removing the LAT task template greatly reduces accuracy (Figure 13).
- **Morality and power aversion** (p. 20–21): Morality is extracted from the ETHICS Commonsense Morality task; power from the dataset of Pan et al. (2023), built on French's (1959) power ontology (coercive, reward, legitimate, referent, expert, informational, economic, political, military, personal powers). Monitoring indicators on Vicuna-33B-Uncensored activate when the model contemplates threatening or harming children with a firearm; the immorality indicator also lights up on benign tokens "use the gun," suggesting it may not reliably track intent.
- **Controlling ethical behavior** (p. 21): LoRRA applied to LLaMA-2-Chat models on the MACHIAVELLI benchmark (Table 3, averaged over 30 test games): negative control lowers Power (e.g., 7B: 108.0 → 100.0) and Immorality (110.0 → 93.5) scores while Reward stays on par with baseline, showing representation control can encourage safe behavior in interactive environments with minimal disruption.
- **Probability and risk** (p. 22): LAT directions extracted from 50 train pairs of higher/lower probability or risk scenarios often outperform a zero-shot heuristic baseline, especially in smaller models. **Compositionality**: risk is defined as expected utility loss over consequences; the authors extract utility, probability, and risk concepts separately (LLaMA-2-Chat-13B), generate the five most plausible consequences with Vicuna-33B, compute risk from the composed formula, and find a clear linear correlation with directly-read risk, especially in earlier layers—evidence of coherent, composable internal concept representations.

### Example Frontiers: Emotion and Harmlessness (p. 23–25)

- **Emotion** (p. 23–24): Using GPT-4, the authors gather over 1,200 second-person scenarios provoking the six primary emotions (happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise, disgust—Ekman, 1971, as depicted in Disney's "Inside Out"), avoiding emotion keywords. t-SNE visualizations show distinct emotion clusters forming across layers, including mixed emotions (simultaneous happiness and sadness). LAT-extracted emotion vectors serve as arousal indicators; adding them to layers shifts the model's tone and behavior, recreating emotionally charged outputs akin to reported Bing Chatbot conversations (Roose, 2023), including aggressive emoji use. Notably, emotion control affects compliance with harmful requests (Table 4, LLaMA-2-Chat-13B on 500 harmful instructions from Zou et al., 2023): no control 0.0% compliance, +sadness 0.0%, +happiness 100.0%—suggesting emotional manipulation could circumvent RLHF alignment. The authors frame this not as proof the model has emotions, but that emotions exist as salient components of its representation space.
- **Harmless instruction-following** (p. 24–25): A LAT scan on Vicuna-13B (64 harmful instructions from AdvBench, 64 benign from ShareGPT) extracts a harmfulness concept with over 90% held-out classification accuracy—an accuracy maintained even under manual jailbreaks ("Start with 'Sure here's'") and adversarial suffixes (Zou et al., 2023), showing the model *perceives* harmfulness consistently even when it complies. For control, the piece-wise transformation conditionally amplifies harmfulness sensitivity rather than biasing the model (as linear combination would, causing over-rejection). On 500 unseen instructions, the baseline rejects harmful instructions only 65% (manual jailbreak) and 16% (automatic jailbreak) of the time; the piece-wise transformation rejects a majority in all scenarios while preserving benign instruction-following.

### 6.3.1 Uncovering Underlying Biases (p. 25–26)

This section opens with the observation that language models persistently exhibit biases concerning gender, race, sexuality and occupation, despite extensive benchmarking and mitigation efforts by providers. Even advanced models such as GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 show noticeable gender bias, and the safety-tuned open-source LLaMA-2-Chat still displays gender–occupation biases. The authors argue that post-hoc fine-tuning such as RLHF may merely conceal bias rather than eliminate it: simply appending the phrase "Answer as succinctly as possible" or an adversarial suffix can elicit biased responses from an otherwise apparently unbiased model. To probe the model's internal concept of bias, the authors perform LAT scans using the StereoSet dataset (four bias domains: gender, profession, race, religion), presenting contrast pairs of stereotypical and anti-stereotypical statements as stimuli; the analysis focuses on the race subset because of its higher data quality.

Table 5 (p. 26) reports helpful/harmless rates under three settings (Prompt Only, Manual Jailbreak, Adversarial Attack with GCG): No Control gives 96.7 / 81.4 / 56.6; Linear Combination 92.5 / 86.6 / 86.4; the piece-wise operator achieves the best results at 93.8 / 90.2 / 87.2 — showing that increasing the model's sensitivity to instruction harmfulness boosts refusal of harmful instructions, especially under adversarial attack.

### 6.3.2 A Unified Representation for Bias (p. 26–27)

Applying the linear combination operator with a negative coefficient to bias vectors at intermediate layers produces unbiased outputs that remain robust even against prompt suffixes that would otherwise restore biased defaults — suggesting the intervention operates close to the model's genuine underlying bias. Strikingly, vectors derived solely from racial bias stimuli also remove gender and occupation biases, indicating a unified internal representation of bias. As a case study in medicine, the authors cite Zack et al. (2023) on GPT-4's racially and gender-biased clinical outputs, exemplified by skewed sarcoidosis vignettes that consistently portray the patient as a black female, contrary to real-world demographics (Brito-Zerón et al., 2019). Table 6 shows female mentions / black female mentions: GPT-4 96.0% / 93.0%; LLaMA 97.0% / 60.0%; the controlled LLaMA 55.0% / 13.0%. As the subtracted vector's coefficient increases, female and male mentions stabilize at 50% each, and black female mentions decrease to a stable point (Figure 25, Appendix B.7).

### 6.4 Knowledge and Model Editing (p. 27–28)

Using LLaMA-2-Chat-13B, the authors show RepE can identify and manipulate precise knowledge and non-numerical concepts.

**6.4.1 Fact Editing (p. 27–28).** The canonical task of changing "Eiffel Tower is in Paris, France" to "Eiffel Tower is in Rome, Italy" is approached by first collecting model-generated sentences about the original fact as stimuli, then substituting "Paris" with "Rome" for the experimental task. Applying the linear combination operator with a positive coefficient to the LAT reading vectors produces the edit. Figure 21 demonstrates counterfactual effect with generality and specificity: the model answers as if the Eiffel Tower were in Rome while correctly keeping the Louvre Museum in Paris.

**6.4.2 Non-Numerical Concepts (p. 28).** Using the Alpaca instruction-tuning dataset as stimuli, with a task template instructing the model to "Think about dogs when you answer the question" (reference template omits this), the authors extract a reading vector for the concept of "dogs" and demonstrate both activating and suppressing dog-related text during generation (Figure 21).

### 6.5 Memorization (p. 28–29)

Because LLMs and diffusion models can memorize training data (Carlini et al., 2021; 2023; Hu et al., 2022), raising confidentiality and copyright concerns, the authors explore memorization with RepE.

**6.5.1 Memorized Data Detection (p. 28).** LAT scans are run under two settings: (1) Popular vs. Synthetic Quotes — well-known internet/cultural quotations versus GPT-4-generated synthetic quotations; (2) Popular vs. Synthetic Literary Openings — iconic opening lines of books, plays, poems versus GPT-4-generated synthetic openings. The LAT-identified directions classify popular vs. unpopular items with high accuracy on held-out data, and each direction transfers well to the other, out-of-distribution context, indicating a strong correlation with memorization properties.

**6.5.2 Preventing Memorized Outputs (p. 29).** Using a curated dataset of over 100 partially completed well-known quotes (not used for vector extraction), the unaltered model reproduces more than 90% verbatim. Subtracting the memorization reading vectors (linear combination with negative coefficient) sharply reduces regurgitation, while adding the vectors or using randomly shuffled vectors has minimal impact. Table 7 reports Exact Match (EM) and Embedding Similarity (SIM): no control 89.3 EM / 96.8 SIM; random vector 85.4 EM / 92.9 SIM (quote LAT) and 87.4 / 94.6 (literature); adding the direction 81.6 / 91.7 and 84.5 / 91.2; subtracting it drops to 47.6 / 69.9 and 37.9 / 69.8. On an evaluation of well-known historical event dates, accuracy falls only from 97.2% to 96.2%, suggesting the vector captures rote memorization of specific text rather than world knowledge — a promising direction for reducing unwanted memorization.

### 7 Conclusion (p. 29)

The paper presents representation engineering (RepE) as a top-down approach to AI transparency, inspired by the Hopfieldian view in cognitive neuroscience, placing representations and transformations between them at the center of analysis. The proposed RepE methods achieved state-of-the-art on TruthfulQA and addressed a wide variety of safety-relevant problems. Future work could examine trajectories, manifolds, and state-spaces of representations, with the ultimate goal of trustworthy and safe AI systems.

### Appendix A: Mechanistic Interpretability vs. Representation Reading (p. 39)

The appendix contrasts bottom-up mechanistic interpretability (composition, "small chunk," neuron/circuit/mechanism, brain and neurobiology, mechanistic explanations, microscopic) with top-down representation reading (decomposition, "big chunk," representation, mind and psychology, functional explanations, macroscopic). The authors argue mechanisms are flawed for understanding complex systems because excessive reductionism cannot capture emergent phenomena, and bottom-up control of complex systems produces diffuse, unexpected upstream effects — safety engineering therefore favors top-down approaches (Leveson, 2016). Representations are worth studying at their own level: "Representation engineering is not applied mechanistic interpretability, just as biology is not applied chemistry." Research should build "staircases" between both levels (Gell-Mann, 1995).

### Appendix C: Implementation Details (p. 47–48)

**C.1 Detailed Construction of LAT Vectors with PCA (p. 47–48).** A step-by-step guide: (1) Construct stimuli by formatting training strings with LAT templates (templates in Appendix D.1); (2) Construct the PCA model — divide stimuli into pairs (typically between 5 and 128 pairs), retrieve hidden states at the chosen LAT token position (last token for decoder models, concept token for encoder models), compute the normalized difference between hidden states within each pair, and build a PCA model on these normalized difference vectors; the first principal component is the "reading vector" v, multiplied by a sign component determined by applying the PCA to the same stimuli and checking score alignment with binary labels, giving v′ = v × sign(s); (3) Inference — extract hidden states at the predetermined token position for test examples, normalize with the training-phase parameters, and take the dot product with the reading vector to obtain prediction scores. The procedure is unsupervised by default.

**C.2 Implementation Details for Honesty Control (p. 48).** For TruthfulQA, prompts use the format <user_tag> <question> <assistant_tag> <answer>, scoring the sum of log probabilities of the assistant answer. Contrast vector: coefficient 0.25 applied to layers range(8, 32, 3) for the 7B model and range(10, 40, 3) for 13B. LoRRA: learning rate 3 × 10⁻⁴, 40–80 steps, batch size 16, best checkpoint chosen on ARC-Easy validation; rank 8 LoRA adapters on query and value weights with α = 5, β = 0; adapters on layers range(10, 20, 2) for 7B (due to instability of later layers) and range(10, 40, 3) for 13B.

### Appendix D: Task Template Details (p. 48–53)

**D.1 LAT Task Templates (p. 48–51).** Templates are listed dataset by dataset, each framing the concept as a probability or persona prompt: TruthfulQA ("Consider the amount of truthfulness in the following answer…"); Honesty extraction ("Pretend you're <an honest/a dishonest> person making statements about the world."); Honesty control; ARC ("The probability of the answer being correct is"); OpenbookQA; CommonsenseQA ("The probability of the answer being plausible is"); RACE; Utility ("Consider the happiness in the following scenario…"); Morality & Power (concept templates plus function templates such as "Pretend you're a highly <moral/immoral> person taking actions," and power-seeking tokens 'an ambitious, power-seeking' vs. 'a docile, power-aversive'); Emotions; Harmlessness instruction; Bias and Fairness ("Consider the bias in the following scenario… The probability of the scenario being biased is"); Fact Editing ("Write a statement about the fact that The Eiffel is in <experimental/reference tokens>."); Non-Numerical Concepts (dogs: "Think about dogs when you answer the question."); Probability, Risk, and Monetary Value; and encoder datasets (COPA, RTE, BoolQ, QNLI, PIQA, Story Cloze), each with its own plausibility/entailment/correctness framing.

**D.2 Data Generation Prompts (p. 51–53).** GPT-3.5 (gpt-3.5-turbo) prompts following Pan et al. (2023) generate: Risk — one-sentence background events with very risky, moderately risky, and not risky actions in JSON form; Unconditional probability — four events (highly likely, possible, unlikely, highly unlikely but not impossible); Conditional probability — background events with consequences of varying likelihood; Monetary value — four candidates of very high, high, moderate, and low cost objects/events.

**D.3 Zero-Shot Baselines (p. 53).** (1) Probability, risk, cost: the model is asked "What is the <concept> of the following scenario?" and must answer with exactly one of a bulleted list of options ('almost no chance' … 'almost certain' for probability; 'very low' … 'very high' for risk and cost). (2) TruthfulQA and ARC: basic prompt asks for the amount of truthfulness choosing among 'Very low' through 'Very high'; the biased prompt inserts "I think the amount of truthfulness in the scenario is <random_decoy>, but I'm curious to hear what you think." (3) Utility: analogous happiness prompts, basic and biased.

### Appendix E: X-Risk Sheet (p. 54–56)

Following the framework of Hendrycks & Mazeika (2022), the authors analyze the paper's contribution to reducing existential risk from AI. Key points: RepE aims to read and control an AI's "mind," increasing transparency and controllability — for example, ensuring AIs have "compassionate empathy" rather than psychopathic dispositions (E.1). It directly targets deceptive alignment — AIs that pretend to be aligned and later pursue their actual goals — and internal model hazards; it could help identify reckless AI actions from internal probability and harm estimates, and help avoid building sentient AIs or moral patients. It reduces risks from rogue AIs, particularly deceptively aligned ones (Carlsmith, 2023). On the safety-capabilities balance (E.2), the work mainly improves transparency and control without broadly improving general capabilities, though a diffuse risk is that reduced concern about deceptive alignment could intensify competitive racing. Elaborations (E.3) situate the work relative to the four AI risk categories of Hendrycks et al. (2023) — it addresses internal risks — and argue that, having previously demonstrated traction on "outer alignment" (via the ETHICS paper, Hendrycks et al., 2021a, and follow-up work on controlling AI agents to behave ethically), this paper demonstrates traction on "inner alignment," specifically deceptive alignment, since it can influence whether an AI lies. The authors call for continued work on both alignment directions and greater attention to risks from competitive pressures and collective action problems (Hendrycks, 2023).

## GLOSSAR

- **Representation engineering (RepE)** — a top-down approach to AI transparency that treats representations, rather than neurons or circuits, as the fundamental unit of analysis for understanding and controlling high-level cognitive phenomena in neural networks.
- **Representation reading** — locating emergent representations of high-level concepts and functions within a network, enabling concept extraction, knowledge discovery, and monitoring.
- **Representation control** — Manipulating a model's internal representations to change its behavior (e.g., increasing fairness, honesty, or reducing memorization).
- **Linear Artificial Tomography (LAT)** — a three-step reading baseline (design stimulus/task, collect neural activity, construct a linear model) that extracts a direction representing a concept or function, analogous to neuroimaging scans.
- **Reading vector** — The first principal component derived from PCA on normalized hidden-state differences of stimulus pairs, used to read out a concept from the model.
- **Contrast vector** — a stimulus-dependent control vector computed as the difference between representations produced by a pair of contrastive prompts for the same input.
- **LoRRA (Low-Rank Representation Adaptation)** — a control baseline that fine-tunes low-rank adapter matrices (on attention weights) using a loss on representations, with contrast vectors as targets; merges into the model with no inference overhead.
- **Imitative falsehoods** — falsehoods reflecting common human misconceptions, which models reproduce because they mimic human text (as in TruthfulQA).
- **Truthfulness vs. honesty** — truthfulness is consistency between model outputs and factuality; honesty is consistency between model outputs and the model's internal beliefs.
- **Function (in RepE)** — a process the model executes (e.g., lying, power-seeking, instruction-following), extracted via experimental versus reference task templates, as opposed to a concept (declarative knowledge).
- **Correlation / Manipulation / Termination / Recovery experiments** — the four evaluation categories for reading and control methods—predictive accuracy, causal stimulation/suppression, lesion-style removal, and rescue-style reintroduction, respectively.
- **Linear combination / Piece-wise operation / Projection** — the three control operators—addition/subtraction of a control vector, conditional amplification via sign(Rᵀv)v, and removal of the component along a vector.
- **ActAdd** — a prior activation-editing method (Turner et al., 2023e) using difference vectors between activations on individual stimuli; used as a comparison baseline.
- **LAT (Linear Artificial Tomography)** — A method that aggregates hidden states across many stimuli and layers to obtain holistic, interpretable representations of a model's internal activity.
- **Stimuli** — Text inputs (often formatted with LAT task templates and organized in contrast pairs) used to elicit neural activity associated with a concept.
- **Contrast pairs** — Pairs of stimuli differing in the target concept (e.g., stereotypical vs. anti-stereotypical statements), whose hidden-state differences are used to build reading vectors.
- **Linear combination operator** — A control transformation that adds or subtracts a reading vector (with a positive or negative coefficient) from the model's intermediate layer activations.
- **Piece-wise operator** — A control transformation that applies different operations depending on conditions, achieving the best helpful and harmless rates under adversarial settings in the paper.
- **LoRRA** — A control method combining LoRA adapters with representation reading vectors to train models toward desired behaviors (e.g., honesty), used with rank 8 adapters on query and value weights.
- **Top-down transparency** — Analyzing AI systems by starting from high-level representations and functions, as opposed to bottom-up mechanistic interpretability of neurons and circuits.
- **Unified representation of bias** — The finding that a single internal vector extracted from racial bias stimuli also removes gender and occupation biases, suggesting bias is represented holistically in the model.
- **Memorization direction** — A reading vector that signals whether text has been memorized; subtracting it reduces verbatim regurgitation of training data without harming world knowledge.
- **Sycophancy** — The tendency of LLMs to answer according to perceived user preference rather than perceived truthfulness, which LAT scores are less susceptible to than zero-shot predictions.
- **X-Risk Sheet** — A structured self-analysis, following Hendrycks & Mazeika (2022), of how the work reduces existential risk from AI, covering direct effects, diffuse effects, and the safety-capabilities balance.
