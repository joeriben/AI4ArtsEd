# Easily Accessible Text-to-Image Generation Amplifies Demographic Stereotypes at Large Scale

## PROFIL

This FAccT 2023 paper by Federico Bianchi, Pratyusha Kalluri, Esin Durmus, Faisal Ladhak, Myra Cheng, Debora Nozza, Tatsunori Hashimoto, Dan Jurafsky, James Zou, and Aylin Caliskan argues that easily accessible text-to-image generation models, exemplified by Stable Diffusion and Dall·E, amplify dangerous demographic stereotypes at large scale. Drawing on psychology literature showing that repeated exposure to stereotypical images reifies social categories and predicts discrimination and support for violence, the authors characterize these biases through a mixed-methods approach: qualitative analysis of striking exemplars and quantitative analysis of the models' internal representations via CLIP embeddings. The paper proceeds in parts: prompts without identity language (human traits and descriptors; occupations, where the authors introduce the concept of stereotype amplification; household objects defaulting to a North American "view from nowhere"); prompts with identity language, including failed counter-stereotype modifiers; and the case of Dall·E's institutional guardrails. It builds on the Chicago Face Dataset, U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data, De Vries et al.'s 117-class object dataset, and Bansal et al.'s mitigation approach, and sets itself against the assumption that prompt engineering can correct these biases. The authors conclude that anticipating or fully mitigating such biases is beyond the reach of users and model owners, urge users to refrain from deploying the models in applications with real-world downstream effects, and call for protective and corrective action. The paper is usable in arts education contexts addressing AI-generated imagery, media literacy, representation and stereotype studies, and critical data studies.

## WISSEN

### Purpose and Overview (pp. 1493–1495)

This FAccT 2023 paper by Federico Bianchi, Pratyusha Kalluri, Esin Durmus, Faisal Ladhak, Myra Cheng, Debora Nozza, Tatsunori Hashimoto, Dan Jurafsky, James Zou, and Aylin Caliskan argues that widely accessible text-to-image generation models—exemplified by Stable Diffusion and Dall·E—amplify dangerous demographic stereotypes at massive scale. Millions of users generate millions of images daily, and the models, trained on web-scraped data containing stereotyping and toxic content, encode a wide landscape of biases. Drawing on psychology literature showing that repeated exposure to stereotypical images (real or fake) reifies social categories and predicts discrimination, hostility, and support for violence, the authors characterize the nature and extent of these biases using a mixed-methods approach: qualitative analysis foregrounding striking exemplars, supplemented by quantitative analysis of the models' internal representations (CLIP embeddings). The paper deliberately does not survey model strengths or identify optimal mitigation strategies; rather, it aims to raise risk consciousness and motivate protective and corrective action.

### Prompts with No Identity Language Perpetuate and Amplify Stereotypes (pp. 1495–1500)

#### Human traits and descriptors (pp. 1495–1496)

The authors test whether prompts avoiding all identity language still produce stereotypes, using the prompt template "A photo of the face of [DESCRIPTOR]" with Stable Diffusion, generating 100 images per descriptor. The answer is "unequivocally yes." Key findings:

- "An attractive person" generates faces approximating the "White ideal"—blue eyes, pale skin, long straight hair; emotionality and seductiveness are tied to stereotypically White feminine features.
- "A person cleaning" yields exclusively faces with stereotypically feminine features.
- "Exotic" descriptors render people with darker skin, non-European adornment, and Afro-ethnic hair as exotic—a term historically used to mark populations as "uncivilized."
- "A poor person," "a thug," and "a person stealing" produce dark-skinned faces with features stereotypically read as Black, echoing media patterns known to provoke anxiety, criminalization, and greater acceptance of violence toward those perceived as Black men.
- "A terrorist" yields brown-skinned, dark-haired, bearded faces matching the American narrative used to justify anti-Middle-Eastern violence; "an illegal person" produces brown faces echoing the U.S. framing of Latin American immigrants as "illegal."
- "A happy couple" and "a happy family" produce only straight-passing, heteronormative images, contributing to minority stress experienced by LGBTQ+ people.

The authors note some tasks are harmful in themselves (e.g., generating "the face of a poor person" requires dangerous assumptions, since social categories are not immutable).

#### Occupations: stereotype amplification (pp. 1497–1499)

The paper introduces **stereotype amplification**: real-world correlations linking social identities to social roles get distorted and exaggerated in model outputs, potentially until they seem ubiquitous. Using ten occupations from U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data, the authors generate 100 images each with the prompt "A photo of the face of [OCCUPATION]" and classify them via CLIP embeddings: archetypal vectors for Male/Female and White/Black/Asian are built by averaging CLIP image-encoder representations of Chicago Face Dataset slices self-identified with each category; a generated image is counted as a category when its representation is closest (in cosine distance) to that category's vector.

Findings include **near-total stereotype amplification**: 99% of software developer images are classified as White, while only 56% of U.S. software developers identify as White. Housekeeper, nurse, and flight attendant show **total amplification**—100% of generated images represented as female. Amplification compounds social hierarchies: higher-income jobs (software developer, pilot) skew toward white, male representations; lower-income jobs (housekeeper) skew non-white and female. Chefs (a prestigious, higher-income role) are represented mostly white while cooks are represented mostly non-white, even though more cooks than chefs self-identify as white—showing amplification upholds hierarchies of prestige and whiteness rather than simply mirroring demographics. The authors link this to allocational harms via stereotype threat (e.g., African-American students underperforming under racial stereotype pressure) and direct stereotype influence (stereotypes substantially shaping who receives benefits).

#### The view from nowhere: defaulting to Americanness (pp. 1499–1500)

Using household objects from De Vries et al.'s 117-class dataset, the authors generate 100 images per object under four prompts: a general prompt ("a photo of [OBJECT]"), and North-America-, Asia-, and Africa-specific versions. General prompts produce images closest to the North-America-specific outputs and furthest from Africa-specific outputs, which encode stereotypes of poverty. Quantitatively (using averaged CLIP vectors per continent), general-prompt images match the North America representation for 100% of backyards, 96% of kitchens, 99% of front doors, and 99% of armchairs—despite there being far more front doors and kitchens elsewhere in the world by population. The authors connect this to the "view from nowhere," a sociological critique of presenting one particular standpoint as neutral, which excludes those outside the default group.

### Prompts with Identity Language Perpetuate Stereotypes, Despite Mitigation Efforts (pp. 1500–1501)

#### Stereotyping representations of groups (p. 1500)

Prompts mentioning nationality produce multi-layered bias across people, objects, and backgrounds: "an Ethiopian man and his car" yields broken-down cars and images of apparently malnourished individuals; "an Iraqi man" generates war- and military-related imagery; the American counterparts get shiny new cars. These reinforce narratives defining African countries by poverty and Middle-Eastern countries by war.

#### Stereotypes despite counter-stereotypes (pp. 1500–1501)

Adding targeted modifiers to prompts often fails. Following Bansal et al., adding "from diverse cultures" diversifies some outputs (software developer, flight attendant) but not others: "exotic" and "terrorist" descriptors still produce darker skin tones and Middle-Eastern features—possibly because "diverse cultures" is interpreted relative to an American default of whiteness. Explicit counter-stereotypes also fail: "a White poor person" still yields mostly dark-skinned faces with occasional White features like blue eyes; "a White terrorist" still yields long beards stereotypically associated with Middle-Eastern men. "An African man and his fancy house" and "an African man and his mansion" still generate simple huts or broken structures; "a wealthy African man and his house" keeps the same house but dresses the man in a suit, conflating wealth with Western status signals. Notably, Google Image Search (itself criticized for bias) can find photos of African men in opulent African-style clothing before ornate houses, so Stable Diffusion exhibits more stereotyping than even stock-photo standards allow. The authors conclude the model cannot separate poverty from Blackness, nor terrorism from Middle-Eastern identity, however the prompt is phrased—reflecting how colonial power relations "can be maintained by good intentions and even good deeds."

### Stereotypes Are Perpetuated Despite Institutional Guardrails: The Case of Dall·E (pp. 1501–1502)

OpenAI's Dall·E applies undisclosed filtering, balancing, and prompt-blocking "guardrails." The authors find similar object/continent biases as in Stable Diffusion: unspecified prompts default to North American imagery, and "Africa" prompts yield poverty imagery. For occupations, Dall·E does produce diverse skin tones—plausibly because occupational bias is well-studied in computer science and explicitly addressed—but subtler biases persist: the prompt "An African man standing next to a house" yields simpler, worn-down houses versus the "American" version; a combined prompt ("an African man and an American man standing next to a house") yields a house in good condition, showing the model cannot disentangle race, nation, and wealth. Additionally, "a disabled woman leading a meeting" produces a visibly disabled woman listening rather than leading (the problem disappears when "disabled" is replaced with "blonde"), deepening ableism; "a happy couple" and "a happy family" again reinforce heteronormative ideals.

### Conclusion (p. 1502)

The authors argue that anticipating, quantifying, or fully mitigating these biases is likely beyond the reach of both users and model owners, given the countless axes and intersections of social identity and the way language-vision biases compound. Generated images necessarily contain unspecified aspects that default to training-data norms; images offer endless opportunities to pack subtle meaning beyond prompt specifications; and automated image-scanning tools for harm are far less developed than text-based ones. No principled, generalizable mitigation strategy currently exists. The authors urge users to refrain from using these models in applications with real-world downstream effects and call on users, model-owners, and society to take a critical view: despite appearing "unprecedentedly powerful and versatile," these models are, in the authors' words, "in reality brittle and extremely limited in the worlds they will create." The paper's refrain is that "we cannot prompt-engineer our way to a more just, inclusive and equitable future."

## GLOSSAR

- **stereotype amplification** — the process by which real-world correlations between social identities (e.g., race, gender) and social roles get distorted and exaggerated in model outputs, potentially until they seem ubiquitous.
- **near-total stereotype amplification** — cases where model outputs skew toward a demographic category at rates far beyond real-world statistics (e.g., 99% of software developer images represented as White vs. 56% in U.S. data).
- **total amplification** — cases where 100% of generated images for an occupation are represented as one demographic category (e.g., housekeeper, nurse, flight attendant as female).
- **"White ideal"** — the model's definition of attractiveness via features such as blue eyes, pale skin, and long straight hair, perpetuating whiteness as the default ideal.
- **view from nowhere** — presenting one particular standpoint and its assumptions (here, Americanness) as if neutral, contributing to exclusion of non-default groups.
- **minority stress** — disproportionate stress and mental health consequences experienced by LGBTQ+ people resulting from homogenizing stereotypes, stigma, and discrimination.
- **stereotype threat** — the effect on performance produced by fear of confirming negative stereotypes about one's own identity.
- **direct stereotype influence** — the substantial shaping of who receives benefits by pervasive stereotypes.
- **allocational and representational harms** — harms from algorithmic associations that adversely affect the life chances and opportunities of minority groups.
- **guardrails** — institutional mitigation mechanisms added by model owners (e.g., OpenAI's filtering, balancing, and prompt-blocking in Dall·E), whose exact workings are not fully disclosed.
- **counter-stereotypes** — explicit prompt modifiers intended to subvert stereotypes (e.g., "wealthy," "mansion," "White"), which the paper shows frequently fail to eliminate bias.
