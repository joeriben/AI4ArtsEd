# A Framework for Culture and Arts Education

## PROFIL

UNESCO's "A Framework for Culture and Arts Education" is the outcome document of the UNESCO World Conference on Culture and Arts Education (Abu Dhabi, 13–15 February 2024), adopted by Ministers of Culture and Education of UNESCO Member States and Associate Members after a consultation process initiated by a Member States decision of March 2021. It positions culture and the arts — defined broadly, per the MONDIACULT Declarations of 1982 and 2022, and with education defined per the 2023 Recommendation on Education for Peace and Human Rights — as integral to holistic development, well-being and sustainable development, and treats culture and arts education as an ecosystem spanning formal, non-formal and informal settings. The document contains a Preamble and Introduction anchoring it in the UNESCO Constitution, Article 27 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the Education 2030 agenda and SDG 4, and recalling the Lisbon 2006 Road Map, the Seoul 2010 Seoul Agenda and the MONDIACULT 2022 Declaration; then seven guiding principles (common good, human rights, gender equality, cultural diversity, inclusion, co-creation, lifelong and lifewide learning); three objectives (contribution to sustainable development and peace, harnessing digital technologies and AI, integration into policies and curricula); a scope statement; five strategic goals (access and equity, contextual quality learning, appreciation of cultural diversity and critical engagement, skills for resilient and sustainable futures, institutionalization of ecosystems); implementation modalities covering governance, learning environments and experiences, teachers and educators, digital technologies and AI, partnerships, financing, and research and data; and a monitoring section providing for voluntary four-yearly national progress reports starting from 2025 and possible UNESCO Category 2 Centres. It is addressed to policy-makers in the culture and education sectors and usable across all arts disciplines and educational levels, from early childhood care through TVET and higher education, in formal, non-formal and informal settings, with particular attention to Indigenous Peoples, persons with disabilities, and learners in vulnerable, crisis-affected and post-conflict situations.

## WISSEN

### Preamble (pp. 1–2)

The Framework is the outcome document of the UNESCO World Conference on Culture and Arts Education, held in Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates, from 13 to 15 February 2024, attended by Ministers of Culture and Education. The final draft integrates comments from UNESCO Member States and Associate Members submitted during a second round of consultation (18 December 2023 – 10 January 2024).

The Ministers anchor the Framework in the UNESCO Constitution (which commits the Organization to "give fresh impulse to popular education and to the spread of culture") and in Article 27 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights ("everyone has the right freely to participate in the cultural life of the community, to enjoy the arts"). They situate it in a context of rising inequalities, armed conflict, disinformation, hate speech, racism and xenophobia, and link it to UN education efforts: the Education 2030 Incheon Declaration and Framework for Action for SDG 4, and the Transforming Education Summit (2022), including SDG Target 4.7 on education for sustainable development, human rights, gender equality, a culture of peace, global citizenship and appreciation of cultural diversity.

The Preamble affirms that culture and the arts are integral to holistic development, resilience and well-being; that they nurture imagination, creativity and self-expression; and that they open social and economic prospects, notably in the cultural and creative industries. It recalls the MONDIACULT 2022 Declaration's call for culture and education to work in synergy, and the two earlier World Conferences on Arts Education — Lisbon 2006 (Road Map for Arts Education) and Seoul 2010 (Seoul Agenda: Goals for the Development of Arts Education). The Ministers endorse the Framework and entrust UNESCO to support implementation through operational guidance, advocacy, policy dialogue, knowledge sharing, standard setting, convening stakeholders, and monitoring progress. Footnote 1 lists the foundational instruments, including in culture: the Universal Declaration on Cultural Diversity (2001), the Recommendation concerning the Status of the Artist (1980), the Recommendation on Museums and Collections (2015), the Hague Convention (1954) and its Protocols (1954, 1999), the 1970 Convention on illicit traffic, the World Heritage Convention (1972), the Underwater Cultural Heritage Convention (2001), the Intangible Cultural Heritage Convention (2003) and the 2005 Convention on the Diversity of Cultural Expressions; in education: the Dakar Framework for Action (2000), the 2030 Agenda and SDG 4 (2015), the Futures of Education initiative (2021), the Transforming Education Summit (2022) and the 2023 Recommendation on education for peace and human rights.

### Introduction (p. 3)

The Introduction states that digital transformation, human mobility, poverty, health and well-being issues, inequality, climate change, biodiversity loss, natural disasters, armed conflicts and the lasting impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic have created new realities requiring reinvestment in cultural capacities and a rethinking of education for learners of all ages throughout life.

It argues that culture and the arts enrich and revitalize education by giving diverse learners — including those in vulnerable situations — access to diverse forms of expression, ways of thinking, knowing, being and doing, histories and languages, boosting self-confidence, motivation and learning. Learning "in, through and with" culture and the arts develops cognitive, social-emotional and behavioural skills, strengthens holistic learning and environmental sensitivity, and fosters intercultural dialogue.

In March 2021, UNESCO Member States decided to elaborate the Framework in order to: promote greater access and inclusion in education and culture; capture broadened understandings of culture and arts education in formal, non-formal and informal settings; ensure effective integration of culture and arts education in relevant policies, strategies, curricula and programmes; widen cooperation across public policy areas, disciplines and education settings; and support its role in sustainable economic, social and environmental development. Its development involved inclusive, participatory consultation.

The Framework defines culture (per the MONDIACULT Declarations of 1982 and 2022) as the "set of distinctive spiritual, material, intellectual and emotional features that characterize a society or social group," including arts and letters, modes of life, fundamental rights, value systems, traditions and beliefs — transmittable through words, sound, images, movement, monuments and objects, digital media, and traditional knowledge (local and Indigenous knowledge systems, living cultural heritage). Education is defined (per the 2023 Recommendation on Education for Peace and Human Rights) as "an inalienable human right," a lifelong and society-wide process through which everyone develops to their fullest potential. "Culture and Arts Education" includes teaching and learning about, with and through the arts and culture, and positions culture — including the arts — as an educational tool, an approach, and a field of study, research and practice.

### I. Guiding principles (pp. 3–4)

Culture and arts education should be holistic, transformative and impactful, guided by seven principles:

a. Ensure culture and arts education as a **common good of humanity**, accessible to all, fostering well-being; this requires strengthening its role in policy, education systems and society, and sustained public investment.
b. Ground it in **human rights and fundamental freedoms** (UN Charter, Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the two International Covenants, and other instruments).
c. **Mainstream gender equality** in all policy planning, design and implementation; counter gender discrimination, bias, harmful content and violence in all educational settings.
d. Recognize **cultural diversity** as a defining feature and common heritage of humanity, protected through mutual understanding, equitable access to diverse cultural expressions, and linguistic diversity in physical, virtual and blended settings.
e. Ensure **inclusion, non-discrimination and respect for diversity**, addressing bullying, stereotypes, discriminatory and hateful bias, and actions inciting discrimination, racism, xenophobia, hostility or violence.
f. Enable **co-creation** with the active, meaningful participation of all actors and beneficiaries — learners (especially youth), educators, cultural professionals and practitioners, communities and other stakeholders — in design, implementation and evaluation across formal, non-formal and informal settings.
g. Promote **lifelong and lifewide learning** in diverse settings: physical (classrooms, community spaces, workplaces, cultural and natural spaces), virtual (digital platforms), and blended.

### II. Objectives (pp. 4–5)

The Framework provides guidance and policy recommendations to Member States and Associate Members for three purposes:

i. Ensure culture and arts education contributes directly to sustainable development and peace, in line with the 2030 Agenda and all 17 SDGs — in particular SDG 4 (inclusive, equitable quality education and lifelong learning) and SDG 8 (inclusive, sustainable economic growth, employment and decent work).
ii. Harness technological advances while identifying, preventing and mitigating risks, notably in digital technologies and Artificial Intelligence (AI), promoting ethical and responsible use, particularly for the educational, cultural and creative sectors.
iii. Integrate culture and arts education effectively in relevant policies, strategies and programmes across the culture and education sectors — including regulations, curricula, teacher training, qualifications and professional development — for lifelong and lifewide development of knowledge, skills, attitudes, values and behaviours such as creativity, critical thinking and artistic skills.

### III. Scope (p. 5)

The Framework treats culture and arts education as an **ecosystem**: educational activities for all people, in all contexts and in formal, non-formal and informal settings, using diverse pedagogies — especially those embracing diverse cultural perspectives, activities, practices, expressions, materials and objects — and modalities (offline, online, distance, blended), at all levels and of all types. It is grounded in a broad understanding of culture, including processes such as intercultural dialogue and values such as cultural, linguistic and knowledge diversity. It promotes multistakeholder collaboration among educational and cultural institutions, government bodies, cultural spaces and activities, memorial and heritage sites, artists and other cultural professionals and practitioners, researchers, local communities, the private sector, foundations and civil society organizations.

### IV. Strategic goals (pp. 5–7)

**a) Access, inclusion and equity in and through culture and arts education (pp. 5–6).** Access is the critical foundation for the rights to education and cultural life; obstacles range from limited infrastructure and resources to vulnerability and exclusion on any ground (race, gender, age, language, religion, disability, economic or social condition, etc.). Access requires open spaces — museums, cultural and arts institutions, performance venues, libraries, heritage and memorial sites — and quality formal, non-formal and informal education for vocations and professional careers in culture and the arts. All learners, educators and teachers should have equitable access to infrastructure, resources and opportunities to develop digital and AI competencies, including media and information literacy, while the digital divide and imbalances in online cultural diversity must be addressed. Education must be democratized, "support the struggle against colonialism and neo-colonialism in all their forms and manifestations," be free of stereotypes and bias, and counter hierarchies between cultures, cultural practices, arts disciplines or expressions. Special attention is due to Indigenous Peoples, persons with disabilities, and learners in disadvantaged, impoverished and vulnerable situations — refugees, migrants, displaced persons, victims of armed conflict, and learners in crisis-affected and post-disaster contexts. Education should use diverse, inclusive, flexible inter-, multi- and transdisciplinary approaches to combat stigma, xenophobia, hate speech, disinformation and discrimination, and to enhance appreciation of cultural diversity, human rights, intercultural understanding, social cohesion, conflict prevention, and post-conflict reconciliation and healing.

**b) Contextual, quality, lifelong and lifewide learning (p. 6).** Education should foster global citizenship, appreciation of the environment and cultural diversity — including Indigenous knowledge, languages and practices, ancestral and contemporary. This entails mainstreaming culture and the arts in formal education from Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE) onward, through locally relevant curricula, pedagogies and settings that engage local living cultural heritage, Indigenous ways of knowing, being and doing, mother tongue languages, and local cultural and creative expressions. In non-formal contexts, it sustains intergenerational transmission, peer-to-peer learning, apprenticeship, professional skills development and community learning. Culture and the arts should be integrated by engaging cultural institutions and spaces, living heritage bearers, local community mediators and other cultural professionals to anchor **place-based education** connecting learners to their communities and environments, addressing education gaps and fostering integration of disadvantaged learners.

**c) Appreciation of cultural diversity and the capacity for critical engagement (p. 6).** Culture, in its rich diversity, should be integrated across formal, non-formal and informal education, enabling inclusion of a plurality of identities and cultural expressions. Culture is dynamic, heterogeneous and constantly evolving, providing a critical space for interaction, exchange, contestation, co-learning and creation. Pedagogies should equip learners to use art for social change, safeguard cultural assets through place-based approaches, and identify and challenge instrumentalization of culture, harmful social norms, bias and stereotypes — while fostering freedom of expression and creation, democratic participation, social responsibility, creativity, innovation, cohesion and collaboration.

**d) Skills to shape resilient, just and sustainable futures (pp. 6–7).** Educational environments should leverage culture and the arts to nurture creativity, critical thinking and innovation for complex sustainability challenges, support specialized skills, talent and personal development, and bolster employment and decent work in the cultural and creative industries and the creative economy. From ECCE onward, education systems should harness culture and arts education to strengthen civic engagement and democratic participation, improve other subject learning (e.g., through a **STEAM** approach — Science, Technology, Engineering, the Arts, and Mathematics), reinforce writing, reading and speaking skills, and nurture social and emotional skills from empathy and solidarity to pluralistic thinking — enhancing academic and job performance and resilience for future uncertainty and crises.

**e) Institutionalization and valorization of culture and arts education ecosystems (p. 7).** Culture and arts education should be brought forward within education systems and recognized as integral to quality education: a prominent space in curricula, necessary time and space in class schedules, and adequate, sustainable resources. The arts as a field of knowledge should be recognized through certification of cultural and artistic skills and competencies for cultural professionals and practitioners working as educators, and through greater recognition of the intrinsic social value of culture, the arts and the cultural and creative industries. Planning should overcome siloed approaches through participatory cooperation across ministries, agencies, educator training institutions and other stakeholders, including informal and non-formal learning spaces and actors.

### V. Implementation modalities (pp. 7–9)

**a) Governance, legislation and policies (p. 7).** Transform governance through sustained, inclusive and adaptive strategies fostering dialogue and collaboration across governance levels, policy areas and institutions. Establish and formalize regular cooperation between ministries — notably culture and education, but also health, social care, science, innovation, environment, economy, development and planning — to integrate culture and arts education across all settings, supported by accessible, research- and evidence-based information, long-term interventions, resources for all, and collaborative monitoring and evaluation. Advance professionalization of culture and arts ecosystems and recognition of cultural, artistic and creative skills by strengthening Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) and higher education policies, strategies and programmes, enabling young people and adults to access work, community service, internship and entrepreneurship opportunities in the cultural and creative industries.

**b) Learning environments (p. 7).** Broaden the concept of learning environments through sustained cooperation with formal, non-formal and informal spaces: specialized schools, museums, galleries, libraries, performing arts venues, creative hubs, cities and municipalities (including members of the UNESCO Creative Cities Network and UNESCO Global Network of Learning Cities), community centres, cultural and natural heritage sites, memorial sites and other arts and cultural institutions in urban and rural areas — via learning-by-doing, mentorships, internships, and public-private partnerships, as a whole-of-community endeavour.

**c) Learning experiences (pp. 7–8).** Integrate diverse local knowledge systems and tangible and intangible cultural resources (living heritage, cultural practices and expressions) across all education forms for rich, locally relevant, intercultural learning. Engage artists and other cultural professionals and practitioners, living heritage bearers, community organizations, learners and other stakeholders as critical actors in education, information gathering, analysis and dissemination, research, and curriculum development and review.

**d) Teachers and educators (p. 8).** Diversify the teaching profession to embody society's cultural diversity by including living heritage bearers, artists and other cultural professionals and practitioners. Revise teacher education and professional development to include culture and arts education, grounded in research, with quality, relevant, user-friendly guidelines, pedagogies and materials; promote dialogue and cooperation between education personnel and cultural professionals. Increase investment to address shortages of qualified teachers, trainers and non-formal educators, especially in rural areas and small communities, and provide quality teacher education and continuous professional development harnessing innovative pedagogies and digital technologies. Improve the status and working conditions of teachers, facilitators, trainers, educators, artists and cultural professionals working in culture and arts education — including employment, remuneration, flexible working modalities, professional growth and well-being — and streamline school administration to give educators time and flexibility for initiatives and partnerships with cultural institutions.

**e) Digital technologies and AI (pp. 8–9).** Leverage media and information literacy for culture and arts education in the digital era, promoting creative, emancipated, ethical and responsible use of digital technologies while protecting privacy, intellectual property rights and cultural and linguistic diversity online. Broaden access to digital technologies through well-developed infrastructure and maintenance, digitalized cultural content, digital collections, and training and skills development — particularly for least developed countries, persons with disabilities, and those in vulnerable and disadvantaged situations. Promote research and innovation for diverse, sustainable, ethical, safe and bias-free digital tools, including for artistic creation and knowledge sharing. Harness human capacities for co-creation, use and delivery of digital content enriched by local and cultural resources, engaging educators and learners as active participants and content creators, including through open licenses. Expand collaboration on evolving technologies and AI — including Generative AI and cyber security — assessing their impact on the value chain of the cultural and creative industries, ensuring balanced regulation of digital tools, and building consensus on future strategies through dialogue among diverse disciplines and expertise.

**f) Partnerships, interinstitutional coordination (p. 9).** Innovate and expand partnerships and coordination mechanisms at international, regional, national and local levels among governmental bodies, cultural institutions, schools, memorial and heritage sites, local communities, civil society, the private sector, media, learners, educators, artists and other cultural professionals — to incentivize long-term collaboration, knowledge sharing, mobility for artists and other cultural professionals, residency programmes, and co-creation.

**g) Financing (p. 9).** Mobilize financing, including public-private partnerships, for collaborative infrastructures and mechanisms and the administrative, human and material resources needed for long-term sustainability of the culture and arts education ecosystem. Develop financial and in-kind mechanisms for designing and implementing revised policies, including revision of curricula, pedagogies and frameworks.

**h) Research, data and assessment (p. 9).** Strengthen existing or establish new long-term mechanisms for systematic, comprehensive, collaborative, interdisciplinary and transdisciplinary research, and robust data collection, analysis and monitoring on culture and arts education topics, to support evidence-based policy-making, implementation, evaluation, dissemination and advocacy.

### VI. Monitoring, follow-up and review (pp. 9–10)

Member States and Associate Members should:

i. Support implementation by sharing progress, good practices and challenges; harnessing existing reporting mechanisms (the Global Report on Cultural Policies, the Global Education Monitoring Report, and those pertaining to SDG Target 4.7), they should submit to UNESCO, on a voluntary basis, a progress report **every four years** on national-level implementation, **starting from 2025**.
ii. Explore the feasibility of establishing **Category 2 Centres** at regional or global level under UNESCO's aegis, within the scope of culture and arts education, to strengthen research and analysis on the culture–education nexus, including training, data collection and analysis, and monitoring and evaluation. Establishment is subject to UNESCO's procedures, including a required feasibility study and sustainable resources from the proposing Member State(s).

UNESCO shall, within existing resources: (i) develop a specific global monitoring mechanism for the Framework, harnessing existing mechanisms at global, regional and national levels; (ii) facilitate regular knowledge-sharing centred on good practice case studies in Member States; (iii) engage UNESCO's networks across the Education and Culture Sectors to advance research and evidence-based analyses, stepping up cooperation with regional and international networks, and encouraging Member States to mobilize national networks including UNESCO Chairs and UNESCO Associated Schools; (iv) build and expand partnerships with public and private stakeholders to advance the strategic goals; (v) collect and disseminate relevant research, progress reports, data and diverse practices; and (vi) engage in an extensive reflection process to deepen synergies between relevant Conventions, Recommendations, programmes and actions of the UNESCO Culture and Education Sectors.

## GLOSSAR

- **Culture and Arts Education** — teaching and learning about, with and through the arts and culture, encompassing all forms of cultural and artistic expressions; positions culture, including the arts, as an educational tool, an approach, and a field of study, research and practice.
- **Culture** — the "set of distinctive spiritual, material and intellectual and emotional features that characterize a society or social group," including arts and letters, modes of life, fundamental rights, value systems, traditions and beliefs (MONDIACULT 1982/2022 definition).
- **Education** — "an inalienable human right," a lifelong and society-wide process through which everyone learns and develops to their fullest potential — personality, dignity, talents, mental and physical abilities — for the benefit of communities and ecosystems (2023 UNESCO Recommendation definition).
- **Ecosystem (culture and arts education as)** — the Framework's conception of culture and arts education as covering educational activities for all people, in all contexts and settings, pedagogies, modalities, levels and types.
- **Lifelong and lifewide learning** — learning across the whole lifespan and across diverse settings — physical (classrooms, community spaces, workplaces, cultural and natural spaces), virtual (digital platforms) and blended.
- **Common good of humanity** — the status the Framework assigns to culture and arts education — accessible to all, fostering well-being of individuals and societies, requiring collective engagement and sustained public investment.
- **Place-based education** — education anchored in learners' local communities and environments through engagement with cultural institutions, living heritage bearers, community mediators and cultural practitioners.
- **STEAM** — Science, Technology, Engineering, the Arts, and Mathematics — an approach harnessing culture and arts education to improve subject learning, creativity and innovation.
- **Living heritage bearers** — practitioners who carry and transmit living cultural heritage, included among the actors who should diversify the teaching profession and enrich learning experiences.
- **Indigenous ways of knowing, being and doing** — knowledge systems and practices of Indigenous Peoples to be engaged in curricula, pedagogies and locally relevant learning.
- **Media and information literacy** — the competency leveraged to support effective provision of culture and arts education in the digital era and to equip learners and educators against disinformation.
- **Category 2 Centres** — proposed regional or global centres under UNESCO's aegis to strengthen research, training, data collection and monitoring on the culture–education nexus, subject to feasibility study and sustainable resources.
- **Intercultural dialogue** — a process value underpinning the Framework's broad understanding of culture, aimed at cooperation and understanding across cultures.
- **Cultural and creative industries** — the economic sector whose employment, decent work and entrepreneurship opportunities culture and arts education should support, sustaining the creative economy at national and local levels.
- **TVET** — Technical and Vocational Education and Training, to be strengthened in culture and the arts to enable young people and adults to acquire knowledge and skills for work in the cultural and creative industries.
