# Basic Texts of the 2003 Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage (2024 Edition)

## PROFIL

Issued by UNESCO, this 2024 edition compiles the basic texts of the 2003 Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage, addressed to its States Parties (183, near-universal ratification) and to all actors involved in implementing the instrument more than two decades after its adoption. The Convention defines intangible cultural heritage as the practices, representations, expressions, knowledge and skills that communities recognize as their heritage, manifested in oral traditions, performing arts, social practices, knowledge concerning nature and the universe, and traditional craftsmanship, and compatible with human rights, mutual respect and sustainable development. The volume contains the Convention itself (purposes, definitions, organs — the General Assembly and the Intergovernmental Committee —, safeguarding at national and international level, international assistance, the Intangible Cultural Heritage Fund, reporting and final clauses), followed by the Operational Directives in chapters on international-level safeguarding and assistance (listing criteria, evaluation, timetables, transfers and removals), the Fund, participation of communities, experts and NGOs, awareness-raising and use of the emblem, and reporting to the Committee. The foreword records two changes adopted at the June 2024 General Assembly after the Global reflection on the listing mechanisms (2018–2022): simplified selection criteria for the Register of Good Safeguarding Practices and enlarged access to preparatory assistance for States with no element inscribed on the Representative List. The Convention builds on the Universal Declaration on Human Rights, the two International Covenants of 1966, the 1989 Recommendation on Traditional Culture and Folklore, the 2001 Universal Declaration on Cultural Diversity and the 2002 Istanbul Declaration, and is set apart from the 1972 World Heritage Convention, whose protection it leaves unaffected. It is usable in arts education wherever intangible heritage, transmission through formal and non-formal education, community participation and safeguarding practice are at issue.

## WISSEN

### Basic Texts of the 2003 Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage (2024 Edition) (p. 1–67)

UNESCO's foreword situates the Convention more than two decades after its 2003 adoption. It describes intangible cultural heritage as "traditions and living expressions inherited from our ancestors and passed on to our descendants" that helps people understand "who we are, where we come from, and where we need to go." The Convention now has 183 States Parties (near-universal ratification) and has shifted understanding of heritage beyond monuments and natural sites (Galapagos Islands, Versailles, Taj Mahal) to oral traditions, celebrations and cultural practices. Two major changes to the Operational Directives followed the Global reflection on the listing mechanisms (2018–2022) and the June 2024 General Assembly: (1) simplified selection criteria for the Register of Good Safeguarding Practices to redress its underutilization; (2) enlarged access to International Assistance, so preparatory assistance can now benefit States with no element inscribed on the Representative List (excluding elements proposed via multinational nominations) who wish to submit a nomination. Special attention is urged for Indigenous Peoples and marginalized and minority communities.

### Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage — Preamble and General Provisions (p. 3–6)

Adopted in Paris on 17 October 2003 by the UNESCO General Conference (32nd session), the Convention's preamble refers to the Universal Declaration on Human Rights (1948) and the two International Covenants of 1966; considers intangible cultural heritage a mainspring of cultural diversity and a guarantee of sustainable development (echoing the 1989 Recommendation on Traditional Culture and Folklore, the 2001 Universal Declaration on Cultural Diversity, and the 2002 Istanbul Declaration); notes the interdependence between intangible and tangible heritage; recognizes that globalization and social transformation, alongside intolerance, create grave threats of deterioration and disappearance; and stresses the role of communities, particularly indigenous communities, in producing, safeguarding, maintaining and recreating such heritage. It notes that no binding multilateral instrument yet existed for this heritage and recalls the Proclamation of Masterpieces of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity.

**Article 1 — Purposes:** (a) to safeguard the intangible cultural heritage; (b) to ensure respect for it among the communities, groups and individuals concerned; (c) to raise awareness at local, national and international levels of its importance and ensure mutual appreciation; (d) to provide for international cooperation and assistance.

**Article 2 — Definitions:**
1. 'Intangible Cultural Heritage' (ICH) means the practices, representations, expressions, knowledge, skills — and associated instruments, objects, artefacts and cultural spaces — that communities, groups and, in some cases, individuals recognize as part of their cultural heritage; transmitted from generation to generation, constantly recreated in response to environment, interaction with nature and history, providing a sense of identity and continuity. Only ICH compatible with existing international human rights instruments, mutual respect among communities, groups and individuals, and sustainable development is considered.
2. ICH is manifested inter alia in five domains: (a) oral traditions and expressions, including language as a vehicle of ICH; (b) performing arts; (c) social practices, rituals and festive events; (d) knowledge and practices concerning nature and the universe; (e) traditional craftsmanship.
3. 'Safeguarding' means measures aimed at ensuring viability: identification, documentation, research, preservation, protection, promotion, enhancement, transmission (particularly through formal and non-formal education), and revitalization.
4. 'States Parties' are States bound by the Convention.
5. The Convention applies mutatis mutandis to territories referred to in Article 33.

**Article 3:** Nothing in the Convention alters the protection of World Heritage properties under the 1972 Convention, or affects rights and obligations under instruments on intellectual property or biological and ecological resources.

### Organs of the Convention (p. 7–9)

**Article 4:** A General Assembly of States Parties is the sovereign body, meeting in ordinary session every two years (extraordinary sessions possible on decision, Committee request, or request of at least one-third of States Parties); it adopts its own Rules of Procedure.

**Article 5:** An Intergovernmental Committee (the "Committee") within UNESCO comprises representatives of 18 States Parties elected by the General Assembly; the number rises to 24 once the Convention reaches 50 States Parties.

**Article 6:** Elections obey equitable geographical representation and rotation; Members serve four-year terms; half of the first-elected Members serve only two years (chosen by lot); half the Committee is renewed every two years; vacancies are filled; a Member may not be elected for two consecutive terms; representatives must be qualified in the various ICH fields.

**Article 7 — Committee functions:** (a) promote the Convention's objectives and monitor implementation; (b) provide guidance on best practices and recommendations; (c) prepare a draft plan for use of the Fund's resources; (d) seek means of increasing resources; (e) prepare and submit Operational Directives for General Assembly approval; (f) examine States Parties' reports and summarize them; (g) examine requests against objective selection criteria for (i) inscription on the lists (Articles 16, 17, 18) and (ii) granting international assistance (Article 22).

**Article 8:** The Committee is answerable to the General Assembly; adopts its Rules of Procedure by two-thirds majority; may establish temporary ad hoc consultative bodies; may invite public or private bodies and private persons with recognized competence to consult on specific matters.

**Article 9:** The Committee proposes to the General Assembly the accreditation of NGOs with recognized competence in an advisory capacity, and the criteria and modalities for such accreditation.

**Article 10:** The UNESCO Secretariat assists the Committee, prepares documentation and draft agendas, and ensures implementation of decisions.

### Safeguarding at the National Level (p. 10–12)

**Article 11:** Each State Party shall (a) take necessary measures to safeguard ICH present in its territory; (b) identify and define the various elements of ICH with the participation of communities, groups and relevant NGOs.

**Article 12:** Each State Party shall draw up, in a manner geared to its own situation, one or more inventories of the ICH present in its territory, regularly updated, and provide relevant information on these inventories in its periodic reports.

**Article 13:** States shall endeavour to: (a) adopt a general policy promoting ICH in society and integrate safeguarding into planning programmes; (b) designate or establish competent safeguarding bodies; (c) foster scientific, technical and artistic studies and research methodologies, especially for ICH in danger; (d) adopt appropriate legal, technical, administrative and financial measures to (i) foster training institutions and transmission forums, (ii) ensure access to ICH while respecting customary practices governing access, (iii) establish documentation institutions and facilitate access to them.

**Article 14 — Education, awareness-raising and capacity-building:** States shall endeavour to (a) ensure recognition, respect and enhancement of ICH through (i) educational, awareness-raising and information programmes for the general public, especially young people; (ii) specific educational and training programmes within the communities concerned; (iii) capacity-building activities, particularly management and scientific research; (iv) non-formal means of transmitting knowledge; (b) keep the public informed of threats to the heritage and Convention activities; (c) promote education for the protection of natural spaces and places of memory necessary for expressing ICH.

**Article 15:** States shall endeavour to ensure the widest possible participation of communities, groups and, where appropriate, individuals that create, maintain and transmit the heritage, and involve them actively in its management.

### Safeguarding at the International Level (p. 12–13)

**Article 16:** The Committee shall establish, keep up to date and publish a Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, upon proposals of States Parties, to ensure visibility and awareness of significance and encourage dialogue respecting cultural diversity; criteria are submitted to the General Assembly for approval.

**Article 17:** The Committee shall likewise establish and publish a List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding, inscribing heritage at the request of the State Party concerned. In cases of extreme urgency (objective criteria approved by the General Assembly), the Committee may inscribe an item in consultation with the State Party concerned.

**Article 18:** On the basis of proposals by States Parties, and criteria defined by the Committee and approved by the General Assembly, the Committee shall periodically select and promote national, subregional and regional programmes, projects and activities best reflecting the Convention's principles and objectives, taking into account the special needs of developing countries; it receives and approves requests for international assistance for preparing such proposals and accompanies implementation by disseminating best practices.

### International Cooperation and Assistance (p. 13–15)

**Article 19:** International cooperation includes exchange of information and experience, joint initiatives, and establishment of an assistance mechanism; States Parties recognize that safeguarding ICH is of general interest to humanity and undertake to cooperate at bilateral, subregional, regional and international levels.

**Article 20 — Purposes of international assistance:** (a) safeguarding of heritage inscribed on the Urgent Safeguarding List; (b) preparation of inventories (Articles 11 and 12); (c) support for national, subregional and regional safeguarding programmes, projects and activities; (d) any other purpose the Committee deems necessary.

**Article 21 — Forms of assistance:** (a) studies; (b) provision of experts and practitioners; (c) training of staff; (d) elaboration of standard-setting and other measures; (e) creation and operation of infrastructures; (f) supply of equipment and know-how; (g) other financial and technical assistance, including low-interest loans and donations.

**Article 22:** The Committee establishes the examination procedure and required information (measures envisaged, interventions required, cost assessment); emergency requests are examined as a matter of priority; the Committee may undertake studies and consultations as necessary.

**Article 23:** Each State Party may submit a request; two or more States Parties may submit jointly; the request includes the Article 22.1 information plus documentation.

**Article 24:** Assistance is regulated by an agreement between the beneficiary State and the Committee; as a general rule the beneficiary State shares the cost within the limits of its resources; it must report on the use made of the assistance.

### The Intangible Cultural Heritage Fund (p. 15–18)

**Article 25:** A 'Fund for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage' is established, consisting of funds-in-trust under UNESCO's Financial Regulations. Resources comprise: (a) contributions by States Parties; (b) UNESCO General Conference appropriations; (c) contributions, gifts or bequests from other States, UN-system organizations and programmes (particularly UNDP), other international organizations, and public or private bodies or individuals; (d) interest; (e) funds raised through collections and events; (f) other authorized resources. Use of resources follows General Assembly guidelines; the Committee may accept contributions for general and specific approved projects; no political, economic or other incompatible conditions may be attached to contributions.

**Article 26:** States Parties undertake to pay into the Fund, at least every two years, a contribution in the form of a uniform percentage applicable to all States, determined by the General Assembly; no State's contribution may exceed 1% of its contribution to UNESCO's regular budget. A State may declare, at deposit of its instruments, that it is not bound by this provision; such a State should endeavour to withdraw the declaration and, meanwhile, pay voluntary contributions as close as possible to what it would have owed. A State in arrears for the current year and the preceding calendar year is ineligible for Committee membership (not applying to the first election), and the term of any such sitting Member ends at the next elections.

**Article 27:** States wishing to make voluntary supplementary contributions shall inform the Committee promptly.

**Article 28:** States shall, insofar as possible, support international fund-raising campaigns for the Fund under UNESCO's auspices.

### Reports, Transitional Clause and Final Clauses (p. 18–22)

**Article 29:** States Parties submit to the Committee reports on legislative, regulatory and other measures for implementation, in forms and periodicity defined by the Committee.

**Article 30:** The Committee reports to the General Assembly at each session; the report is brought to the attention of the UNESCO General Conference.

**Article 31:** The Committee incorporates into the Representative List the items proclaimed 'Masterpieces of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity' before the Convention's entry into force; this incorporation does not prejudge future inscription criteria; no further Proclamation will be made.

**Articles 32–40:** The Convention is subject to ratification, acceptance or approval by UNESCO Members (instruments deposited with the Director-General) and open to accession by non-Member States invited by the General Conference and by territories with full internal self-government recognized by the UN (General Assembly resolution 1514 (XV)) that have competence over the relevant matters (Article 33). It enters into force three months after the deposit of the thirtieth instrument, and three months after deposit for each subsequent State (Article 34). For federal or non-unitary systems, obligations of the central government match those of non-federal States for centrally governed matters; for matters under constituent-unit jurisdiction, the federal government informs the competent authorities with a recommendation for adoption (Article 35). Denunciation takes effect twelve months after receipt of the instrument and does not affect financial obligations until that date (Article 36). The Director-General is Depositary, informing UNESCO Members, non-Members concerned and the UN of deposits and denunciations (Article 37). Amendments may be proposed by a State Party; if at least one half of States Parties reply favourably within six months, the proposal goes to the General Assembly; adoption requires a two-thirds majority of States present and voting; amendments enter into force three months after deposit of instruments by two-thirds of States Parties (except amendments to Article 5 on Committee size, which enter into force upon adoption) (Article 38). The Convention is drawn up in Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian and Spanish, all equally authoritative (Article 39), and is registered with the UN Secretariat (Article 40).

### Operational Directives — Chapter I: Safeguarding at the International Level, Cooperation and International Assistance (p. 25–52)

The Operational Directives were adopted by the General Assembly at its second session (Paris, 16–19 June 2008) and amended at its third through tenth sessions (2010, 2012, 2014, 2016, 2018, 2020, 2022, and 11–12 June 2024).

**I.1 Criteria for the Urgent Safeguarding List (para. 1, p. 30):** An element must satisfy all of:
- U.1 The element constitutes ICH as defined in Article 2.
- U.2 (a) The element is in urgent need of safeguarding because its viability is at risk despite efforts; or (b) it is in extremely urgent need because grave threats mean it cannot be expected to survive without immediate safeguarding.
- U.3 A safeguarding plan is elaborated enabling continued practice and transmission.
- U.4 The element was nominated following the widest possible participation of the community, group or individuals concerned and with their free, prior and informed consent.
- U.5 The element is included in an inventory (Articles 11 and 12).
- U.6 In cases of extreme urgency, the State(s) Party(ies) concerned has (have) been duly consulted (Article 17.3).

**I.2 Criteria for the Representative List (para. 2, p. 31):** All of:
- R.1 The element constitutes ICH as defined in Article 2.
- R.2 Inscription will contribute to ensuring visibility and awareness of the significance of ICH and to encouraging dialogue, reflecting cultural diversity worldwide and testifying to human creativity.
- R.3 Safeguarding measures are elaborated that may protect and promote the element.
- R.4 Nominated following the widest possible participation of the community, group or individuals concerned and with their free, prior and informed consent.
- R.5 The element is included in an inventory (Articles 11 and 12).

**I.3 Criteria for programmes, projects and activities (paras 3–7, p. 31–32):** States Parties are encouraged to propose national, subregional or regional programmes, projects and activities; the Committee may call for proposals characterized by international cooperation (Article 19) and/or focusing on specific priority aspects. Proposals may be completed or in progress. The Committee pays special attention to developing countries' needs and equitable geographic distribution, strengthening South-South and North-South-South cooperation. Selection requires all of:
- G.1 The programme involves safeguarding as defined in Article 2.3, reflecting the Convention's principles and objectives.
- G.2 It has demonstrated effectiveness in contributing to the viability of the ICH concerned.
- G.3 It is or has been implemented with the participation of the community, group or individuals concerned and with their free, prior, sustained and informed consent at the time of submission.
- G.4 The submitting State(s), implementing body(ies) and community concerned are willing to coordinate and cooperate in disseminating good practices if selected, and may serve as a source of inspiration at local, subregional, regional or international levels.

**I.4 Eligibility and selection criteria of International Assistance (paras 8–12, p. 32–34):** All States Parties are eligible; assistance is supplementary to national efforts. Priority is given to: (a) safeguarding of heritage inscribed on the Urgent Safeguarding List; (b) preparation of inventories; (c) support for national, subregional and regional safeguarding programmes; (d) preparatory assistance. The Committee considers equitable geographical distribution, developing countries' special needs, whether the request implies bilateral/regional/international cooperation, and whether assistance may have a multiplier effect. Emergency assistance is possible (Article 22). Decisions are based on all of:
- A.1 The community, group and/or individuals concerned participated in preparing the request and will be involved in implementation, evaluation and follow-up as broadly as possible.
- A.2 The amount requested is appropriate.
- A.3 The proposed activities are well conceived and feasible.
- A.4 The project may have lasting results.
- A.5 The beneficiary State Party shares the cost within the limits of its resources.
- A.6 The assistance aims at building up or reinforcing capacities in safeguarding ICH.
- A.7 The beneficiary State Party has implemented previously financed activities, if any, in line with all regulations and conditions.

**I.5 Multi-national files (paras 13–15, p. 34):** States are encouraged to jointly submit multi-national nominations when an element is found on the territory of more than one State Party; subregional or regional proposals and proposals from geographically discontinuous areas are encouraged; International Assistance requests may be submitted jointly by two or more States Parties.

**I.6 Inscription on an extended or reduced basis (paras 16–19, p. 34–36):** Inscription can be extended to other communities, groups and individuals at national and/or international level upon request of the State(s) Party(ies) concerned (16.1); States should announce intentions to join existing inscribed elements through the Convention webpage's dedicated online form (16.2). At the international level, newly joining States must demonstrate that the extension satisfies all required criteria; communities that consented to the original nomination must agree with the extension and their participation in safeguarding measures (16.3). At the national level, the State must demonstrate the extension satisfies the criteria, taking into account criteria already satisfied (16.4). Inscription can be reduced at the request of the State(s) concerned (17.1), with evidence of free, prior and informed consent of the communities, groups or individuals proposed for removal (17.2). The State(s) submit(s) a new nomination demonstrating that the element as extended or reduced satisfies all criteria (18). If inscribed, the new inscription replaces the original; if not inscribed, the original remains intact (19).

**I.7 Submission of files (paras 20–25, p. 36–37):** Form ICH-01 is used for Urgent Safeguarding List nominations (with an option for simultaneously requesting International Assistance) and for extended/reduced nominations to that List; Form ICH-02 for Representative List nominations (also for extensions/reductions); Form ICH-03 for proposals of programmes, projects and activities (20.1). Form ICH-01 RL to USL is used for transfer from the Representative List to the Urgent Safeguarding List (with option for International Assistance); Form ICH-02 USL to RL, attached to periodic reporting Form ICH-11, for transfer in the other direction (20.2). Preparatory assistance may be requested for: (a) Urgent Safeguarding List nomination files; (b) Representative List nomination files (only for States Parties with no elements already inscribed on that List, excepting elements proposed via multinational nominations); (c) proposals of programmes, projects and activities; (d) transfer requests; (e) extended/reduced nominations (21). Preparatory assistance requests use Form ICH-05; International Assistance requests use Form ICH-04, except those submitted simultaneously with Urgent Safeguarding List nominations or RL-to-USL transfer requests (22). Forms are available at https://ich.unesco.org; files shall include only the information requested (23). Submitting States shall involve the communities concerned in preparation (24). A State may withdraw a file at any time prior to Committee examination, without prejudice to its right to benefit from International Assistance (25).

**I.8 Evaluation of files (paras 26–31, p. 37–39):** Evaluation assesses conformity with the required criteria. Nominations to both Lists, proposals of programmes/projects/activities, and related International Assistance requests are evaluated by the 'Evaluation Body', a consultative body established under Article 8.3, composed of twelve members appointed by the Committee: six experts qualified in the various ICH fields, representatives of States Parties non-Members of the Committee, and six accredited NGOs, with equitable geographical representation and domain coverage (27). A member's term shall not exceed four years; one quarter is renewed annually; up to three candidatures per vacant Electoral Group seat are sent by the Group's Chairperson at least six weeks before the Committee session; members act impartially (28). For the Urgent Safeguarding List, each evaluation includes assessment of the element's viability, the feasibility and sufficiency of the safeguarding plan, and the risk of disappearance due to lack of means, globalization, or social or environmental transformation (29). The Evaluation Body submits a report recommending: to inscribe or not, or refer for additional information (including transfers, extensions, reductions); to select or not, or refer proposals; to approve or not, or refer International Assistance requests submitted with USL nominations or RL-to-USL transfers; or to maintain or remove an inscribed element in cases of 'enhanced follow-up' (30). The Secretariat transmits an overview of all files with summaries and evaluation reports to the Committee and makes them available to States Parties (31).

**I.9 Extremely urgent nominations (para. 32, p. 40):** In cases of extreme urgency (criterion U.6), the Bureau of the Committee may invite the State(s) concerned to submit a nomination on an accelerated schedule; the Committee examines it as quickly as possible under a case-by-case procedure established by the Bureau. Extreme urgency may be brought to the Bureau's attention by the State(s) on whose territory the element is located, any other State Party, the community concerned, or an advisory organization; the State(s) concerned shall be informed in a timely manner.

**I.10 Examination of files by the Committee (paras 33–37, p. 40–42):** The Committee determines two years beforehand the number of files treatable over the two following cycles, in total no more than sixty, covering nominations to both Lists and proposals of programmes, projects and activities (33). The Committee endeavours to examine at least one file per submitting State within this ceiling, giving priority to: (0) files from States which had no file treated during the preceding cycle; (i) files from States having no elements inscribed, no best safeguarding practices selected, and no Urgent Safeguarding List nominations; (ii) multi-national files; (iii) files from States with the fewest elements inscribed and practices selected compared with other submitting States in the same cycle. States submitting several files shall indicate their order of priority and are invited to give priority to the Urgent Safeguarding List (34). After examination, the Committee decides whether to inscribe, not inscribe, or refer for additional information; whether to select a proposal as a best safeguarding practice, not select, or refer; and whether to approve, not approve, or refer International Assistance requests submitted with USL nominations or RL-to-USL transfers (35). Files not inscribed, selected or approved, or referred, may be resubmitted in a following cycle after updating and supplementation (36). A decision to refer does not imply or guarantee future inscription, selection or approval; any resubmission must fully demonstrate that the criteria are satisfied (37).

**I.11 Transfer or removal of an element (paras 38–40, p. 42–46):** An element may not simultaneously be inscribed on both Lists; a State Party may request transfer, initiated with the free, prior and informed consent of the communities concerned and submitted per established procedures and deadlines (38.1). Communities, groups and individuals may express directly to the Secretariat their wish for a transfer; the request is transmitted to the State(s) concerned and the Committee is informed (38.2). Transfer from the Representative List to the Urgent Safeguarding List (Form ICH-01 RL to USL) requires: (a) for U.1, an updated description including justification of urgent need; (b) for U.3, an adequate safeguarding plan; (c) for U.4, consent from the communities that had agreed to the original inscription (39.1). Transfer from the Urgent Safeguarding List to the Representative List (Form ICH-02 USL to RL) requires: (a) for R.1, an updated description addressing changes in viability with reference to the original criterion U.2; (b) for R.2, demonstration of contribution to mutual respect and dialogue and to sustainable development; (c) for R.3, assessment of the safeguarding plan's implementation through periodic reporting and future measures; (d) for R.4, consent from the communities that had agreed to the original inscription (39.2). The Evaluation Body may also recommend including the successful safeguarding experience in the Register of Good Safeguarding Practices (39.3). An element shall be removed from a List when the Committee determines it no longer satisfies the required criteria, paying particular attention to U.1/R.1 and U.4/R.4; removal can be requested by the State Party, communities, groups, individuals, or any third party (40.1). The procedure (40.2): (a) the removal request is registered by the Secretariat; (b) it is transmitted to the State Party, the nomination contact person and community representatives, who may respond with complementary information; (c) if the submitting entity other than States wishes to remain anonymous, an edited version is transmitted; (d) if the request comes from the State Party concerned: (i) the Secretariat gathers information, in particular in relation to Article 2, and transmits the request directly to the Committee with any response and information gathered; (ii) the Committee may then place the element under 'enhanced follow-up' status as an interim measure if more information is needed, or remove the element from the List if the information is complete and there is enough ground, with the possibility of placing it in an Intangible Cultural Heritage Repository (end of procedure); (e) in other cases: (i) the Secretariat may gather information and share results with the State Party and gather its response; the request is transmitted to the Bureau, which recommends whether to include the case on the agenda of the next Committee session; (ii) the Committee may maintain the element on the List if there is insufficient ground for removal (end of procedure), or place it under 'enhanced follow-up' status if additional information is needed. Under 'enhanced follow-up' (40.3): (a) the Evaluation Body evaluates the element, paying particular attention to Article 2, on the basis of additional information gathered through exchange and dialogue, and transmits its report and recommendation; (b) the Committee may then decide to (i) continue 'follow-up' for a determined period if issues persist, recommending reconciliatory/mediatory measures and specifying a session at which the State Party reports back for a final decision; (ii) remove the element from the List if there is enough ground, with the possibility of placing it in an Intangible Cultural Heritage Repository (end of procedure); (iii) maintain the element on the List if there is insufficient ground (end of procedure).

**I.12 Modification of name (para. 41, p. 46):** One or more States Parties may request a change of an inscribed element's name; such requests shall be submitted at least three months prior to a Committee session.

**I.13 Selected programmes, projects and activities (paras 42–46, p. 46):** The Committee shall encourage research, documentation, publication and dissemination of good practices and models with international cooperation; encourage States to create favourable conditions for implementation; compile and make available information about measures, methodologies and experiences in addition to the register of selected programmes; encourage research on and evaluation of the effectiveness of safeguarding measures and promote international cooperation in such research; and, on the basis of experiences gained, provide guidance on best safeguarding practices and make recommendations (Article 7(b)).

**I.14 International Assistance (paras 47–53, p. 47–48):** Requests (including preparatory assistance) shall not exceed US$100,000, except emergency requests and requests submitted simultaneously with a USL nomination or an RL-to-USL transfer request. Requests can be submitted at any time, except those examined and approved by the Committee (timetable under I.15 applies); preparatory assistance requests are due by 31 March (47). The Secretariat assesses completeness and may request missing information, informing the requesting State of possible examination dates (48). Requests up to US$100,000 and emergency requests regardless of amount are examined and approved by the Bureau of the Committee (49). An emergency exists when a State Party finds itself unable to overcome on its own any circumstance due to calamity, natural disaster, armed conflict, serious epidemic or any other natural or human event with severe consequences for the ICH and its bearers (50). Requests submitted simultaneously with USL nominations or RL-to-USL transfer requests are evaluated by the Evaluation Body and examined and approved by the Committee (51). The Secretariat communicates the decision within two weeks and reaches agreement on the details of assistance (52). Assistance is subject to appropriate monitoring, reporting and evaluation (53).

**I.15 Timetable (paras 54–56, p. 48–50):** Phase 1 (Preparation and submission): 31 March Year 0 — deadline for preparatory assistance requests; December Year 0 — deadline for USL-to-RL transfer requests; 31 March Year 1 — deadline for nominations to both Lists (including those with simultaneous International Assistance requests) and proposals of programmes, projects and activities; files received later are examined in the next cycle; the Secretariat posts files online in their original language; 30 June Year 1 — deadline for the Secretariat to process files (registration, acknowledgement; incomplete files are flagged for completion); 30 September Year 1 — deadline for submitting missing information; files that remain incomplete are returned and may be completed for a subsequent cycle; revised files replace the originals online, with English or French translations posted as available; 31 January Year 2 — deadline for RL-to-USL transfer requests, which are transmitted to the Evaluation Body in the same year without completeness checking. Phase 2 (Evaluation), December Year 1 to May Year 2: individual evaluation by Evaluation Body members; June Year 2: meeting at which the Evaluation Body finalizes evaluations and decides which files enter the dialogue process (only those files' evaluations remain pending); the dialogue process is initiated when a short written question-and-answer process with the submitting State(s), through the Secretariat, could influence the evaluation result; two weeks after the June meeting: deadline for the Evaluation Body to transmit questions in one of the two working languages; States respond within four weeks in the two working languages; September Year 2, four weeks prior to the Committee session: the Evaluation Body finalizes evaluations of dialogue-process files and its report on all files; the Secretariat transmits evaluation reports to Committee Members and posts them online. Phase 3 (Examination), November Year 2: the Committee examines the nominations, proposals and requests and makes its decisions.

**I.16 Incorporation of Masterpieces (paras 57–65, p. 50–52):** In conformity with Article 31.1, the Committee automatically incorporates into the Representative List all items proclaimed 'Masterpieces of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity' before the Convention's entry into force (57). This is enforceable upon all States having such items on their territories, whether or not party to the Convention; States non party enjoy all the rights and assume all the obligations of the Convention as regards only those items, on condition of written consent, with rights and obligations inseparable (58). States non party are notified by the Director-General and invited to express, within one year, explicit written consent (59–60); written acceptance addressed to the Director-General as Depositary submits the items to the full legal regime of the Convention (61). If a State non party refuses within one year, the Committee may withdraw the items from the List (62); silence or lack of response within one year is considered refusal, unless circumstances beyond its control prevent notification (63). If an incorporated Masterpiece is found on the territories of both a State Party and a State non party, it benefits from the full legal regime; the State non party is invited to consent to the obligations, and absent explicit consent the Committee may recommend that it refrain from any act that might harm the item (64). The Committee reports to the General Assembly on measures undertaken (65).

### Operational Directives — Chapter II: The Intangible Cultural Heritage Fund (p. 52–55)

**II.1 Guidelines for use of resources (paras 66–67, p. 52–53):** The Fund, managed as a special account, is used primarily for granting International Assistance (Chapter V of the Convention). Resources may further be used: (a) for replenishment of the Reserve Fund; (b) for support of other Committee functions (Article 7), including proposals under Article 18; (c) for the costs of participation in Committee sessions of representatives of developing States Members of the Committee who are experts in ICH, and, if the budget allows, case-by-case, for experts from developing-country Parties not on the Committee; (d) for advisory services by non-governmental and non-profit-making organizations, public or private bodies and private persons at the Committee's request; (e) for the costs of participation of invited public or private bodies and private persons, notably community and group members, consulted on specific matters.

**II.2 Means to increase resources (paras 68–78, p. 53–55):** *Donors (68–71):* the Committee welcomes contributions from the UN and its specialized agencies and programmes (particularly UNDP), other international organizations, States Parties and other States (voluntary contributions), public and private bodies and individuals; it encourages national, public and private foundations or associations promoting the Convention's objectives and calls upon States Parties to support international fund-raising campaigns under UNESCO's auspices. *Conditions (72–75):* no incompatible political, economic or other conditions may be attached; no contributions may be accepted from entities whose activities are incompatible with the Convention's aims and principles, international human rights instruments, sustainable development, or mutual respect among communities; the Secretariat may put specific cases before the Committee. Voluntary contributions are governed by the Fund's financial regulations, the General Assembly's Guidelines and the Committee's Plans; donors have no direct influence on the use of contributions; no individual narrative or financial reporting is provided to the donor; agreements are reached by a single exchange of letters. *Benefits for donors (76–78):* the Secretariat annually informs the Committee of voluntary contributions; the Committee provides visibility if donors wish; contributions are made known on the Convention's website. Recognition: (a) supplementary voluntary contributions by States Parties — an updated alphabetical list published primarily on the website, printed every two years at the General Assembly session; (b) contributions by other States, the UN system and other international organizations and public bodies — an updated alphabetical list, same publication rhythm; (c) contributions by private bodies and individuals — an updated list in decreasing order of amount, same publication rhythm; during the 24 months following deposit, private contributors may promote their cooperation with the Committee in all media formats, with materials reviewed and approved in advance by the Secretariat and no explicit advertising of contributors' products or services. States Parties are encouraged to recognize private contributions through fiscal mechanisms such as tax benefits.

### Operational Directives — Chapter III: Participation in the Implementation of the Convention (p. 56–61)

**III.1 Communities, groups, individuals, experts, centres of expertise and research institutes (paras 79–89, p. 56–58):** Recalling Articles 11(b) and 15, the Committee encourages functional and complementary cooperation among communities, groups and individuals who create, maintain and transmit ICH, as well as experts, centres of expertise and research institutes (79). States are encouraged to create a consultative body or coordination mechanism facilitating participation in: (a) identification and definition of elements; (b) drawing up inventories; (c) elaboration and implementation of programmes, projects and activities; (d) preparation of nomination files; (e) removal of an element from one List or transfer to the other (paras 38–40) (80). States shall raise the awareness of communities regarding the importance and value of their ICH and of the Convention (81), and undertake capacity-building measures for communities (Articles 11–15) (82). States are encouraged to establish and regularly update a directory of experts, centres of expertise, research institutes and regional centres active in the Convention's domains (83). The Committee may involve such experts and centres to consult on specific matters (84). States shall facilitate access by communities to research results and foster respect for practices governing access to specific aspects of ICH (Article 13(d)) (85), and are encouraged to develop subregional and regional networks of communities and experts for joint and interdisciplinary approaches (86). States possessing documentation concerning an element present in another State Party's territory are encouraged to share it, with the other State making it available to the communities and experts concerned (87). States are encouraged to participate in regional cooperation, including activities of category 2 centres under UNESCO's auspices, in the spirit of Article 19 (88). Within available resources, the Committee may invite any public or private body and private persons with recognized competence (including communities, groups and other experts) to its meetings for interactive dialogue and consultation on specific matters (Article 8.4) (89).

**III.2 Non-governmental organizations (paras 90–99, p. 58–61):** *National level (90):* States shall involve relevant NGOs in implementing the Convention, inter alia in identifying and defining ICH and other safeguarding measures, in cooperation with other actors. *Criteria for accreditation (91):* NGOs shall (a) have proven competence, expertise and experience in safeguarding ICH in one or more specific domains; (b) have a local, national, regional or international nature, as appropriate; (c) have objectives in conformity with the spirit of the Convention and preferably conforming statutes or bylaws; (d) cooperate in a spirit of mutual respect with communities, groups and individuals that create, practise and transmit ICH; (e) possess operational capacities, including (i) a regular active membership forming a community linked by shared objectives, (ii) an established domicile and recognized legal personality compatible with domestic law, (iii) having existed and carried out appropriate activities for at least four years when considered for accreditation. *Modalities and review (92–95):* the Secretariat receives requests and submits recommendations; the Committee submits recommendations to the General Assembly for decision (Article 9), paying due attention to equitable geographical representation; accredited NGOs should abide by applicable domestic and international legal and ethical standards; the Committee reviews each advisory organization's contribution and commitment every four years following accreditation; termination may be decided at review, and relations may be suspended pending a termination decision. *Advisory functions (96):* accredited NGOs may be invited to provide evaluation reports as a reference for the Committee's examination of (a) Urgent Safeguarding List nomination files; (b) programmes, projects and activities (Article 18); (c) International Assistance requests; (d) effects of safeguarding plans for elements on the Urgent Safeguarding List. *Procedure (97–99):* an NGO requesting accreditation shall submit: (a) a description including full official name; (b) main objectives; (c) full address; (d) date of founding or approximate duration of existence; (e) countries of activity; (f) documentation of operational capacities (regular active membership; established domicile and legal personality; at least four years of existence and appropriate activities); (g) its activities in safeguarding ICH; (h) a description of its experiences in cooperating with communities, groups and practitioners. Requests use Form ICH-09 and shall include all and only the requested information; they must be received by 30 April of odd-numbered years for examination at the Committee's ordinary session that year. The Secretariat registers proposals and keeps the list of accredited NGOs up to date.

### Operational Directives — Chapter IV: Raising Awareness and Use of the Emblem (p. 61–67)

**IV.1.1 General provisions (paras 100–102, p. 61–62):** States shall endeavour to ensure respect for the ICH of communities, groups and individuals and raise awareness at local, national and international levels. When raising awareness about specific elements, all parties are encouraged to observe: (a) the heritage responds to the Article 2.1 definition; (b) the communities concerned have given free, prior and informed consent and their widest possible participation is ensured; (c) awareness-raising actions fully respect customary practices governing access, particularly secret and sacred aspects; (d) the communities concerned benefit from the actions. All parties should take care that actions will not: (a) de-contextualize or denaturalize the manifestations; (b) mark communities as not participating in contemporary life or harm their image; (c) contribute to justifying any form of political, social, ethnic, religious, linguistic or gender-based discrimination; (d) facilitate misappropriation or abuse of communities' knowledge and skills; (e) lead to over-commercialization or unsustainable tourism that may put the heritage at risk.

**IV.1.2 Local and national levels (paras 103–117, p. 62–67):** States are encouraged to develop and adopt codes of ethics based on the Convention and these Directives (103), and to ensure — through intellectual property rights, privacy rights and other legal protection — that the rights of communities, groups and individuals that create, bear and transmit their heritage are protected during awareness-raising or commercial activities (104). States shall keep the public informed about the importance of ICH and the dangers threatening it, and are encouraged to: (a) support media campaigns and broadcasting using all forms of media; (b) support symposiums, workshops, public forums, seminars, exhibitions, festivals, ICH days and contests; (c) support case studies and field surveys and disseminate the information; (d) promote policies for public recognition of bearers and practitioners; (e) promote and support community associations and information exchange among them; (f) develop policies recognizing the contribution of ICH manifestations to cultural diversity and wealth; (g) support local policies promoting ICH awareness (105). States shall support promotion and dissemination of the programmes, projects and activities selected by the Committee (Article 18) (106).

*Formal and non-formal education measures (107):* States shall ensure recognition, respect and enhancement of ICH through educational and information programmes, capacity-building and non-formal transmission (Article 14(a)), in particular by: (a) promoting ICH as an instrument of integration and intercultural dialogue and multilingual education including vernacular languages; (b) teaching about ICH in school curricula adapted to local specificities, developing books, CDs, videos, documentaries, manuals or brochures; (c) enhancing teachers' capacities and developing guides and manuals; (d) involving parents and parent associations in suggesting themes and modules; (e) involving practitioners and bearers in developing educational programmes and inviting them to explain their heritage in schools; (f) involving youth in collecting and disseminating information about their communities' ICH; (g) acknowledging the value of non-formal transmission of embedded knowledge and skills; (h) privileging practical experience through participatory methodologies, including games, home-tutoring and apprenticeships; (i) developing activities such as summer training, open-days, visits, photo and video contests, cultural heritage itineraries, or school trips to natural spaces and places of memory; (j) making full use, where appropriate, of information and communication technologies; (k) teaching about ICH in universities and fostering interdisciplinary scientific, technical and artistic studies and research methodologies; (l) providing vocational guidance to youth on the value of ICH for personal and career development; (m) training communities, groups or individuals in managing small businesses dealing with ICH.

*Community centres, museums, archives and similar entities (108–109):* Community centres and associations created and managed by communities themselves can play a vital role in supporting transmission and informing the public; they are encouraged to (a) serve as cultural spaces where ICH is safeguarded through non-formal means; (b) serve as places for transmitting traditional knowledge and skills, contributing to intergenerational dialogue; (c) serve as information centres about a community's ICH. Research institutes, centres of expertise, museums, archives, libraries and documentation centres play an important role in collecting, documenting, archiving and conserving data and raising awareness; they are encouraged to (a) involve practitioners and bearers when organizing exhibitions, lectures, seminars, debates and training; (b) introduce participatory approaches presenting ICH as living heritage in constant evolution; (c) focus on continuous recreation and transmission of knowledge and skills rather than on associated objects; (d) employ information and communication technologies to communicate the meaning and value of ICH; (e) involve practitioners and bearers in management, with participatory systems for local development.

*Communications and media (110–115):* The media can effectively contribute to raising awareness (110), and are encouraged to foster social cohesion, sustainable development and prevention of conflict rather than focus only on aesthetic or entertainment aspects (111); to raise awareness of the diversity of ICH manifestations through specialized programmes and products for different target groups (112); audiovisual media are encouraged to create quality television and radio programmes and documentaries, with local broadcasting networks and community radios playing a major role in local languages, culture and good safeguarding practices (113); the media are encouraged to support communities' safeguarding efforts through their networks and discussion forums (114); information technology institutions are encouraged to facilitate interactive exchange and enhance non-formal transmission, particularly through interactive programmes and games targeting youth (115).

*Commercial activities related to intangible heritage (116–117):* Commercial activities emerging from ICH and trade in related cultural goods and services can raise awareness and generate income for practitioners, improving living standards, enhancing local economies and contributing to social cohesion — but should not threaten the viability of the heritage, and communities should be their primary beneficiaries; particular attention should be given to effects on the nature and viability of ICH, especially in the domains of rituals, social practices or knowledge about nature and the universe (116). Particular attention should be paid to avoiding commercial misappropriation, managing tourism sustainably, balancing the interests of the commercial party, public administration and cultural practitioners, and ensuring that commercial use does not distort the meaning and purpose of the heritage for the community concerned (117).

### IV.1.3 International level — visibility and awareness-raising (p. 68–69)

The Committee updates and publishes annually the three international instruments of the Convention: the List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding, the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, and the Register of good safeguarding practices (programmes, projects and activities). To ensure visibility of intangible cultural heritage (ICH) at local, national and international levels, the Committee encourages dissemination of the Lists through: (a) schools, including UNESCO's Associated Schools network; (b) community centres, museums, archives, libraries and similar entities; (c) universities, centres of expertise and research institutes; (d) all forms of media, including UNESCO's website. The Committee also encourages audiovisual and digital material, publications, maps, stamps, posters and stickers. When publicizing inscribed elements, care must be taken to present them in their context and focus on their value and meaning for the communities concerned, "rather than only on their aesthetic appeal or entertainment value" (para. 120).

The UNESCO Secretariat assists by: (a) acting as a clearing house for collecting, exchanging and disseminating information (databases, information management system, website); (b) facilitating information exchange among communities, civil society, NGOs, centres of expertise and research institutes; (c) producing training and information material that is easily reproduced and translated locally; (d) organizing workshops, seminars and conferences; (e) coordinating awareness-raising with other UNESCO instruments, UN agencies and intergovernmental organizations; (f) promoting ICH in international celebrations (International Mother Language Day, World Day for Cultural Diversity for Dialogue and Development) and launching campaigns to raise voluntary contributions to the Fund; (g) including ICH training in UNESCO scholarships and traineeships.

### IV.2 Use of the emblem of the Convention (p. 70–75)

The Convention's emblem is its official seal and must always be accompanied by UNESCO's emblem; it may not be used in isolation, and each emblem is governed by separate rules (paras. 124–128). Only the statutory organs — the General Assembly and the Committee — and the Secretariat may use the Convention's emblem without prior authorization (para. 129). Authorization is the prerogative of the statutory organs, which may delegate to the Director-General for patronage, contractual arrangements, partnerships and promotional activities; the power cannot be granted to other bodies. Any authorization must rest on two criteria: (i) relevance to the Convention's purposes and objectives, and (ii) compliance with its principles (para. 134).

**Patronage** (paras. 137–139) may be granted for performances, films, publications, congresses, prizes and events embodying ICH, based on the criteria of *Impact* (exceptional activities with real safeguarding impact and significantly enhanced visibility) and *Reliability* (assurances about those in charge and the activity's feasibility). Conditions: requests must reach the Secretariat at least three months in advance; authorization is in writing, exclusively by the Director-General; national activities require obligatory consultation with the host State Party; the Convention must receive appropriate visibility; recurring activities need fixed duration and periodic renewal. Communities, groups and individuals are encouraged to use the emblem for activities safeguarding their inscribed heritage.

**Commercial use** (paras. 140–143): sale of goods or services bearing the emblem chiefly for profit is "commercial use" and requires express written authorization by the Director-General under a specific contract; if connected to an inscribed element, the State(s) Party(ies) concerned must be consulted. When profit is anticipated, the Intangible Cultural Heritage Fund must receive a fair share of revenues.

**Protection** (paras. 144–150): the emblem must be reproduced according to graphical standards published on the Convention's website and not altered. Under Article 6ter of the Paris Convention, UNESCO can rely on Member States' domestic systems to prevent falsely suggestive or abusive use. States Parties should designate national authorities for emblem management; the Director-General institutes proceedings against unauthorized international use, national authorities at national level.

### Chapter V: Reporting to the Committee (p. 75–80)

**Periodic reports by States Parties** (paras. 151–159): each State Party reports every six years by 15 December, on a region-by-region rotation set by the Committee, using Form ICH-10 (online at ich.unesco.org). Reports cover: (a) inventories (Articles 11–12 of the Convention); (b) other safeguarding measures — general policy, scientific/technical/artistic studies, access to information respecting customary practices; (c) institutional capacity — competent bodies, training institutions, documentation institutions; (d) recognition, respect and enhancement — educational and awareness programmes, community training, capacity-building, non-formal transmission, education for natural spaces and places of memory; (e) international cooperation measures (Article 19); (f) the current status of all elements inscribed on the Representative List, addressing for each element its social and cultural functions, viability and risks, contribution to the List's goals, promotion efforts, and community/NGO participation and continued commitment; and (g) the institutional context — competent bodies and community organizations. Special attention must be paid to the role of gender and to the widest possible participation of communities and NGOs.

**Reports on Urgent Safeguarding List elements** (paras. 160–164): submitted at the Committee's request or, in extreme urgency, after consultation, normally by 15 December of the fourth year following inscription and every fourth year thereafter, using Form ICH-11 (the Committee may set a case-specific timetable at inscription). Reports cover the element's social and cultural functions, viability and risks, impacts of the safeguarding plan, an updated safeguarding plan, community participation, and the institutional context.

**Processing** (paras. 165–166): the Secretariat registers and acknowledges reports, advises on completion of incomplete ones, and transmits an overview to the Committee four weeks before its session; reports are published online in the language of submission. **States non-party** with former Masterpieces on the Representative List report by 15 December 2014 and every sixth year thereafter (paras. 168–169).

### Chapter VI: Safeguarding ICH and sustainable development at the national level (p. 80–96)

States Parties shall endeavour, by all appropriate means, to recognize the importance and strengthen the role of intangible cultural heritage as a driver and guarantee of sustainable development, and to fully integrate the safeguarding of ICH into their development plans, policies and programmes at all levels, balancing the economic, social and environmental dimensions of sustainable development and their interdependence with peace and security (para. 170). Safeguarding must target only heritage compatible with human rights instruments, mutual respect and sustainable development. Communities must be the primary beneficiaries — morally and materially — of plans and policies, which must respect ethical considerations and not decontextualize heritage (para. 171). States Parties should assess development impacts on ICH, protect communities' rights (including through intellectual property and privacy rights), ensure inclusiveness of all sectors — indigenous peoples, migrants, immigrants, refugees, people of different ages and genders, persons with disabilities, vulnerable groups — and prevent misuse of inscriptions for short-term economic gain (paras. 172–176).

**VI.1 Inclusive social development** (p. 82–87) covers five areas:
- *Food security* (para. 178): recognize and enhance farming, fishing, hunting, pastoral, food-gathering, preparation and preservation knowledge; foster studies on their efficacy and resilience to climate change; adopt measures for equitable benefit-sharing and transmission; respect customary rights to land, sea and forest ecosystems.
- *Health care* (para. 179): recognize health practices contributing to well-being; promote access to healing knowledge and raw materials in consultation with knowledge holders, respecting customary access rules; enhance collaboration among diverse health systems.
- *Quality education* (para. 180): integrate ICH into all educational disciplines at curricular, cross-curricular and extra-curricular levels; ensure education does not alienate people from their heritage or stigmatize communities; harness community-recognized transmission methods within formal and non-formal systems; promote education for biodiversity conservation and protection of natural spaces and places of memory.
- *Gender equality* (para. 181): ICH is a privileged context in which gender identities are shaped; use it to create dialogue spaces, build mutual respect among groups with different gender conceptions, help communities examine their expressions' impact on gender equality, and ensure gender equality in planning and implementing safeguarding measures.
- *Water* (para. 182): ensure viability of ICH water management systems promoting equitable access to safe drinking water and sustainable use, and strengthen their resilience to climate change.

**VI.2 Inclusive economic development** (p. 87–90): ICH contributes to equitable, inclusive economic growth and strengthening local economies; States should respect communities' choice of collective or individual management and promote fair trade and ethical economic relations (paras. 183–184). Sub-areas: *income generation and sustainable livelihoods* (para. 185) — ensure communities are primary beneficiaries and not dispossessed of their heritage to generate income for others; *productive employment and decent work* (para. 186) — including tax incentives and social security protection, again with communities as primary beneficiaries; *tourism* (para. 187) — tourism must show due respect to ICH and communities' rights and wishes; assess impacts before activities begin; ensure communities lead and primarily benefit from tourism; ensure viability, social functions and cultural meanings are not diminished; guide industry interventions and tourist behaviour.

**VI.3 Environmental sustainability** (p. 90–93): *knowledge and practices concerning nature and the universe* (para. 189) — recognize communities as bearers of such knowledge and essential environmental actors; promote access and transmission while respecting customary practices; conserve natural spaces necessary for expressing ICH. *Environmental impacts of safeguarding* (para. 190) — recognize potential impacts of intensified practices and mitigate harm. *Community-based resilience to natural disasters and climate change* (para. 191) — harness geoscience and climate knowledge for risk reduction and recovery; integrate knowledge bearers into disaster risk reduction and climate adaptation programmes.

**VI.4 ICH and peace** (p. 93–96): sustainable development cannot be realized without peace and security, and vice versa (para. 192). Sub-areas: *social cohesion and equity* (para. 194) — attend to practices that transcend differences of gender, colour, ethnicity, origin, class and locality; *preventing and resolving disputes* (para. 195) — support conflict-preventing expressions and integrate them into public programmes; *restoring peace and security* (para. 196) — use ICH's potential in reconciliation, re-establishing safety and recovery; *achieving lasting peace* (para. 197) — ensure respect for the heritage of indigenous peoples, migrants, refugees and vulnerable groups, widen participation, and promote peace-building through intercultural dialogue and respect for cultural diversity.

### Rules of Procedure of the General Assembly of the States Parties (p. 97–112)

Adopted at the first session (Paris, 27–29 June 2006) and amended in 2008, 2014 and 2022. The General Assembly is "the sovereign body of the Convention" (Rule 1). All States Parties participate with the right to vote; UNESCO Member States not party, Associate Members, permanent observer missions, UN bodies and invited intergovernmental/non-governmental organizations attend as observers without vote (Rules 2–3).

The Assembly meets every two years in ordinary session; extraordinary sessions occur on its decision, at the Committee's request, or at the request of at least one-third of States Parties (Rule 4). The Director-General sets dates; sessions are at UNESCO Headquarters unless decided otherwise (Rule 5). **Online sessions** (Rule 6) are allowed only in emergencies or exceptional circumstances, by simple majority decision, or by correspondence if at least one-third of States Parties propose it (unless one-third disagree); elections by secret ballot during online sessions must be conducted *in praesentia*. The provisional agenda is prepared by the Director-General and circulated at least sixty days before ordinary sessions (fifteen days for extraordinary); the Assembly may amend the agenda by two-thirds majority (Rules 7–9).

The Bureau comprises the Chairperson, up to five Vice-Chairpersons and a Rapporteur, elected at each session's opening with equitable geographical representation; officers serve for the session and may not be immediately re-elected after two consecutive terms (Rules 10–11). The Chairperson directs discussions, rules on points of order and does not vote (may delegate voting within the delegation) (Rule 12). A quorum is a majority of States Parties represented (Rule 13); meetings are public unless decided otherwise (Rule 14). Subsidiary bodies may be established with defined mandates (Rule 15). Rules govern speeches, draft resolutions (circulated reasonably in advance), points of order, and procedural motions — suspension/adjournment of the meeting, adjournment or closure of debate — with a fixed order of precedence (Rules 16–23).

Working languages are Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian and Spanish; documents are distributed at least thirty days before ordinary sessions; summary records are prepared in English and French (Rules 24–26). Each State Party has one vote; every effort is made to decide by consensus, otherwise by simple majority of States Parties present and voting (abstentions count as not voting) (Rules 27–34). The contribution percentage for States that have not made the Article 26.2 declaration is set by simple majority of those States (Rule 30.2); voting is by show of hands, with roll-call on doubt or request of two States Parties (Rule 31).

**Elections to the Committee** (Rules 35–37): conducted on the basis of UNESCO electoral groups (Group V split into African and Arab States); the 24 Committee seats are distributed proportionally, with at least three seats per group. Candidatures must reach the Secretariat at least six weeks before the Assembly; no contributions to the Fund are accepted later than a week before opening; the list is finalized three working days before opening. Election is by secret ballot (or acclamation where candidates equal or fewer than seats), with separate ballot papers per electoral group; ties are resolved by restricted second ballot, then by drawing lots.

The Director-General (or representative) participates without vote and appoints the Secretary of the Assembly (Rule 38). The Rules may be amended or suspended by two-thirds majority, except where they reproduce provisions of the Convention (Rules 39–40).

### Rules of Procedure of the Intergovernmental Committee (p. 113–132)

Adopted at the first session (Algiers, 18–19 November 2006) and amended at sessions in Sofia (2008), Istanbul (2008), Nairobi (2010), Baku (2013), Windhoek (2015) and Jeju Island (2017). The Committee comprises States Parties elected under Article 5 of the Convention (Rule 1). It meets at least once a year in ordinary session, and in extraordinary session at the request of at least two-thirds of its Members (Rule 2); sessions are convened by the Chairperson in consultation with the Director-General, with at least sixty days' notice for ordinary sessions (thirty for extraordinary) (Rule 3). The Committee determines the next session's date and place, with equitable rotation among regions; any Member may invite the Committee to its territory (Rule 4).

Each Member State is represented by one delegate assisted by alternates, advisers and experts, qualified in ICH fields (Rule 5); the budget covers participation of experts from developing States Members (and, if funds allow, developing States Parties not on the Committee), with requests at least eight weeks before sessions and normally no more than one representative per State financed. Accredited NGOs attend in an advisory capacity (Rule 6); the Committee may invite competent bodies and persons for consultation (Rule 7). States Parties not on the Committee, UNESCO/UN Member States, Associate Members, observer missions and UN organizations attend as observers; the Committee may authorize other organizations and persons upon written request; public meetings are open to the public within space limits (Rule 8).

The provisional agenda is prepared by the Director-General and adopted at the session's beginning; amendments require a two-thirds majority (Rules 9–11). The Bureau consists of the Chairperson, Vice-Chairpersons and Rapporteur, elected at the end of each ordinary session from Members whose term continues, with equitable geographical representation and balance among ICH fields; officers are eligible for one immediate re-election (Rules 12–13). The Chairperson directs proceedings and does not vote; a Chairperson must abstain from exercising functions on issues relating to an element present in the territory of the State of which he/she is a national (Rules 14–15). A Vice-Chairperson replaces an absent or incapacitated Chairperson or Rapporteur (Rules 15–16).

A quorum is a majority of Members (subsidiary bodies: majority of their members) (Rule 17). Meetings are public unless decided otherwise — a rule the Bureau may not suspend (Rule 18); decisions of private meetings are presented at subsequent public meetings, and private-meeting documents become public after twenty years (Rule 19). The Committee may establish ad hoc consultative bodies (Article 8.3) and subsidiary bodies composed only of Committee Members, with equitable regional representation (Rules 20–21). Notably, representatives of a State Party — Member or observer — shall not speak to advocate inscription of their own nomination or endorse their own assistance request, but only provide information in reply to questions (Rule 22.4). Rules govern circulation of proposal texts, division of proposals, voting on amendments (furthest from the original first), withdrawal of proposals, points of order and procedural motions (Rules 23–33).

Decisions are taken by simple majority of States Members present and voting (abstentions not voting) (Rules 34–38). Voting is normally by show of hands, with roll-call on doubt or request of two Members; a decision is voted by secret ballot whenever two or more Members so request (Rule 39). Elections to ad hoc consultative and subsidiary bodies follow the same secret-ballot procedure as General Assembly elections, with tellers, separate ballot papers per electoral group, invalid ballots for over-circling or blank indication, and lot-drawing after tied second ballots (Rules 39.7–39.16).

The UNESCO Secretariat assists the Committee; the Director-General participates without vote and appoints the Secretary (Rule 40). Working languages are English and French, with efforts to facilitate other UN official languages; documents are issued in English and French at least four weeks before sessions (Rules 41–42). The Committee adopts its report as a list of decisions, published within the month following closure; summary records are prepared for approval at the next session; documentation is transmitted to Members, States Parties, accredited organizations and invited bodies; the Committee reports to the General Assembly and the UNESCO General Conference at each ordinary session (Rules 43–46). The Rules are adopted, amended and suspended by two-thirds majorities, except where they reproduce Convention provisions (Rules 47–49).

### Financial Regulations of the Special Account for the Fund for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage (p. 133–139)

Adopted by the Committee at its first extraordinary session (Chengdu, China, 23–27 May 2007) and revised by the General Assembly at its eighth session (Paris, 8–10 September 2020). Article 25 of the Convention establishes the Fund, for which a Special Account is created under UNESCO's Financial Regulations (Art. 1). The financial period for budget estimates is two consecutive calendar years beginning with an even-numbered year; accounting is annual (Art. 2). The purpose is to receive contributions and make payments to assist ICH safeguarding (Art. 3). The General Assembly has authority over appropriation; the Committee prepares the draft plan for use of resources; the Director-General manages the funds and reports biennially (Art. 4).

**Income** (Art. 5) consists of: (a) contributions by States Parties; (b) UNESCO General Conference appropriations; (c) contributions, gifts or bequests from other States, UN system organizations and programmes (particularly UNDP), other international organizations, and public or private bodies or individuals; (d) interest; (e) funds raised through collections and benefit events; (f) any other resources authorized by the Committee. States Parties that have not made the Article 26.2 declaration contribute according to the uniform percentage set by the General Assembly (Art. 5.2). Expenditure is approved biennially and made within available funds (Art. 6). A **reserve fund** meets requests for assistance in cases of extreme urgency (Articles 17.3 and 22.2 of the Convention), its amount set by the Committee (Art. 7). Unused balances are carried forward; accounts are part of UNESCO's consolidated statements audited by the External Auditor; contributions in kind are recorded outside the Special Account (Art. 8). The Director-General may invest sums, with revenue credited to the Account (Art. 9). Biennial financial and narrative reports go to the Committee and General Assembly (Art. 10). Closure requires consultation of the General Assembly and transmission of its decision to the Executive Board (Art. 11); amendments require General Assembly approval, and otherwise the Account is administered under UNESCO's Financial Regulations (Art. 12).

### Ethical Principles for Safeguarding Intangible Cultural Heritage (p. 143–145)

Adopted at the Committee's tenth session (Windhoek, Namibia, 30 November–4 December 2015), these twelve principles were drafted in the spirit of the 2003 Convention and of existing international norms protecting human rights and indigenous peoples' rights. They form a set of overarching, aspirational guidelines widely accepted as good practice for any government, organization or individual whose work touches intangible cultural heritage (ICH) directly or indirectly, with the aim of securing its viability and acknowledging what it contributes to peace and sustainable development. Sitting alongside the Convention, its Operational Directives and national laws, they are meant as groundwork for developing codes of ethics and tools tailored to local and sectoral conditions.

The twelve principles, in order:

1. The primary role in safeguarding their own ICH belongs to "communities, groups and, where applicable, individuals" (the Convention's recurring formula, shortened below).
2. Their entitlement to keep alive the "practices, representations, expressions, knowledge and skills" that their ICH needs in order to remain viable deserves recognition and respect.
3. Interactions between States, and among communities, groups and individuals, should be governed by mutual respect and by respect for, and appreciation of, one another's ICH.
4. Any interaction with those who create, safeguard, maintain and transmit ICH must be collaborative and transparent — built on dialogue, negotiation and consultation — and dependent on their "free, prior, sustained and informed consent".
5. Communities, groups and individuals must be able to reach the instruments, objects and artefacts, and the cultural and natural spaces and the places of memory without which their ICH cannot be expressed, even in armed conflict. Customary rules governing access deserve full respect even where they restrict wider public access.
6. The value of ICH is for each community, group or individual itself to assess; outsiders should not sit in judgement of its value or worth.
7. Those who create ICH should enjoy protection for the moral and material interests arising from it — especially interests arising when community members or others use, research, document, promote or adapt it.
8. ICH's living, dynamic character deserves continuous respect; authenticity and exclusivity should be neither a preoccupation nor an obstacle in safeguarding.
9. Any action likely to bear on the viability of ICH, or on the communities practising it, calls for careful assessment by communities, groups, organizations (local, national and transnational) and individuals of its impacts — direct and indirect, short- and long-term, potential and definitive.
10. What counts as a threat to their ICH — decontextualization, commodification and misrepresentation among them — and how to prevent or soften such threats, is for communities, groups and individuals to weigh significantly.
11. Cultural diversity and the identities of communities, groups and individuals merit full respect. Safeguarding measures should be designed and carried out with regard for the values those communities recognize, sensitivity to cultural norms, and particular attention to gender equality, the involvement of youth and respect for ethnic identities.
12. Because safeguarding ICH concerns humanity as a whole, it should proceed through cooperation at bilateral, subregional, regional and international levels — yet communities, groups and individuals must never be cut off from their own heritage.

### Operational Principles and Modalities for Safeguarding Intangible Cultural Heritage in Emergencies (p. 147–154)

Adopted by the General Assembly at its eighth session (Paris, UNESCO Headquarters, 8–10 September 2020), the text followed a category VI expert meeting at UNESCO Headquarters (21–22 May 2019) and was endorsed by the Committee at its fourteenth session (Bogotá, Colombia, 9–14 December 2019). It guides States Parties and other national or international actors on how best to engage and safeguard ICH amid emergencies of various kinds.

The document stresses ICH's dual role in emergencies: they can directly threaten it, yet it can also equip communities to get ready for, react to and bounce back from them. The text aligns with UNESCO's strategy for reinforcing its action to protect culture and promote cultural pluralism during armed conflict, its Addendum on disasters arising from natural and human-induced hazards, UN Security Council Resolution 2347 (2017), the Convention and its Operational Directives (especially Chapter VI on ICH and sustainable development at national level), and the Ethical Principles.

#### Principles (p. 150–151)

Six principles underpin all interventions:

1. ICH exists only as communities enact it, practise it and pass it on; it cannot be separated from their social, cultural and economic life, so safeguarding it is inseparable from protecting its bearers' lives and well-being.
2. Communities whose ICH an emergency may touch include people inside the disaster or conflict zone, displaced people and those who host them, and anyone else connected with that heritage.
3. Whatever the phase of the emergency, identifying the ICH is primarily the communities' task. They must be directly involved in working out how the emergency has affected it, what safeguarding measures are required, and how it can serve as a resource — strengthening resilience, aiding recovery and rebuilding trust and peaceful coexistence within and between communities.
4. Under Article 11 of the Convention, States Parties must take the measures needed to safeguard the ICH found on their territory — an obligation that holds in emergencies too. They should also strive for the widest possible community participation in safeguarding, extending to refugees, internally displaced persons and migrants on their territory.
5. Emergency-management actors at national and international level — disaster-preparedness and relief specialists, humanitarians, NGOs and armed forces — can do much to protect affected ICH and to help communities mobilize it when preparing for and responding to emergencies.
6. ICH is dynamic and adaptive, continually remade by communities in response to their environment, their interaction with nature and their history, emergencies included. All safeguarding or engagement efforts should acknowledge and respect that quality.

#### Modalities (p. 151–153)

The modalities map actions onto the three phases of the emergency management cycle — preparedness, response and recovery — which differ in length, may overlap, and whose applicability depends on local circumstances.

**Preparedness** (p. 151–152):
1. Make relevant stakeholders aware of ICH's dual character in emergencies and of these principles and modalities, and build their capacities.
2. Resource and support communities' capacity to take part in every aspect of risk reduction and emergency preparedness, consulting other stakeholders, above all in emergency-prone regions and countries.
3. Add to ICH inventories (as foreseen by the Convention) data on how vulnerable elements are to possible emergencies, covering their mitigation capacity plus details of the locations and communities concerned, so that elements can be identified and reached during response.
4. Build emergency preparedness into safeguarding plans for individual elements: preventive steps against potential vulnerability, preparatory steps to strengthen and mobilize mitigation capacity, and a method for assessing the element's condition during response.
5. Work relevant ICH into risk-reduction and preparedness planning at local, national, subregional and regional levels.
6. Connect ICH-safeguarding bodies with those responsible for emergency preparedness.

**Response** (p. 152–153):
1. As early as possible, identify, locate and contact communities whose ICH the emergency is known or thought to have affected.
2. Give first priority to resourcing and supporting communities' own capacity — via a community-based approach — to pinpoint and meet immediate safeguarding needs and to use their ICH to soften the emergency's immediate effects (community-based needs identification). In some situations this can only happen during recovery.
3. Share information among affected States Parties and with other actors — humanitarians, relevant NGOs and/or armed forces — on how far ICH has been disrupted and how it might be engaged in mitigation, so that relief operations fully account for existing ICH and help safeguard it.
4. Where a post-disaster or post-conflict needs assessment takes place, particularly within multiparty international crisis-response mechanisms, make sure ICH is included; communities should help assess the emergency's effects on their heritage, along with related economic damage and losses and human development impacts.

**Recovery** (p. 153):
1. Where it could not be done earlier, conduct the community-based needs identification.
2. On that basis, resource and support communities in developing and carrying out safeguarding measures or plans that strengthen their ICH's mitigation capacity — an engagement to be sustained through recovery into the next preparedness phase and through the shift from humanitarian assistance to development.
3. Mobilize ICH to encourage dialogue, mutual understanding and reconciliation — both within communities and between them, displaced populations and host communities included.

A closing note (p. 153–154) states that funding should be sought from emergency-related sources, among them the UNESCO Heritage Emergency Fund and the Intangible Cultural Heritage Fund (emergency International Assistance). The Convention's lists can raise the profile of elements that help communities prepare for, respond to and recover from disasters and conflicts (Representative List, Urgent Safeguarding List, Register of Good Safeguarding Practices), or alert the international community to elements especially endangered (see the accelerated nomination procedure to the Urgent Safeguarding List, criterion U.6, Chapter I.1 of the Operational Directives).

### Overall Results Framework for the Convention (p. 155–169)

Approved by the General Assembly at its seventh session (Paris, UNESCO Headquarters, 4–6 June 2018), the framework grew out of a recommendation from UNESCO's Internal Oversight Service, made during its 2013 evaluation of the Culture Sector's standard-setting work. A category VI expert meeting (Beijing, China, 7–9 September 2016) produced a high-level results map endorsed by the Committee at its eleventh session that year; an open-ended intergovernmental working group (Chengdu, China, 11–13 June 2017) then drafted the framework, which the Committee endorsed at its twelfth session (December 2017) and the General Assembly approved roughly six months later.

The framework gauges the Convention's impact at several levels via clearly set objectives, indicators and benchmarks and a results-oriented monitoring system. It comprises two tables: the high-level framework (impacts, expected outcomes, the eight thematic areas, twenty-six core indicators) and a second pairing the core indicators with eighty-six assessment factors grouped by thematic area. Guidance notes exist for all twenty-six indicators to help States Parties and others apply the framework. Periodic reporting was reformed in parallel: form ICH-10 was rewritten to match the framework so data on the relevant core indicators can be gathered; during reporting, States Parties may set country-level targets and baselines; and the guidance notes are built into the online ICH-10 form.

#### High-level framework (p. 158–159)

Impact: communities, groups and individuals safeguard ICH as its active, ongoing stewards; this stewardship feeds sustainable development, advancing human well-being, dignity and creativity within peaceful, inclusive societies.

Long-term outcomes: ICH keeps being practised and transmitted; the diversity of ICH is respected; the importance of ICH and its safeguarding gains recognition and awareness; and safeguarding engagement and international cooperation deepen among all stakeholders, at every level.

Mid-term outcomes: diverse communities, groups and individuals build effective relationships with other stakeholders for safeguarding purposes, and they lead the dynamic development and execution of safeguarding measures or plans for particular elements.

Short-term outcomes: capacities grow both for supporting ICH safeguarding in general and for putting safeguarding measures or plans for particular elements into effect.

The eight thematic areas and their twenty-six brief core indicators:

- **Institutional and human capacities**: (1) competent bodies back practice and transmission; (2) programmes build human capacities for safeguarding; (3) training is run by, or directed at, communities and culture and heritage workers.
- **Transmission and education**: (4) formal and non-formal education both reinforce transmission and encourage respect; (5) primary and secondary schooling integrates ICH; (6) post-secondary education supports safeguarding and the study of ICH.
- **Inventorying and research**: (7) inventories mirror ICH's diversity and aid its safeguarding; (8) inventorying is inclusive, respects diversity and supports communities' and groups' safeguarding; (9) research and documentation serve safeguarding; (10) research findings are available and used.
- **Policies and legal/administrative measures**: (11) cultural policies and legal and administrative measures take account of ICH's diversity and are implemented; (12) the same holds in education; (13) the same holds in fields beyond culture and education; (14) such policies and measures honour customary rights, practices and expressions.
- **Role of ICH and its safeguarding in society**: (15) society at large, and the communities concerned, widely acknowledge ICH's importance; (16) inclusive plans and programmes affirm safeguarding's importance and nurture self-respect and mutual respect.
- **Awareness raising**: (17) communities, groups and individuals take broad part in awareness raising; (18) media contribute to it; (19) public information measures build awareness; (20) awareness raising observes the ethical principles.
- **Engagement of communities, groups and individuals, and of other stakeholders**: (21) stakeholder engagement for ICH safeguarding deepens; (22) civil society helps monitor safeguarding.
- **International engagement**: (23) the Committee draws on NGOs, public and private bodies and private persons (monitored only at the global level); (24) States Parties work together on safeguarding; (25) States Parties take part in international networking and institutional cooperation; (26) the ICH Fund underpins safeguarding and international engagement (monitored only at the global level).

#### Core indicators and assessment factors (p. 160–169)

Table 2 sets out the eighty-six assessment factors. Highlights by area:

- **Institutional and human capacities** (p. 160): factors 1.1–1.5 cover the designation or establishment of competent safeguarding bodies (including bodies for specific elements, inscribed or not — "whether or not inscribed" meaning inscribed on either the Urgent Safeguarding List or the Representative List); broad, inclusive participation — especially of the communities, groups and individuals concerned — fostered via consultative bodies or other coordination mechanisms; support for documentation institutions and initiatives and use of their materials; and contributions from cultural centres, museums, archives, libraries, centres of expertise and research institutions. Factors 2.1–2.3 cover tertiary curricula and degrees, government-run training, and community- or NGO-based training, all delivered "on an inclusive basis": reaching every sector and stratum of society — indigenous peoples; migrants, immigrants and refugees; people of all ages and genders; persons with disabilities; and members of vulnerable groups (States Parties are encouraged to supply disaggregated data or explain how inclusiveness is ensured). Factors 3.1–3.2 cover training addressed inclusively to communities, groups and individuals, and to culture and heritage workers.
- **Transmission and education** (p. 161): factors 4.1–4.4 cover the inclusive involvement of practitioners and bearers in designing ICH education programmes or in presenting and transmitting their heritage; the learning or strengthening of transmission modes and methods recognized by communities within formal and non-formal programmes; community-, group-, NGO- or heritage-institution-run programmes and extra-curricular activities available and supported; and teacher training and training for non-formal educators covering the integration of ICH. Factors 5.1–5.4 cover ICH in its diversity within relevant school disciplines, both for its own sake and to illuminate other subjects; students learning to respect and reflect on their own and others' ICH; learners' heritage reflected through mother-tongue or multilingual teaching and/or "local content" in curricula; and teaching about protecting the natural and cultural spaces and the places of memory ICH depends on. Factors 6.1–6.2 cover post-secondary curricula and degrees (music, arts, crafts, technical and vocational education and training, etc.) that reinforce practice and transmission, and curricula and degrees for studying ICH and its social, cultural and other dimensions.
- **Inventorying and research** (p. 162): factors 7.1–7.4 cover safeguarding-oriented inventorying systems reflecting ICH diversity, established or revised since ratification; specialized inventories and inventories of varying scope; inventories updated during the reporting period, notably to reflect elements' current viability; and access facilitated while customary access rules are respected, with inventories used to strengthen safeguarding. Factors 8.1–8.2 cover inclusive participation of communities, groups and relevant NGOs in inventorying that informs and strengthens safeguarding, and the process honouring the diversity of ICH and its practitioners across all sectors of society, all genders and all regions. Factors 9.1–9.3 cover financial and other support for research, studies of a scientific, technical or artistic kind, documentation and archiving oriented to safeguarding and consistent with the ethical principles; research on safeguarding approaches and impacts, for ICH generally and for specific elements, inscribed or not; and the participation of practitioners and bearers in managing, implementing and disseminating research and studies, always with their "free, prior, sustained and informed consent".
- **Policies and legal/administrative measures** (p. 163–164): factors 11.1–11.4 cover cultural policies and/or legal and administrative measures integrating ICH and its diversity, established or revised and being implemented; national or sub-national strategies and action plans, including element-specific safeguarding plans; equitable public financial or technical support relative to overall culture and heritage funding, with priority for elements needing urgent safeguarding; and policies informed by communities' active participation. Factors 12.1–12.3 cover education policies and measures ensuring recognition, respect for and enhancement of ICH; strengthening its transmission and practice; and promoting mother-tongue instruction and multilingual education. Factors 13.1–13.5 cover respect for the Ethical Principles in development plans, policies and programmes; policies for inclusive social development and environmental sustainability revised to consider ICH; disaster- or conflict-response policies revised to include affected ICH and acknowledge its importance for affected populations' resilience; policies for inclusive economic development revised to consider ICH; and favourable financial or fiscal measures or incentives easing practice and transmission and increasing the availability of the natural and other resources required. Factors 14.1–14.3 cover legal protection (intellectual property and privacy rights) for practitioners, bearers and communities whose ICH others exploit commercially or otherwise; recognition of communities' and groups' customary rights to the land, sea and forest ecosystems their ICH requires; and recognition of ICH expressions, practices and representations that help prevent disputes and resolve conflicts peacefully.
- **Role of ICH and its safeguarding in society** (p. 165): factors 15.1–15.3 cover communities using ICH for well-being, including within sustainable development programmes; for dialogue that furthers mutual respect, conflict resolution and peace-building; and development interventions treating ICH as a source of identity, continuity, knowledge and skills and reinforcing its role as a sustainable development resource. Factors 16.1–16.2 cover safeguarding plans and programmes reaching all sectors and strata (indigenous peoples; groups with different ethnic identities; migrants, immigrants and refugees; people of different ages; people of different genders; persons with disabilities; members of vulnerable groups) and fostering self-respect and mutual respect among communities, groups and individuals.
- **Awareness raising** (p. 166–167): factors 17.1–17.5 cover awareness raising that reflects the inclusive, widest possible participation of the communities concerned; their "free, prior, sustained and informed consent" obtained for activities about specific elements; protection of their rights and moral and material interests; active youth engagement, including in collecting and spreading information about their communities' ICH; and use of ICTs and all media, new media especially. Factors 18.1–18.4 cover media coverage that builds awareness and mutual respect; cooperation activities or programmes (capacity building included) between ICH stakeholders and media organizations; inclusive media programming in the communities' languages and/or aimed at different target groups; and coverage consistent with the Convention's concepts and terminology. Factors 19.1–19.4 cover public acknowledgement of practitioners and bearers through policies and programmes; public events on ICH, its safeguarding and the Convention for communities, the general public, researchers, media and other stakeholders; support for promoting and disseminating good safeguarding practices; and public information encouraging mutual respect and appreciation within and between communities and groups. Factors 20.1–20.2 cover respect for the Ethical Principles, and for ethical principles embodied in professional codes or standards, in awareness raising.
- **Engagement of communities, groups and individuals, and of other stakeholders** (p. 167): factors 21.1–21.3 cover the inclusive, widest possible participation of communities, groups and individuals in safeguarding ICH generally and specific elements; participation of NGOs and other civil society actors; and participation of private sector entities, in line with the Ethical Principles.
- **International engagement** (p. 168–169): factors 22.1–22.3 cover enabling environments for communities, groups and individuals; NGOs and other civil society bodies; and scholars, experts, research institutions and centres of expertise — each able to monitor and conduct scientific, technical or artistic studies of safeguarding programmes and measures. Factors 23.1–23.3 cover the numbers and geographic spread of accredited NGOs and the domains they represent; the share of accredited NGOs attending sessions and working groups of the Convention's governing bodies, and their distribution; and occasions on which the Committee consults accredited NGOs beyond the evaluation mechanisms. Factors 24.1–24.4 cover bilateral, multilateral, regional or international cooperation implementing safeguarding measures for ICH generally and for specific elements (those in danger, those spanning more than one State's territory, cross-border elements); exchange of information and experience, good safeguarding practices included; and sharing of documentation on an element located in another State Party's territory. Factors 25.1–25.3 cover engagement with category 2 centres for ICH as host or beneficiary; international networking among communities, groups and individuals, NGOs, experts, centres of expertise and research institutes active in ICH; and participation in the ICH-related activities of international and regional bodies beyond UNESCO. ## GLOSSAR

- **Intangible cultural heritage (ICH)** — the practices, representations, expressions, knowledge, skills — and associated instruments, objects, artefacts and cultural spaces — that communities, groups and, in some cases, individuals recognize as part of their cultural heritage; transmitted from generation to generation and constantly recreated, providing a sense of identity and continuity; only compatible with human rights instruments, mutual respect and sustainable development is considered.
- **Safeguarding** — measures aimed at ensuring the viability of intangible cultural heritage, including identification, documentation, research, preservation, protection, promotion, enhancement, transmission (particularly through formal and non-formal education) and revitalization.
- **States Parties** — States bound by the Convention and among which it is in force (also referring, mutatis mutandis, to territories under Article 33).
- **General Assembly** — the sovereign body of the Convention, meeting in ordinary session every two years.
- **Committee** — the Intergovernmental Committee for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage, of 18 (rising to 24) States Parties' representatives elected by the General Assembly.
- **Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity** — the List established to ensure better visibility and awareness of the significance of ICH and encourage dialogue respecting cultural diversity.
- **List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding** — the List of heritage whose viability is at risk or facing grave threats, inscribed at the request of the State Party concerned.
- **Fund** — the Fund for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage, established under Article 25, used primarily for granting International Assistance.
- **Evaluation Body** — the Committee's consultative body of twelve members (six experts from States Parties non-Members of the Committee and six accredited NGOs) that evaluates nominations, proposals and certain International Assistance requests.
- **Enhanced follow-up** — an interim status under which an inscribed element is placed pending additional information in removal procedures.
- **Intangible Cultural Heritage Repository** — where a removed element may be placed at the end of a removal procedure.
- **Emergency assistance** — International Assistance granted when a State Party cannot overcome on its own circumstances due to calamity, natural disaster, armed conflict, serious epidemic or another natural or human event with severe consequences for the heritage and its bearers.
- **Free, prior and informed consent** — the consent required from communities, groups and, where applicable, individuals for nominations, extensions, reductions, transfers and awareness-raising concerning their heritage.
- **Preparatory assistance** — International Assistance for elaborating nomination files, proposals, transfer requests and extended/reduced nominations, requested via Form ICH-05 by 31 March.
- **Masterpieces of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity** — items proclaimed under the earlier UNESCO programme, automatically incorporated into the Representative List under Article 31.
- **commercial use** — the sale of goods or services bearing the Convention's emblem chiefly for profit, requiring express written authorization by the Director-General under a specific contractual arrangement (para. 142)
- **patronage** — authorization to use the Convention's emblem for activities such as performances, publications, congresses, prizes and events, granted on the criteria of Impact and Reliability (paras. 137–138)
- **Impact (patronage criterion)** — use may be granted only for exceptional activities likely to have a real impact on safeguarding ICH and significantly enhance the Convention's visibility (para. 138(a)(i))
- **Reliability (patronage criterion)** — adequate assurance concerning those in charge (experience, reputation, references, legal and financial guarantees) and the activity's political, legal, financial and technical feasibility (para. 138(a)(ii))
- **States Parties present and voting** — States Parties casting an affirmative or negative vote; those abstaining are considered as not voting (Rule 34, General Assembly; Rule 38, Committee)
- **amendment** — a motion that merely adds to, deletes from or revises part of a proposal (Rules 33.3 and 25.3)
- **Special Account** — the account created under UNESCO's Financial Regulations to operate the Fund for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage established by Article 25 of the Convention (Art. 1)
- **reserve fund** — the fund within the Special Account established to meet requests for assistance in cases of extreme urgency, as foreseen in Articles 17.3 and 22.2 of the Convention (Art. 7)
- **clearing house** — the Secretariat's function of collecting, exchanging and disseminating information on intangible cultural heritage through databases, an information management system and a website (para. 123(a))
- **Ethical Principles for Safeguarding Intangible Cultural Heritage** — the twelve aspirational principles adopted in 2015, spelling out good practice for anyone affecting ICH and meant as groundwork for locally and sectorally adapted codes of ethics.
- **Free, prior, sustained and informed consent** — the standard required before any interaction with, research on, or awareness raising about the ICH of communities, groups and individuals.
- **Community-based needs identification** — affected communities' own process of pinpointing and meeting immediate safeguarding needs and drawing on their ICH to soften an emergency's immediate effects.
- **Emergency** — armed conflict or disaster arising from natural and human-induced hazards ('natural disasters') that threatens ICH's transmission and viability.
- **Emergency management cycle** — the three phases — preparedness, response and recovery — which vary in length and may overlap.
- **Overall Results Framework** — the 2018 tool for gauging the Convention's impact through impacts, outcomes, eight thematic areas, twenty-six core indicators and eighty-six assessment factors.
- **Core indicators** — the twenty-six indicators, grouped by thematic area, through which impact is measured; indicators 23 and 26 are tracked only at the global level.
- **Assessment factors** — the eighty-six factors, arranged by thematic area, against which the core indicators are assessed.
- **"Whether or not inscribed"** — shorthand for inscription on the List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding or, alternatively, the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
- **"Inclusive" / "on an inclusive basis"** — reaching every sector and stratum of society — indigenous peoples; migrants, immigrants and refugees; people of all ages and genders; persons with disabilities; and members of vulnerable groups.
- **Practitioners and bearers** — members of communities, groups and individuals who play a particular role with respect to their ICH; used in some assessment factors and Operational Directives in place of the Convention's usual phrasing.
- **Inclusive social development** — as defined in Chapter VI of the Operational Directives — food security, health care, gender equality, access to clean, safe water and its sustainable use (quality education sits under indicator 12).
- **Inclusive economic development** — as defined in Chapter VI of the Operational Directives — sustainable livelihoods and income generation, decent and productive employment, and tourism's effects on ICH safeguarding and the reverse.
- **Places of memory** — cultural and natural spaces, and associated locations, needed for expressing ICH, to which communities' access must be guaranteed.
