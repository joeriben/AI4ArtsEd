# A Friendly Face: Do Text-to-Image Systems Rely on Stereotypes when the Input is Under-Specified?

## PROFIL

Written by Kathleen C. Fraser, Svetlina Kiritchenko, and Isar Nejadgholi of the National Research Council Canada, this scholarly article investigates whether text-to-image systems produce images with stereotypical demographic characteristics when prompted with under-specified but socially salient trait words. The authors invert the usual approach of stereotype research in social psychology: instead of giving a demographic group and asking for associated traits, they use a trait (e.g., "an emotional person") as the prompt and observe the skin colour, gender, and age of the resulting images. They test DALL-E 2, Midjourney, and Stable Diffusion, and find that while not all traits generate stereotypical images, each model shows idiosyncratic biases, including an intersectional bias in all three systems associating the adjective "poor" with darker-skinned males. The study is grounded in the ABC Model of social cognition (Koch et al. 2016), with its three dimensions of Agency, Beliefs, and Communion broken into 16 polar traits, and adopts the frameworks of Hutchinson, Baldridge, and Prabhakaran (2022) on ambiguity and under-specification ("Ambiguity In, Ambiguity Out" and "Ambiguity In, Diversity Out"). It builds on prior work on bias in computer vision (Buolamwini and Gebru 2018; Mitchell et al. 2020; van Miltenburg 2016; Agarwal et al. 2021; Hendricks et al. 2018; Raji et al. 2020) and on multi-modal bias (Wolfe and Caliskan 2022; Cho, Zala, and Bansal 2022), and draws on survey-based stereotype literature (Fiske et al. 2002; Nicolas, Bai, and Fiske 2022; Cao et al. 2022). The paper contains a theoretical section, related work, hypotheses, a methodology (408 images per model, annotated by three annotators on gender, skin colour, and age), results with inter-annotator agreement, AIAO rates, baselines, per-dimension and intersectional analyses, a discussion offering three explanatory factors (training-data reporting bias, intended use case, and normative design choices), conclusions with future work, and an appendix with ethical considerations, system details, disaggregated results, and annotator instructions. The authors argue that each system demonstrated room for improvement, flag age-related bias and the encoded relationships between race, gender, and economic status as research areas, and leave open the tension between freedom of expression and harm reduction. The article is usable in arts education contexts addressing AI-generated imagery, bias and representation in visual culture, and the ethics of generative tools in art practice.

## WISSEN

### Purpose and Research Question (p. 1)

This paper by Kathleen C. Fraser, Svetlana Kiritchenko, and Isar Nejadgholi (National Research Council Canada) investigates whether text-to-image systems produce images with stereotypical demographic characteristics when prompted with under-specified but socially salient trait words. The authors invert the usual approach of stereotype research in social psychology: instead of giving a demographic group and asking for associated traits, they use a trait (e.g., "an emotional person") as the prompt and observe the demographics (skin colour, gender, age) of the resulting images. Their research question: if a model is prompted to generate a person with particular social traits, will the images show the stereotypical demographic characteristics associated with those traits? They test three popular systems—DALL-E 2, Midjourney, and Stable Diffusion—and find that while not all traits generate stereotypical images, each model shows idiosyncratic biases, including an intersectional bias in all three systems associating the adjective "poor" with darker-skinned males.

### Theoretical Grounding: The ABC Model (p. 1)

The study is grounded in the ABC Model of social cognition (Koch et al. 2016), which proposes three dimensions of social judgement: Agency (A), Beliefs (B), and Communion (C), broken down into 16 polar traits. Agency includes powerful vs. powerless, high-status vs. low-status, dominated vs. dominating, poor vs. wealthy, meek vs. confident, passive vs. competitive. Beliefs include religious vs. science-oriented, conventional vs. alternative, conservative vs. liberal, traditional vs. modern. Communion includes untrustworthy vs. trustworthy, dishonest vs. sincere, unfriendly vs. friendly, threatening vs. benevolent, unpleasant vs. likable, egoistic vs. altruistic. The model holds that stereotypes of social groups can be located in this three-dimensional space (e.g., tech entrepreneurs stereotyped as wealthy, science-oriented, and greedy). The authors note that trait adjectives are visually under-specified—there is no fixed appearance for "a powerful person"—and it is precisely this under-specificity that can produce biased outputs, since the model must "fill in the blanks" with cultural knowledge from its training data.

### Related Work (p. 2)

The authors situate their study within prior work on bias in computer vision: unbalanced class distributions in training data (Buolamwini and Gebru 2018 on facial recognition error rates for darker-skinned females); dataset diversity metrics (Mitchell et al. 2020); annotation biases (van Miltenburg 2016; Misra et al. 2016); model-design effects on fairness (Agarwal et al. 2021; Hendricks et al. 2018 on image captioning exaggerating stereotypical cues); and auditing frameworks for commercial face recognition (Raji et al. 2020). Recent multi-modal bias work includes Wolfe and Caliskan (2022) on racial biases in "American" identity, and Cho, Zala, and Bansal (2022) on occupation–race/gender stereotypes. They adopt the frameworks of Hutchinson, Baldridge, and Prabhakaran (2022), who identified ambiguity and under-specification as root causes of bias in text-to-image systems and proposed two approaches: "Ambiguity In, Ambiguity Out" (AIAO), where the model generates ambiguous images for under-specified concepts, and "Ambiguity In, Diversity Out" (AIDO), where the model generates a maximally diverse set of images covering the space of possibilities.

### Hypotheses (p. 2)

Drawing on survey-based stereotype literature (Koch et al. 2016; Fiske et al. 2002; Nicolas, Bai, and Fiske 2022; Cao et al. 2022), the authors hypothesize that, for under-specified prompts, the models will default to learned social stereotypes:

- High-agency words will generate images of people with lighter skin, older age, and male gender; low-agency words will generate darker skin, younger age, and female gender.
- High-belief (progressive) words will generate younger and lighter-skinned people; low-belief (conservative) words will generate older and darker-skinned people.
- High-communion words will generate lighter skin, older age, and female gender; low-communion words will generate darker skin, younger age, and male gender.

### Methodology (p. 2–4)

**Systems.** Three systems were evaluated, all generating images from text prompts and trained on millions/billions of web-scraped text-image pairs; all images were generated in October 2022. DALL-E 2 (OpenAI, beta July 2022) has been explicitly extended to diversify output for under-specified person prompts. Midjourney (independent lab, beta July 2022; version v3 used) is a social app used via Discord; its details are not public. Stable Diffusion (Stability AI, August 2022, v1.5 via DreamStudio API) is a latent diffusion model trained on LAION-Aesthetics.

**Prompt construction.** The basic prompt was "portrait of a 〈adjective〉 person." Some original ABC adjectives were replaced for reliability: warm, cold, and repellent became friendly, unfriendly, and unpleasant (too semantically ambiguous); the low-frequency words unconfident and unassertive became meek and passive. Model-specific adaptations: for DALL-E, the prompt worked as written (non-photorealistic results were rerun); for Midjourney, the flag --testp plus the keywords "Kodak Portra 400" (a colour film stock) produced colour, photorealistic results; the same keywords were added for Stable Diffusion to avoid painterly, abstract outputs.

**Volume.** 24 images per trait (12 per pole), plus 24 baseline images per model using "portrait of a person" with no adjective—408 images per model in total.

**Annotation.** Each image was annotated by three annotators (the authors) on gender, skin colour, and age, each with four possible values: gender (male, female, gender neutral, no gender information); skin colour (darker, lighter, in-between, no information); age (older, younger, in-between, no information). Following Buolamwini and Gebru (2018), skin colour rather than race/ethnicity was used as the more objective measure. Categorical annotations were converted to numerical values and averaged equally across annotators (e.g., two "darker" +1 ratings and one "in-between" 0 yields 0.67). The authors stress they annotated *perceived* characteristics of AI-generated images, not real people, and did not make annotations public to avoid facilitating categorization of real individuals.

### Results (p. 4–6)

**Inter-annotator agreement** (Cohen's Kappa): gender highest (Midjourney 0.84, DALL-E 0.92, Stable Diffusion 0.75), skin colour lowest (0.57, 0.64, 0.54), age intermediate (0.76, 0.68, 0.54). Skin colour was harder due to the full spectrum and varying lighting; Stable Diffusion's lower values reflect its lower photorealism.

**AIAO rates.** DALL-E produced almost no ambiguous images (gender 0.00, skin colour 0.03, age 0.04); Midjourney 0.06/0.06/0.08; Stable Diffusion 0.06/0.01/0.11. Ambiguity was created by facing subjects away, obscuring faces, non-realistic skin colours, or blurring. These images were removed and the analysis focused on AIDO.

**Baselines.** For "portrait of a person": Midjourney and Stable Diffusion tended to produce female subjects, DALL-E males; all three favoured lighter skin tones (DALL-E less so); all three strongly favoured younger-looking subjects.

**ABC dimension results** (significant differences at p < 0.05, Mann-Whitney U-Test; the authors distinguish *stereotype direction*—a group associated with the positive or negative pole of a trait—from *representativeness*—a group highly associated with a trait regardless of polarity, following Nicolas, Bai, and Fiske 2022):

- *Gender:* Midjourney showed significant differences in Agency (high-agency → male, e.g., high-status, dominating; low-agency → female, e.g., powerless) and Communion (low-communion → male, e.g., dishonest, unpleasant; friendly and likable → female). DALL-E showed no significant directional difference but over-represented males across all dimensions. Stable Diffusion showed low-communion → male and general male over-representation.
- *Skin colour:* Midjourney showed progressive Beliefs associated with lighter skin (driven by science-oriented vs. religious and modern vs. traditional), with lighter skin over-represented overall. DALL-E showed more equal representation, but significantly associated low-communion words (untrustworthy, threatening, unpleasant) with lighter skin—contradicting the hypothesis. Stable Diffusion over-represented lighter skin in all dimensions, with significant associations of progressive Beliefs and low Communion (dishonest, threatening) with lighter skin.
- *Age:* Midjourney significantly associated low-agency and high-communion words with younger people (the latter contrary to hypothesis). DALL-E associated the positive poles of all three dimensions with younger age (all significant). Stable Diffusion showed the same trend, significant only for Beliefs, with a highly skewed representativeness toward younger age. Across all three systems, likable was strongly associated with younger age and unpleasant with older age.

**Intersectional results.** In Midjourney, the only strong tendency to generate darker-skinned males was for the adjective poor (slight tendencies: competitive → darker-skinned male athletes; traditional → darker-skinned women in "traditional" dress). DALL-E generated a range of skin colours but mostly males (alternative → lighter-skinned women; traditional → darker-skinned women); poor again generated darker-skinned males. Stable Diffusion likewise generated darker-skinned males mostly for poor, with benevolent and sincere near the skin-colour midpoint and powerful and likable associated with lighter-skinned women. For age vs. skin colour, poor was again anomalous—closest to "oldest and darkest-skinned" for DALL-E and Stable Diffusion. For age vs. gender, Midjourney showed a linear negative trend (older males / younger females, no traits for older females, only competitive for younger males); DALL-E and Stable Diffusion trended toward younger males.

### Summary and Discussion (p. 6–7)

Many significant differences confirmed the hypotheses (high-Agency and low-Communion words → more men; Midjourney's low-Agency → younger age; progressive Beliefs → lighter skin in Midjourney and Stable Diffusion, driven by modern-traditional and science-oriented-religious, as also reported by Cao et al. 2022). Contrary to hypotheses, lighter skin was associated with low-communion adjectives in DALL-E and Stable Diffusion, and high-communion was unexpectedly associated with younger age; combined with the general preference for younger faces, age-based bias appears to need developer attention. The lack of representation of darker-skinned females is striking, echoing prior scholarship on intersectional bias in AI (D'ignazio and Klein 2020), and the association of poverty with dark-skinned males in all three systems warrants further investigation. The authors offer three explanatory factors: (1) training data scraped from the web, where human reporting bias means stereotype-consistent "default" traits go unmentioned in captions (e.g., van Miltenburg 2016 found the race of babies in Flickr30k is only mentioned when the baby is Black or Asian—white babies are assumed default); (2) intended use case—Midjourney and Stable Diffusion are optimized for "aesthetically-pleasing" AI artwork, and Western art has historically favoured the female form, youth, and beauty (Nead 2002); (3) normative design choices—OpenAI actively works to reduce bias in DALL-E 2 (visible as fewer extreme disparities, especially in skin colour), while Stable Diffusion rejects content filtering on grounds of user freedom, leaving responsibility to users. The tension between freedom of expression and harm reduction remains open and possibly unsolvable.

### Conclusion and Future Work (p. 7–8)

Although the systems showed different stereotypical biases, each demonstrated room for improvement; age-related bias is flagged as a fruitful research area, as are the encoded relationships between race, gender, and economic status. Results are preliminary due to small sample size and limited prompt variety; future work should confirm or dis-confirm biases with bigger samples and more varied prompts. Key open questions include whether bias originates in training data, model architecture, or model parameters (which would inform de-biasing at training time), and mitigating bias at inference time through prompt engineering—e.g., Bansal et al. (2022) report that adding phrases like "irrespective of gender" encourages gender-diverse outputs.

### Appendix (p. 10–17)

The appendix covers ethical considerations (annotating perceived characteristics of AI-generated, not real, people; the three annotators were female, in their 30s–40s, lighter-to-medium skin toned, highly educated in Western universities, raised in different world regions); further system details (DALL-E 2's two-level architecture of a prior converting captions to CLIP embeddings plus a diffusion-based decoder, trained on 250 million text-image pairs for the research version; Stable Diffusion trained on 2.3B pairs from laion2B-en and 170M from laion-high-resolution, subsets of LAION 5B's 5.85 billion pairs, then LAION-Aesthetics, a 600M subset); disaggregated per-trait results for gender, skin colour, and age; intersectional scatter plots (skin colour × age, gender × age); sample images for the poor/wealthy prompts; and full annotator instructions (e.g., "younger" = perceived as under 30, "older" = perceived as over 60).

## GLOSSAR

- **ABC Model** — A social cognition model (Koch et al. 2016) proposing three dimensions of social judgement—Agency, Beliefs, Communion—broken into 16 polar traits, in which group stereotypes can be located.
- **Agency (A)** — ABC dimension covering traits such as powerful/powerless, high-status/low-status, dominating/dominated, wealthy/poor, confident/meek, competitive/passive.
- **Beliefs (B)** — ABC dimension running from progressive to conservative, covering religious/science-oriented, conventional/alternative, conservative/liberal, traditional/modern.
- **Communion (C)** — ABC dimension covering warmth-related traits such as trustworthy/untrustworthy, sincere/dishonest, friendly/unfriendly, benevolent/threatening, likable/unpleasant, altruistic/egoistic.
- **AIAO (Ambiguity In, Ambiguity Out)** — Strategy in which a text-to-image model generates ambiguous or under-specified images when the input concept is under-specified.
- **AIDO (Ambiguity In, Diversity Out)** — Strategy in which the model generates a maximally diverse set of images covering the space of possibilities for an under-specified concept.
- **Stereotype direction** — The association of a group with the positive or negative pole of a given trait (Nicolas, Bai, and Fiske 2022).
- **Representativeness** — A group being highly associated with a given trait regardless of polarity (Nicolas, Bai, and Fiske 2022).
- **Under-specification** — The property of prompts that do not specify visual details (e.g., demographic characteristics), forcing the model to "fill in the blanks" from learned cultural knowledge.
- **Intersectional bias** — Bias arising at the intersection of multiple demographic variables, e.g., the association of "poor" with darker-skinned males found in all three systems.
