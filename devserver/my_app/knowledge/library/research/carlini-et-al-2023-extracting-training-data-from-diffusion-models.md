# Extracting Training Data from Diffusion Models

## PROFIL

The paper "Extracting Training Data from Diffusion Models" is written by Nicholas Carlini, Jamie Hayes, Milad Nasr, Matthew Jagielski, Vikash Sehwag, Florian Tramèr, Borja Balle, Daphne Ippolito, and Eric Wallace, affiliated with Google, DeepMind, ETHZ, Princeton, and UC Berkeley, and is addressed to the machine-learning research community working on generative models, privacy, and memorization. Its subject is the privacy of image diffusion models, and its central claim is that models such as Stable Diffusion and Imagen memorize individual training images and emit them at generation time, making diffusion models much less private than prior generative models such as GANs. The paper first defines memorization for images through the notions of "(ℓ, d)-Diffusion Extraction" and "(k, ℓ, d)-Eidetic Memorization", then presents a generate-and-filter attack that extracts over a thousand training examples from Stable Diffusion, Imagen, and models trained on CIFAR-10, covering untargeted extraction, membership inference (loss threshold and LiRA attacks), and inpainting-based reconstruction. It compares diffusion models with GANs trained via BigGAN, MHGAN, and StyleGAN, showing that diffusion models leak more and that attack success grows with model quality. It evaluates defenses — deduplication, differentially-private training (DP-SGD, which caused training to diverge), and canary-based auditing — and closes with recommendations to deduplicate training data, audit privacy risk, adopt privacy-preserving techniques once practical, and temper the expectation that synthetic data gives privacy for free. The work builds on memorization research for language models (Carlini et al.'s extraction attacks), on membership-inference attacks (Yeom et al.; LiRA), and on GAN replication studies, while setting itself against claims that diffusion models "do not find overfitting to be an issue" or could "protect the privacy of real images", and against concurrent work by Wu et al., Hu et al., and Somepalli et al. by using a more restrictive, worst-case notion of memorization across a wider range of models. It is usable in arts education wherever generative AI, dataset ethics, copyright, and the provenance of AI-produced images are taught, particularly in media arts, digital art, and design curricula addressing the relation between training data and generated output.

## WISSEN

### Purpose and Thesis (p. 1–3)

This paper, "Extracting Training Data from Diffusion Models" by Nicholas Carlini, Jamie Hayes, Milad Nasr, Matthew Jagielski, Vikash Sehwag, Florian Tramèr, Borja Balle, Daphne Ippolito, and Eric Wallace (affiliated with Google, DeepMind, ETHZ, Princeton, and UC Berkeley), demonstrates that image diffusion models such as DALL-E 2, Imagen, and Stable Diffusion memorize individual images from their training data and emit them at generation time. Using a generate-and-filter pipeline, the authors extract over a thousand training examples from state-of-the-art models, ranging from photographs of individual people to trademarked company logos. They further train hundreds of diffusion models in controlled settings to analyze how modeling and data decisions affect privacy. Their overall conclusion is that diffusion models are much less private than prior generative models such as GANs, and that mitigating these vulnerabilities may require new advances in privacy-preserving training.

The work is motivated by two concerns: (1) privacy risks — diffusion models trained on web-scraped data pose privacy and copyright risks similar to those of language models, and some researchers have even suggested diffusion models could "protect the privacy of real images" by generating synthetic data, a claim this paper undermines; and (2) understanding generalization — whether impressive generative results arise from truly novel generations or from direct copying and remixing of training data. Notably, the Imagen authors had stated they "do not find overfitting to be an issue," yet this paper shows that model memorizes individual examples.

### Background and Threat Model (p. 2–3)

Diffusion models are image denoisers: during training, noise is added to a clean image and the model learns to predict the noise that was added; generation proceeds by iteratively removing noise from a random vector. Models may be class-conditional (conditioned on a class label) or text-conditioned (conditioned on a text prompt via an encoder such as CLIP).

The threat model considers an adversary interacting with a diffusion model to extract training images. Two adversary capabilities are defined: a black-box adversary who can query the generator (with arbitrary prompts for conditional models) but cannot control internal randomness; and a white-box adversary with full access to the model, who can control randomness and denoise arbitrary inputs. Three adversarial goals are enumerated, from strongest to weakest: (1) data extraction — recovering an image from the training set; (2) data reconstruction — recovering a full image from partial knowledge (analogous to attribute inference); (3) membership inference — determining whether a given image was in the training set.

An ethics section notes the authors studied only publicly available data (LAION, CIFAR-10), showed only images of public figures, and shared an advance copy of the paper with the authors of the large-scale models studied.

### Defining Image Memorization (p. 4)

Because verbatim definitions of memorization from language-model research do not suit high-resolution images, the authors propose approximate definitions based on image similarity:

- Definition 1, "(ℓ, d)-Diffusion Extraction": an example x is extractable if an efficient algorithm (not receiving x as input) produces an output within distance d of x under distance function ℓ. The paper uses normalized Euclidean ℓ2 distance.
- Definition 2, "(k, ℓ, d)-Eidetic Memorization": an example is eidetically memorized if it is extractable and there are at most k near-duplicates of it in the dataset. Small k means memorization is likely problematic; larger k might be expected but could still be problematic (e.g., copyrighted data).

The definition is intentionally conservative: a recognizable generated image of Barack Obama would not count as memorized because it matches no particular training image closely — yet such generation could still cause privacy harms.

### Extracting Data from Stable Diffusion (p. 4–6)

Stable Diffusion is an 890-million-parameter text-conditioned model trained on 160 million images. The attack proceeds in two steps:

1. Generate many examples using the model with prompts derived from highly duplicated training images (duplication is targeted because duplicated examples are orders of magnitude more likely to be memorized). Near-duplicates are found by comparing CLIP embeddings (increasing efficiency by over 1500×), since all-pairs pixel comparison of 160 million images is intractable. 500 candidate images are generated per prompt.
2. Perform membership inference to flag memorized generations: because novel generations vary with the random seed while memorized ones do not, the authors build a graph over the 500 generations per prompt, connect near-identical images, and predict memorization if the largest clique has at least 10 members. Distance is a modified ℓ2 measure computed over 16 non-overlapping 128×128 tiles (taking the maximum tile distance), which avoids spurious matches such as shared gray backgrounds.

Results: from the 350,000 most-duplicated prompts (175 million generated images total), 94 images are (ℓ2, 0.15)-extracted; manual annotation of the top-1000 adds 13 more, for a total of 109 near-copies. The attack is exceptionally precise: 50 memorized images can be identified with 0 false positives out of 175 million generations. Eidetic memorization occurs mostly for images duplicated at least k = 100 times (though this is an upper bound given the methodology). Qualitatively, 58% of extracted images are photographs of recognizable people, 17% products for sale, 14% logos/posters; 35% carry an explicit non-permissive copyright notice, and 61% have no explicit notice but may fall under general website copyright.

### Extracting Data from Imagen and Outlier Examples (p. 7)

Applying the same procedure to Imagen (a 2-billion-parameter model), focusing on the top-1000 most duplicated prompts with 500 generations each, the authors identify 23 memorized training examples — a significantly higher rate than Stable Diffusion, showing memorization depends on model size, training time, and dataset size.

To attack non-duplicated ("low k") examples, the authors target outlier images, scoring each training image's "outlierness" as the average CLIP-embedding distance to its 1000 nearest neighbors. Imagen memorized and regurgitated 3 of the 500 most out-of-distribution images (each unique in training), while the same approach found no memorization in Stable Diffusion even for the 10,000 most-outlier samples. Imagen thus appears less private than Stable Diffusion, likely due to higher model capacity, more training iterations, and a smaller dataset.

### Controlled Experiments on CIFAR-10: Extraction and Membership Inference (p. 7–11)

The authors train 16 diffusion models on random halves of CIFAR-10 (class-conditional models reaching FID below 3.5, unconditional models below 4) and run three attack types.

Untargeted extraction (p. 8–9): generating 2^16 images per model (2^20 total), a fixed ℓ2 threshold fails (it flags only blue skies and green landscapes), so the authors define an image as extracted if its ℓ2 distance to the nearest training image is abnormally low compared to that image's 50 nearest training neighbors (using a ratio with exponent a = 0.5). This yields 1,280 unique extracted images — 2.5% of the dataset.

Membership inference (p. 9–11): two attacks are used — the loss threshold attack (Yeom et al.) and LiRA (Carlini et al.), which trains shadow models and compares per-example loss distributions. Because diffusion loss depends on the noise timestep, the authors find the optimal attack window is t ∈ [50, 300] — a "Goldilocks zone" where the noised image is neither too close to the original nor too close to pure noise. At t = 100, LiRA achieves over 70% true positive rate (TPR) at 1% false positive rate (FPR), far worse than classifiers (under 20% TPR at 1% FPR). Averaging the loss over multiple noise samples and over augmentations (horizontal flips) further strengthens the attack: at 0.1% FPR, TPR increases sixfold from 7% to 44%. The easiest-to-attack images are visually out-of-distribution outliers; the hardest are duplicated images (where membership is ill-defined). Critically, attack success grows with model quality: as FID decreases, TPR at 1% FPR rises from 7% to nearly 100%, suggesting stronger future models will be even less private.

Inpainting attacks (p. 11–12): as a targeted attack, the authors mask the left half of an image and use the model to inpaint it, repeating 5,000 times and selecting the top-10 reconstructions by a membership-inference score (main-model loss at timestep 100 divided by a shadow model's loss). Reconstructions are substantially closer to the target when the image was in training, showing partial knowledge of an image substantially improves extraction.

### Comparison with GANs (p. 12–13)

Diffusion models are explicitly trained to reconstruct training data, whereas GAN generators only receive indirect information through the discriminator's gradients. The authors develop membership inference attacks for GANs (using the discriminator's loss with the loss threshold and LiRA attacks, training 256 GANs on random 50% splits of CIFAR-10, using BigGAN, MHGAN, and StyleGAN via the StudioGAN framework). Diffusion models leak more: 50% TPR at 0.1% FPR versus under 30% for GANs.

For data extraction from off-the-shelf pretrained models (1 million unconditional generations each), diffusion models memorize more than GANs at similar quality: OpenAI-DDPM extracts 301 images at FID 2.9, DDPM extracts 232 at FID 3.2, versus StyleGAN-ADA's 150 at FID 2.9, DiffBigGAN's 57 at FID 4.6, E2GAN's 95 at FID 11.3, NDA's 70 at FID 12.6, and WGAN-ALP's 49 at FID 13.0. Better models (lower FID) memorize more. Strikingly, diffusion models and GANs memorize many of the same images: 244 images are memorized in common between a diffusion model (1,280 memorized) and a StyleGAN (361 memorized), versus about 10 expected by chance (p < 10^-261), showing some images are inherently less private than others.

### Defenses and Recommendations (p. 14–15)

- Deduplication: deduplicating CIFAR-10 (removing 5,275 of 50,000 images with similarity above 0.85 using the imagededup tool) reduces extracted images from 1,280 to 986 — helpful but insufficient; the authors encourage more advanced deduplication (e.g., ℓ2 or CLIP similarity) in future datasets.
- Differentially-private training (DP-SGD): applying DP-SGD caused training to consistently diverge, even at high privacy budgets (ε around 50); even gradient clipping or noising alone broke training. New advances in privacy-preserving training may be required.
- Auditing with canaries: inserting randomly generated noise-image canaries at different duplication rates and measuring exposure shows maximum exposure (10) can be reached after inserting a canary only twice — a lightweight alternative to expensive shadow-model attacks.

### Related Work, Conclusion, and Practical Consequences (p. 15)

The paper situates itself relative to work on memorization in language models (e.g., Carlini et al.'s extraction attacks), memorization in image generation (GAN replication, metrics for training-data similarity), and concurrent work on diffusion-model privacy (Wu et al., Hu et al. on membership inference; Somepalli et al. on "style copying" — this paper instead uses a more restrictive, worst-case notion of memorization across a wider range of models).

The conclusion raises four practical recommendations: (1) deduplicate training datasets and minimize over-training; (2) use the paper's attack or other auditing techniques to estimate privacy risk; (3) use privacy-preserving techniques once they become practical; (4) temper the heuristic expectation that synthetic data gives privacy for free. The authors also note the difficulty of defining memorization and argue that definitions of overfitting should be broadened to include memorization and privacy metrics.

## GLOSSAR

- **(ℓ, d)-Diffusion Extraction** — an example x is extractable from a diffusion model if an efficient algorithm that does not receive x as input can produce an output within distance d of x under distance function ℓ.
- **(k, ℓ, d)-Eidetic Memorization** — an example is eidetically memorized if it is extractable and there are at most k near-duplicates of it in the training dataset.
- **Data extraction** — an attack recovering an image from the training set that is almost identical to some training example.
- **Data reconstruction** — an attack where an adversary with partial knowledge of a training image (e.g., a subset of it) aims to recover the full image.
- **Membership inference** — an attack inferring whether a given image was in the training set.
- **Black-box adversary** — an adversary who can query the generator (with arbitrary prompts for conditional models) but cannot control its internal randomness.
- **White-box adversary** — an adversary with full access to the model who can control randomness and denoise arbitrary inputs.
- **Loss threshold attack** — a membership inference attack that reports "member" when an example's loss falls below a chosen threshold.
- **LiRA (Likelihood Ratio Attack)** — a membership inference attack training shadow models and comparing an example's loss to Gaussian distributions fitted to losses from models that did versus did not see the example.
- **Shadow models** — models trained on random subsets of the training dataset, used to calibrate membership inference attacks.
- **Inpainting attack** — the paper's targeted attribute-inference attack that masks part of an image and uses the model to reconstruct the masked region.
- **Canary** — a deliberately inserted training example (here, random noise images) whose post-training exposure measures memorization.
- **Exposure** — a loss-based metric measuring how many bits were learned about inserted canaries compared to a larger pool of non-inserted canaries.
- **Outlierness** — the paper's score for a training image, computed as the average CLIP-embedding distance to its 1,000 nearest neighbors in the training dataset.
- **Goldilocks zone** — the timestep range t ∈ [50, 300] where membership inference is most effective — the noised image is neither too similar to the original nor too similar to pure noise.
