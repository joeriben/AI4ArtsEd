## 5. Where a newcomer stumbles

- The asterisk convention: the base prompt must contain "*" and the variations go into the asterisk; the label says so, but the preselected example hides whether a prompt without "*" would still work.
- Only a counter in the header during the run; nothing says that each model has to be loaded first.
- The columns keep old pictures when the prompt changes; a user who edits the base prompt expects everything to be recomputed, and must look at the counter to see what was.
- Text in the Flux 2 picture ("Yoruba First Meal Othalion…"): nothing on the surface says that a model writes into pictures by itself.
- The note's numbers (102 of 102, 44 %) read like results of this surface.

## 6. What the help text should tell a user

The Help panel's own text, which opens on arrival, is quoted in 1: it says that the models receive the base prompt once on its own and beside it with a culture or a country named in it, that the rows hold the models and the columns the variations, computed with one seed and this exact wording, what the first column and the further columns show, and that a single model is run on five seeds. It should add: the base prompt holds an asterisk, and every line in the variations field replaces it once; the header names the weight file while a model works, and a model that is not loaded yet is loaded before its first picture; changing one line recomputes that column only; the note on the left cites what research found about such defaults, not what this run found. Down a column the grid compares the models on one culture, which the Help text does not say; across a row it shows what the name changes for one model, which it does. And it should say who can tell whether the practice meant is shown: the person who knows it. The variations field takes any own word, the list only appends; the two prefilled names are an example, not the choice.

## 7. Use possibilities, action-oriented

- Write the name of your own region, town, community or language into the variations field (the list only appends; the field takes any word) and judge the pictures by what you know. The base column shows what the model takes as unmarked.
- Before the run, write down in two columns what the prompt says and what you expect without saying it; after the run, tick each line against each picture.
- Read one column top to bottom: three models, one named culture, three pictures. What all three share is the cliché candidate; what only one of them shows is that model’s own default.
- Read one row left to right: one model, the prompt without a name and with three names. Note what the name changed in the picture and what stayed (the plate, the camera angle, the absence of people).
- Write what the models missed into the base prompt by name and run again; only the changed cells are recomputed. Then press Choose Perspective on the base prompt and run the written prompt.
- Choose one model only: the surface then draws five seeds per column. Count in how many of the five a missed element is missing.
- Switch the set from "Current models" to "SD History" and compare how the same practice is drawn across three model generations.

## 8. Teaching scenarios

The instrument puts one question: whether an image model honours cultural knowledge or stands indifferent to it. The knowledge that can answer it is in the room, with the people who know a practice, and not in the model. Every scenario below is a small research project built on that: an expectation written down before the run, a grid with a control column and one seed, a judgement by the person who knows, a repetition, and a written result.

**S1 — My practice, their picture** (formal, any subject in a class of mixed backgrounds, grades 7–10, 90 min). Each pupil or pair writes a base prompt about a practice they know from home (a meal, a greeting, a festival day, a wedding, a game) with the asterisk where the place goes, and writes the name of their own region, town, community or language into the variations field; the list is a help, the own word goes in. Before the run, an expectation card on paper in two columns: what the prompt says, and what I expect without saying it (the method of the CULTURALFRAMES benchmark, which the note cites). Run on three models. Then every expectation is ticked per picture: shown, half shown, missed; a missed explicit expectation is marked. A partner who does not know the practice judges the same column, and the two cards are compared. The base column shows what each model takes as the unmarked case. Sequence: 15 min prompt and card; 25 min run; 25 min ticking; 25 min partner swap and plenary: what the models got right stood in the prompt; what they missed, nobody in the model knew, and only someone in the room could tell; and what such a picture does when it stands in a worksheet or a textbook before people who do not know the practice. Subject link: cultural knowledge as the basis of judgement; the judgement is situated, and that is the finding, not a flaw. Uncertainty: I ran only the prefilled example; "Franconian" gave three generic hearty breakfasts without anything a Franconian would recognise as theirs, "Japanese" twice the rice, soup and fish tray and once sushi.

**S2 — Saying it, and insisting** (formal, language teaching or ethics, grades 9–13, 90 min, after S1 or with its grid). The missed explicit elements of a practice are written into the base prompt by name (the bread, the dish, the fire, the people who must be there), and the series is run again; only the changed cells are recomputed. Then a second attempt: Choose Perspective on the base prompt, a perspective picked, the written prompt run. Per model a record: what improved after the first rewrite, what after the perspective, what stayed wrong in both. Sequence: 15 min rewrite; 20 min run; 15 min perspective and run; 25 min record; 15 min plenary on what wording can and cannot reach. Subject link: the reach and the limit of the prompt; Prompt Interception as worked formulation. Uncertainty: I did not press Choose Perspective on this surface; in S1's grid the Yoruba column changed the dishes with the name alone, the Franconian column did not.

**S3 — Slip or system?** (formal, computer science, mathematics or social studies, grades 9–12, 45 min). One model only; the surface then draws five seeds per column, as the Help text says. For one named practice the class takes one missed element from S1's card and counts in how many of the five pictures it is missing; the count, not one picture, is the result. The note's second study re-ran its prompts and counted a fault only where it recurred. Sequence: 10 min element and prediction; 15 min run; 20 min counting and comparison with the prediction. Subject link: repetition, distribution against anecdote. Uncertainty: I did not run the single-model mode; the five seeds are the Help text's statement.

**S4 — Whose everyday is the unmarked one** (formal, social studies or geography, grades 8–11, 60 min, runs prepared by the teacher). Five base prompts (a meal, a house, a wedding, a school morning, a street) with two or three variations each, run beforehand on three models. The class reads the base columns only: which place, which people, which clothing count as obvious to the models, and who in the room recognises that everyday as theirs. Then the note's first study: "Seasons in January" gave northern-hemisphere winter in 102 pictures out of 102; the class replicates it in miniature with "a season in January in *" and three places on three continents, the base column as the unmarked case. Last, two questions on the board. First: who chose the data and released the model. The hover card of each model chip names maker and year (for the models here Stability AI and Black Forest Labs), and the note's studies say what the training data hold: where a region was rare, the model fills in what was frequent; to train on such data and to release the model was the makers' decision, and the everyday of the majority in the data becomes the standard picture. Second: whom the standard serves once an institution uses the model for its pictures, a publisher for a textbook, a school for a worksheet, a tourism office for a poster. On one side those who find their everyday in the standard picture, on the other those whose practice arrives as the exotic variant or as the cliché; the class names for its own five prompts who stands where. Sequence: 15 min base columns; 15 min replication; 15 min comparison of the class's count with the study's; 15 min the two questions. Subject link: the default as the outcome of decisions (which data, whose release, which use), not a fact of nature; a published finding checked with own runs; who gains and who loses by a standard picture. Uncertainty: I did not run the replication prompt; the chips' hover card with maker and year was not opened here, it stands in the build and in the server's model register.

**S5 — Three model generations, one practice** (non-formal, media workshop, 2 h, age 13+). Participants keep one practice they know and switch the set from "Current models" to "SD History" (SD 1.5, SDXL, SD 3.5 Large), run, and judge each generation's column with the card from S1: did three years of training change what the model shows of the practice, or only how sharply it draws it (resolution, hands, text in the picture)? They collect every picture in which a model wrote text by itself, as Flux 2 did here with "Yoruba First Meal Othalion 7uth of de poay", and ask what a caption that is not words says about the data a model learned from. Subject link: what more training changed and what it did not; text–image pairs as the data. Uncertainty: I did not run the SD History set; the pseudo-text appeared in one picture of twelve.
