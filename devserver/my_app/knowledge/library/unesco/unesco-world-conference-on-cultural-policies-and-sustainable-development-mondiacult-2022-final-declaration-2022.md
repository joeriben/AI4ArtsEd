# UNESCO World Conference on Cultural Policies and Sustainable Development (MONDIACULT 2022): Final Declaration

## PROFIL

- Authored by UNESCO, the text is the Final Declaration adopted by the Ministers of Culture of UNESCO's Member States, gathered in Mexico City from 28 to 30 September 2022 at the World Conference on Cultural Policies and Sustainable Development (MONDIACULT 2022), forty years after the 1982 MONDIACULT Conference in Mexico and twenty-four years after the 1998 Stockholm Conference.

- Its subject is the future of cultural policies; its claim is that culture is a global public good whose transformative impact enables and drives sustainable development, and it sets a forward-looking agenda reaffirming international commitment in the face of contemporary crises in multicultural societies.

- It contains a preamble reaffirming the principles and conceptual advances of the 1982 and 1998 conferences — including the definition of culture and the acknowledgement of cultural diversity as necessary for humankind — expressing concern over crises linked to climate change, conflicts, pandemics and the digital divide, and acknowledging UNESCO's standard-setting architecture from the 1952 Universal Copyright Convention to the 2005 Convention on the Protection and Promotion of the Diversity of Cultural Expressions.

- A call to action sets out the Ministers' commitments: protecting cultural diversity as a foundational principle; fostering human and cultural rights, including artistic freedom, the status of artists and linguistic diversity; anchoring culture in public policies at all levels; strengthening financing; enhancing synergies between culture and education, including arts education, digital literacy, TVET in the cultural sector and the educational role of museums, libraries and cultural institutions, with support for a revised UNESCO framework on culture and arts education; protecting heritage in crisis; integrating culture into climate action; fighting illicit trafficking; pursuing return and restitution of cultural property; and fostering sustainable cultural and creative sectors in the digital transformation.

- Closing strategic directions request UNESCO to work towards joint implementation: anchoring culture as a specific goal in the development agenda beyond 2030, producing a quadrennial Global Report on Cultural Policies, convening a World Forum on Cultural Policies every four years from 2025, and developing a plan with concrete actions and timeframe within UNESCO's Medium-Term Strategy and Programme and Budget.

- It builds on the entire body of UNESCO Culture Conventions and Recommendations, the 1980 Recommendation concerning the Status of the Artist, the 2017 Operational Guidelines on the Digital Environment, the 2021 Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence, and cooperation with bodies such as INTERPOL, UNIDROIT, ICOM, WIPO, WTO and UNCTAD.

- For arts education it is usable where policy frameworks, curricula, lifelong learning, cultural rights, the status and livelihoods of artists and cultural professionals, and the systemic integration of culture into formal, informal and non-formal education are at stake.

## WISSEN

### Purpose and Context (p. 1)

The Ministers of Culture of UNESCO's Member States, gathered in Mexico City from 28 to 30 September 2022 at the World Conference on Cultural Policies and Sustainable Development (MONDIACULT 2022), adopt this Final Declaration. The conference marks 40 years since the historic 1982 MONDIACULT Conference in Mexico and 24 years since the 1998 Stockholm Conference, both convened by UNESCO. The Declaration's purpose is to share a vision of the future of cultural policies, reaffirm international commitment in the face of urgent contemporary challenges in multicultural societies, and set a forward-looking agenda that harnesses culture's transformative impact for sustainable development.

### Preamble: Principles, Concerns and Acknowledgements (p. 1–3)

The preamble reaffirms the fundamental principles and conceptual advances of the 1982 and 1998 conferences, including the definition of culture as a "set of distinctive spiritual, material, intellectual and emotional features that characterize a society or social group, [which] includes not only arts and letters, but also modes of life, the fundamental rights of the human being, value systems, traditions and beliefs", which laid the ground for UNESCO's normative action, notably the 2001 Universal Declaration on Cultural Diversity, which acknowledges cultural diversity as "a source of exchange, innovation, creativity, [...] which is as necessary for humankind as biodiversity is for nature".

The Ministers express concern about multiple, protracted and multidimensional crises linked to climate change and biodiversity loss, armed conflicts, natural hazards, pandemics, uncontrolled urbanisation and unsustainable development patterns, which increase poverty, cause setbacks in fundamental rights, accelerate migrations and mobility, and exacerbate inequalities, including the digital divide.

They commend the new impetus given to culture for sustainable development, peace and stability, as a force for resilience, social inclusion and cohesion, environmental protection and inclusive growth, and note the growing engagement with culture within the UN system, the UNESCO Meetings of Ministers of Culture (2019, 2020), and culture's historical integration into political, economic and social fora at global, regional and interregional levels.

They express concern about the cultural sector's continued vulnerability after the global COVID-19 crisis, which disrupted the cultural ecosystem, exacerbating structural fragilities and inequalities, including social and gender gaps, unequal access to culture, restrictions on fundamental freedoms, notably artistic freedom, and the status and livelihoods of artists, cultural professionals, practitioners and communities, particularly women, across the cultural value chain.

They reaffirm the imperative of protecting and promoting human rights and cultural diversity in view of increasing threats to culture in armed conflicts, resulting in intentional or collateral destruction of cultural heritage, accelerated illicit trafficking of cultural property, violations of human and cultural rights including discrimination, disruption of living cultural practices, and exacerbated vulnerability of cultural actors, institutions, sites and markets.

They acknowledge UNESCO's standard-setting architecture, listing: the Declaration of the Principles of International Cultural Cooperation (1966), the UNESCO Universal Declaration on Cultural Diversity (2001), the UNESCO Declaration concerning the Intentional Destruction of Cultural Heritage (2003); the Recommendation concerning the Status of the Artist (1980), the Recommendation on the Safeguarding of Traditional Culture and Folklore (1989), the Recommendation concerning Historic Urban Landscapes (2011) and the Recommendation concerning the Protection and Promotion of Museums and Collections, their Diversity and their Role in Society (2015); the Universal Copyright Convention (1952), the Hague Convention for the Protection of Cultural Property in the Event of Armed Conflict (1954) and its two Protocols (1954 and 1999), the Convention on the Means of Prohibiting and Preventing the Illicit Import, Export and Transfer of Ownership of Cultural Property (1970), the Convention concerning the Protection of the World Cultural and Natural Heritage (1972), the Convention on the Protection of the Underwater Cultural Heritage (2001), the Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage (2003) and the Convention on the Protection and Promotion of the Diversity of Cultural Expressions (2005).

They welcome the growing transversality of culture in public policies, enabling inclusive and participatory cultural policies involving governments, local authorities, civil society organizations, IGOs, the private sector and communities, including women, youth, children, indigenous peoples, persons with disabilities and vulnerable groups. They underline the structural impact of the digital transformation on the cultural sector, which opens prospects for broadening access to culture and stimulating creativity, while posing challenges: reinforced global imbalance of flows of cultural goods and services, impoverishment of cultural and linguistic diversity online linked to artificial intelligence systems and insufficient regulation of algorithms, unequal access to culture, unfair remuneration of artists, and deepened inequalities due to the unequal concentration of global cultural platforms.

### Call to Action: Commitments of the Ministers (p. 3–7)

**Cultural diversity as a foundational commitment (para. 9):** The Ministers reaffirm their commitment to protect and promote cultural diversity as the foundation of the identity of peoples and the founding principle of all UNESCO Conventions, Recommendations and Declarations in the field of culture, inseparable from respect for human dignity and all human rights. They reiterate the individual and collective responsibility, on behalf of future generations, to ensure the conservation, safeguarding and promotion of the entire cultural sector, tangible and intangible, as an ethical imperative, ensuring greater equity, geographical balance and representativity of heritage across all regions, and promoting cultural and creative industries.

**Human and cultural rights (para. 10):** They commit to foster an enabling environment for the respect and exercise of all human rights, in particular cultural rights, individual and collective, in all areas of culture, including the digital environment, to reduce inequalities for women, youth, children, indigenous peoples, people of African descent, persons with disabilities and vulnerable groups, by:
(i) supporting access to culture for all and participation in cultural life and its benefits, treated as an ethical, social and economic imperative;
(ii) reinforcing the economic and social rights of artists, cultural professionals and practitioners, easing their mobility and defending their status, including by strengthening intellectual property;
(iii) defending and advancing artistic freedom and freedom of expression;
(iv) protecting and nurturing the diversity of cultural contents, as well as linguistic diversity;
(v) putting in place legal and public policy frameworks that defend the rights of peoples and communities to their cultural identity and heritage, including the expressions of the cultures of indigenous peoples;
(vi) stepping up efforts to advance the safeguarding, return and restitution of cultural property, including in consultation with the populations concerned and with their free, prior and informed consent.

**Systemic anchoring of culture in public policies (para. 11):** They advocate anchoring culture in public policies at international, regional, sub-regional, national and local levels, and within other relevant UN funds and programmes, as an enabler and driver of resilience, social inclusion and economic growth — from education, employment (especially for women and youth), health and emotional wellbeing to poverty reduction, gender equality, environmental sustainability, tourism, trade and transport.

**Strengthening cultural policies and financing (para. 12):** They call for strengthening and adapting cultural policies through effective implementation of UNESCO Culture Conventions and Recommendations, engaging a systemic participation of diverse stakeholders — national and local actors, including through the UNESCO Creative Cities Network, cultural institutions, civil society, professional networks, experts and concerned communities, for instance via the UNESCO Inter-Agency Platform on Culture for Sustainable Development — and urge preserving and strengthening financing for culture, with the medium-term aim of allocating a progressively increasing national budget to the culture sector.

**Culture and education (para. 13):** They underline the importance of enhancing synergies between culture and education, with a view to:
(i) expanding learning outcomes and enhancing quality education — especially for young people — and the appreciation of cultural diversity, multilingualism, arts education and digital literacy, including in school curricula and lifelong learning, as well as through artificial intelligence;
(ii) fostering the systemic integration of culture into formal, informal and non-formal education, including by valuing the contribution of all segments of society to the transmission of knowledge;
(iii) supporting technical and vocational education and training (TVET) in the cultural sector through structural investment in public policies to adapt skills and support employment;
(iv) investing in the educational and social role of museums, creative hubs, libraries, archives and cultural institutions.
They support the development of a revised UNESCO framework on culture and arts education, encompassing the evolutions of the cultural sector, in collaboration with relevant stakeholders such as UNESCO National Commissions and expert networks.

**Protection of cultural heritage in crisis (para. 14):** They reiterate the call for protection of cultural heritage, tangible and intangible, and cultural expressions, notably in times of crisis including extreme climate events and natural hazards, condemn actions targeting culture in armed conflicts and the use of cultural properties or their surroundings for military purposes, and support effective implementation of international norms: the UNESCO 1954 Convention and its two Protocols (1954 and 1999), the UNESCO 1970, 1972, 2001, 2003 and 2005 Conventions, the UNIDROIT 1995 Convention and the UNESCO Declaration concerning the Intentional Destruction of Cultural Heritage (2003), including strengthened action in emergencies as an ethical and security imperative to combat organised crime and the financing of terrorism.

**Culture and climate change (para. 15):** They stress the importance of integrating cultural heritage and creativity into international discussions on climate change, acknowledging the role of culture for climate action, notably through traditional and indigenous knowledge systems. They encourage UNESCO to develop operational guidelines on the subject, in the framework of the UNESCO 1972, 2001, 2003 and 2005 Conventions and the 2011 and 2015 Recommendations, in synergy with the UNFCCC, the CBD, the WMO, the FAO, UNEP, the IPCC, and the Flexible Mechanism for addressing the impacts of climate change on cultural and natural heritage (2020), to assist Member States in addressing extreme climate impacts on the entire cultural sector, and to deepen dialogue on reducing the cultural sector's carbon footprint.

**Fight against illicit trafficking (para. 16):** They commit to fight illicit trafficking of cultural property through effective implementation of the UNESCO 1970 Convention, strengthening cooperation with INTERPOL, the World Customs Organization (WCO), UNIDROIT, the International Council of Museums (ICOM), the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) and the art market. They request UNESCO to scale up its response globally by:
(i) strengthening advocacy and action on how illicit trafficking harms the memory, identity and future of peoples;
(ii) encouraging the introduction of penal or administrative sanctions in national legislations;
(iii) promoting the development of national and local capacities in all countries;
(iv) fostering increased and effective cooperation with art market actors, notably regarding the codification of the certificate of origin of cultural property and measures to stop museums and private collectors from acquiring unprovenanced objects;
(v) ensuring the engagement, mobilisation and coordination of all stakeholders and the public, including through digital technologies and online platforms, in response to the growth of online trade in cultural property, and supporting awareness-raising.

**Return and restitution of cultural property (para. 17):** They call for an open and inclusive international dialogue for the return and restitution of cultural property, including illegally exported property, to countries of origin under UNESCO's aegis, including property outside the scope of the UNESCO 1970 Convention, as an ethical imperative fostering the right of peoples and communities to enjoy their cultural heritage. They encourage UNESCO to promote effective implementation of existing legal frameworks through proactive mediation by the UNESCO Intergovernmental Committee for Promoting the Return of Cultural Property, to assist countries in conservation and management of cultural property in situ through capacity building and culturally sensitive educational approaches, and to adopt an updated text of the International Code of Ethics for Traders in Cultural Property.

**Sustainable cultural and creative sectors and digital transformation (para. 18):** They are determined to foster sustainable cultural and creative sectors supporting the creative economy through effective implementation of the UNESCO 2005 Convention and the UNESCO 1980 Recommendation, by guaranteeing the social and economic rights of artists and cultural professionals, facilitating mobility, and better regulating digital platforms through participatory dialogue. They call upon UNESCO to assist Member States in harnessing the digital transformation, building on the 2017 Operational Guidelines on the Implementation of the Convention in the Digital Environment and the culture-related provisions of the 2021 UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence, with a view to:
(i) facilitate equitable access to cultural markets;
(ii) design, develop and implement policies, regulatory frameworks and action plans for the production, dissemination and consumption of cultural goods and services in the digital environment, notably by fostering structured dialogue between all stakeholders from the private sector, relevant NGOs and IGOs — in particular the WTO, WIPO, UNCTAD and the ILO — as well as global digital platforms and internet providers;
(iii) build capacities to leverage digital technologies for cultural employment and to improve the safeguarding, promotion, digitisation and inventorying of cultural heritage, including through TVET and by fostering South-South and North-South cooperation.

### Strategic Directions for UNESCO: Culture as a Global Public Good (p. 7–8)

The Ministers pledge themselves to a strengthened multilateralism in which culture is recognised as a global public good whose intrinsic value enables and drives sustainable development, and they request UNESCO to work towards joint implementation of the following strategic directions:

**Para. 19:** They call on the UN Secretary General to firmly anchor culture as a global public good and to integrate it as a specific goal in its own right in the development agenda beyond 2030. They ask the Director-General of UNESCO to launch a broad consultation involving Member States, civil society, academia and the private sector on the multidimensional impact of culture as a global public good, and to strengthen advocacy for the inclusion of culture in the United Nations Future Summit, scheduled in 2024, echoing UNESCO's founding mandate to "build peace in the minds of men and women" through social justice and human dignity.

**Para. 20:** To support the positioning of culture as a global public good, advance implementation of the Our Common Agenda report and the Declaration on the commemoration of the seventy-fifth anniversary of the United Nations, and inform future cultural and public policies and UNESCO's programmatic action, they underline the need to coordinate, strengthen and develop instruments and mechanisms for the integrated analysis, monitoring and measurement of culture and its impact on sustainable development. They call upon the Director-General to develop conceptual studies on the impact of culture in all its dimensions, together with relevant data, and to produce a comprehensive Global Report on Cultural Policies on a quadrennial basis, building on information, data and existing indicators provided by Member States, notably through periodic reports on UNESCO's normative instruments in the field of culture.

**Para. 21:** They consider ministerial-level meetings to measure progress, impacts and opportunities in culture highly important, and call on UNESCO to consider convening, from 2025 onwards, a World Forum on Cultural Policies every four years, within appropriate existing procedures and mechanisms, to address priority areas for cultural policy in a constructive and inclusive dialogue, strengthen multilateral action, collaboration and solidarity between countries, and contribute to UNESCO's strategic lines of work, building on the findings of the Global Report on Cultural Policies.

**Para. 22:** They request that the Director-General of UNESCO develop a plan with concrete actions and timeframe to accelerate implementation of the Declaration's provisions within the framework of the Organization's Medium-Term Strategy and its Programme and Budget, sustain effective dialogue with Member States and relevant stakeholders, and report regularly to Member States on what has been achieved and what obstacles remain.

## GLOSSAR

- **Culture (1982 MONDIACULT definition)** — a "set of distinctive spiritual, material, intellectual and emotional features that characterize a society or social group, [which] includes not only arts and letters, but also modes of life, the fundamental rights of the human being, value systems, traditions and beliefs".
- **Cultural diversity** — a "source of exchange, innovation, creativity, [...] which is as necessary for humankind as biodiversity is for nature"; the foundation of the identity of peoples and the founding principle of all UNESCO culture instruments.
- **Culture as a global public good** — the Declaration's central normative thesis — culture has intrinsic value and enables and drives sustainable development, and should be anchored as a specific goal in the development agenda beyond 2030.
- **Cultural rights** — individual and collective human rights to be respected and exercised in all areas of culture, from heritage to cultural and creative sectors, including the digital environment.
- **Transversality of culture** — the growing integration of culture across public policies, involving multiple actors and expanding the voices of diverse segments of society.
- **Cultural value chain** — the full chain of cultural production in which artists, cultural professionals, practitioners and communities participate.
- **Free, prior and informed consent** — the standard required when consulting populations concerned by the protection, return and restitution of cultural property.
- **Digital divide** — the inequality in access to digital resources, identified as one of the exacerbated inequalities of the contemporary global landscape.
- **Illicit trafficking of cultural property** — the illegal trade in cultural objects, including its growing online dimension, to be fought through the UNESCO 1970 Convention and international cooperation.
- **Return and restitution of cultural property** — the ethical imperative of restoring cultural property, including illegally exported property, to its countries of origin, including items outside the scope of the 1970 Convention.
- **Creative economy** — the economy driven by sustainable cultural and creative sectors, supported through the UNESCO 2005 Convention and the 1980 Recommendation.
- **Global Report on Cultural Policies** — a comprehensive quadrennial report requested from UNESCO's Director-General on cultural policies, based on Member States' data and indicators.
- **World Forum on Cultural Policies** — a proposed ministerial-level forum, to be convened by UNESCO from 2025 onwards every four years, to measure progress and address priority areas for cultural policy.
- **TVET (technical and vocational education and training)** — training in the cultural sector supported through structural investment in public policies to adapt skills and support employment.
- **Cultural diplomacy** — the use of culture to renew and broaden bilateral and multilateral cooperation, promote multilingualism and a culture of peace, and enable dialogue and solidarity within and between countries.
