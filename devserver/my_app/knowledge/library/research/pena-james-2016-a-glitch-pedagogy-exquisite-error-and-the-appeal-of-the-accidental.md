# A Glitch Pedagogy: Exquisite Error and the Appeal of the Accidental

## PROFIL

Written by Edmund Adjapong Pena and Kedrick James, of the Digital Literacy Centre, Department of Language and Literacy Education, University of British Columbia, this scholarly article is offered as a provocation to curriculum thinkers and designers. Its subject is a proposed "glitch pedagogy" that extends and radicalizes John Dewey's principle of "consummatory experience," treating computational error, logical accidents, and procedural glitches as creative and productive forces in the lived curriculum; the authors' claim is that disruptions of programmed processes are fundamental to understanding our digital humanity and carry the same learning potential as mistakes in the arts, sciences, and lifelong learning. The paper first frames error and accident historically and aesthetically, drawing on jazz improvisation (Menezes, Pressing) and process writing (Elbow, Emig), then situates inquiry and technology via Dewey, Schön, and against the positions of Mullis and Aoki on proprietary and computer technology. It defines the glitch, following Menkman and the OED's astronaut origin, as a non-moralizable, contextual event, and presents a method of four hands-on cases: stitch-skipping (provoked panoramic camera mistakes), voice-vaguing (improvised vocal input to speech-to-text software, including infant babble), glot-swapping (misapplied auto-captioning of non-English stories, revealing corporate and nationalistic skewing in algorithms), and a fourth recombinant case combining all three iteratively. It closes with a conclusion on big data, human-robot semiosis, and algorithmic shaping of users, plus a twelve-point Manifesto of Glitch Pedagogy. It builds on affordance theory (Norman, Gaver) and cites documented algorithmic cultural bias (Noble, Gibbs). The article is usable in digital and critical literacy education, arts education concerned with improvisation and error, curriculum design, and teacher education addressing systems-thinking and the politics of representation.

## WISSEN

### Abstract (p. 108)

The authors—Ernesto Peña and Kedrick James of the Digital Literacy Centre, Department of Language and Literacy Education, University of British Columbia—propose a "glitch pedagogy" that extends and radicalizes John Dewey's (1934) principle of "consummatory experience." By deliberately provoking computer glitches, they treat computational error, logical accidents, and procedural glitches as creative and productive forces in the lived curriculum. Their thesis: disruptions of programmed processes that produce unique by-products are fundamental to understanding our digital humanity, and these irregularities carry the same learning potential as mistakes and fortunate accidents in the arts, sciences, and lifelong learning.

### Introduction: Accident and Error (p. 109–110)

The paper opens by noting the negative stigma attached to error, despite the fact that good teachers work with mistakes to activate learning. The myth that children learn languages more easily than adults stems from young children's freedom to err in speech. Error is at the base of logic, since logic is pattern recognition aimed at reducing error; error also accelerates processing activity (a hard drive running error messages overheats). Coded events entail both a goal and complex constraints acting as gatekeepers. Citing Stuart Armstrong (2015) on the difficulty of training artificial agents to make decisions based on values rather than facts (the "Sophisticated Cake or Death problem"), the authors argue that even artificial agents must learn from their errors.

Historically, industrial mechanization sought to eradicate human error through automation, but humans remained vital to resolve conflicts with non-standard materials (a carpenter can work with a knot in wood; a joinery machine could be destroyed by it). The shoe industry could never be fully automated because changing fashions and materials (stilettos in leather, snakeskin boots, sneakers, gumboots, flip-flops) meant machinery costs would never be recouped and production would lag the market by a year (Bright, 1958).

Understanding of error and accidental beauty comes from art, not industry: golden-age jazz improvisers embraced irregular input, viewing errors as "a motor of creativity" (Menezes, 2010, citing Pressing, 1988, on improvisation as a closed-loop feedback system with error-correction). Improvisation is likewise central to glitch: once basic learning is complete, the artist incorporates error-as-learning cycles, turning mistakes into innovation and beauty. This transformation of error into learning and metaknowledge is what Dewey anticipated with consummatory experience in *Art and Education* (1934), where engaged inquiry in process matters as much as or more than product—a tenet shared with the Process Writing movement (Elbow, 1981; Emig, 1971).

### Inquiry and Technology (p. 111–112)

For Dewey, inquiry is a pervasive, hands-on human activity whose tools of thought are valuable for the further knowledge they convey, not their accuracy; inquiry is democratizing, breaking the dominance of theory over practice (Schön, 1992). A consummatory experience is the interplay of means and ends toward a culminating, valuable experience—not necessarily pleasurable—and should emerge directly from teaching (Oral, 2013). Inquiry and consummatory experience are inextricably connected through reflexive inquiry.

Eric Mullis (2009) criticized proprietary digital devices for education because their intended-purpose orientation hides underlying processes (addressing the "whats" without the "hows"), putting instrumentalism at odds with consummatory experience. Criticism of proprietary technologies (Murphy, 2014) has focused on economics and logistics rather than pedagogy. Ted Aoki (1999) warned that overconfidence in computer technology limits the "standing reserve of manifestations of the real," endangering our ability to reveal other possibilities—"it is not computer technology that is dangerous; it is the essence of computer technology that is dangerous" (p. 112). The authors contend that digital devices can serve teaching and inquiry while preserving consummatory experience by adopting glitch as a prompt for inquiry.

### Glitch (p. 112–113)

Per the OED, "glitch" originated among astronauts for a momentary, uncontrollable electrical spike—"A glitch is a minute change of voltage in a wire which is enough to trigger another system out of proper sequence" (Stimson Jr., 1961, p. 232). In computing, glitch tends to be a euphemism for bug and undesirable error; in gaming and art it is seen as serendipitous, privileging unexpectedness over failure, and in glitch art the lack of control is a desirable ontological condition (Menkman, 2011). A glitch requires a norm to digress from; it could be understood, predicted, and become a norm, and what is a glitch for one person may be a norm for another. The authors therefore define a glitch as a *non-moralizable, contextual event*. Treating the glitch as a product instrumental to understanding its own process—a kind of reverse engineering—glitches can interrogate underlying processes, disrupt the standing reserve of realities Aoki denounced, and raise questions about collaboration, agency, authorship, and intentionality (who authors a glitch: the user, the programmer, the device, or all?).

### Method: Cases, Experiments, Protocols, and Provocations (p. 113)

Glitch is envisioned as a hands-on form of inquiry—something to be provoked and performed, and provocative of methodological innovation. Four cases are presented: the first three use different technologies (static imagery, automated literacy, language translation protocols); the fourth combines all three methods recombinantly, glitching an artifact at each stage of production. All resources are available on most current devices and operating systems. The three glitch techniques are: provoked panoramic camera mistakes (*stitch-skipping*), automated voice-to-text fuzzy interpretation (*voice-vaguing*), and natural language machine translation errors (*glot-swapping*).

### Case 1: Stitch-Skipping (p. 113–116)

Drawing on affordance theory (Norman, 1988; Gaver, 1991 on perceived and hidden affordances; interface metaphors and skeuomorphic design), glitches can be provoked by willingly disrupting the perceived affordances of a system. Panoramic photos require a steady sweeping motion with subjects remaining still; failing to comply produces a glitch. The authors first created a reference (canonical) panorama, then, under similar conditions, produced glitched versions by intentionally moving the camera or elements of the scene in non-normative ways, comparing results to devise explanations.

Panoramas break the ergonomic conventions of optical devices (landscape proportions like 4:3 or 16:9, arguably based on the placement of human eyes; Skopec, 2004) and extend perception beyond a normal gaze (Nelson, 2007). Panoramas are assembled by *stitching*—matching edges of contiguous images—and the stitching program's generous margin of error is where glitches emerge. Glitches reveal that panoramas are constructed from discrete sequential images, denoting time and sequence—features alien to standard photography—and visualize the sequential processes of their making. The photographer becomes formally represented in the image even without appearing as subject, disrupting the illusion of reality conveyed by representational media and enhancing critical sensitivity to the politics of representation.

### Case 2: Voice-Vaguing (p. 116–118)

Automated speech-to-text (STT) software performs linguistic, semantic, and pragmatic processing of input (Rost, 2011). Deep learning algorithms recognize words across voices, languages, and dialects without voice-dependent training (Larson & Jones, 2012), predicting expression through spectral analysis of big data rather than matching idealized speaker frames; since the 1990s the NSA has used voice recognition to scan communications (Froomkin, 2015). Since 2010 the gap between human and algorithmic speech recognition has grown exponentially (Deng et al., 2013). David Pogue (2010) predicted the keyboard would persist until 100% accuracy, but errors abound without normative pronunciation or syntax.

The authors used *voice-vaguing*: improvised and extended vocal technique to create ambiguity and illogical interference in input. Following an iterative remix process (James, 2015), online videos of infants babbling were fed to voice recognition software. Young voices are a known weak spot of the software (due to formant distribution and fundamental resonances). Regardless of language background, the software reduced complex babble—though it sounds like speech with adult-modeled inflections and pauses—to monosyllabic words, mainly "are," "or," or "her"; other words in the output resulted from background noise or adult voices. Children up to age five showed the same trend. This "baby-babble text" (Sample 3) became the data source for further iterative glitch experiments.

### Case 3: Glot-Swapping (p. 118–119)

*Glot-swapping* trades the vocal sounds of one language for another—e.g., expecting English diction to be captioned in Swahili. Auto-captioning software (used for subtitles for deaf viewers or muted environments) combines speech recognition and translation algorithms embedded in deep neural networks (Yu & Deng, 2015). The experiment misrepresented input, activating English auto-captioning on video versions of non-English children's stories, forcing homophonic translation.

With adult voices, the language classifier produced diversified English output from Russian, Spanish, and Hungarian sources. Remarkably, the mistranslations frequently featured words pertaining to corporate entities and national and religious identity—e.g., the closed-captioning of *Hedgehog in the Fog* (Norshteyn, 1975)—suggesting a value-motivated skewing of artificial agents toward corporations like Facebook and Google, with nationalistic and imperialistic overtones in probability-generated phrases. The authors connect this to documented cultural bias in algorithms (Maddox, 2015; Noble & Roberts, 2016 on Google) and Microsoft's AI chat-bot that adopted racist traits (Gibbs, 2016). While the algorithms learn from us, we learn from the algorithms; auto-captioning drawing on U.S.-based big data is "a tad xenophobic," and critical literacy scholars should examine these automated interpreters as governments and institutions increasingly rely on them.

### Case 4: Toward Iterative Glitch (p. 120–122)

The authors posit a "perverse desire to witness deformity in the becoming of digital objects," engaging at the level of consummatory experience with mundane software. This recombinant case combined voice-vaguing, stitch-skipping, and glot-swapping: the baby-babble text (Sample 3) was printed on letter-format paper; a glitched panorama photo of it was taken by mobile phone; the resulting graphic text (resembling concrete poetry, Sample 5) was uploaded to a free online OCR program, which recognized the distorted graphemes as letters and ideograms from many languages (Sample 6)—language specialists in the department were enlisted to decipher the multilingual polyglot text. Running text-to-speech identified the unicode characters, "truing" predictions and turning the process into a glot-swapping game.

Additionally, an improvised musical recording of a live vocal performance of the baby-babble text with amplified analogue and synthesized soundscape was played to speech recognition software. Comparison showed the computer-generated remix of children's voices to be highly complex; stitch-skipping distorts at a graphemic level, while sonic voice-vaguing with musical interference produced a more visceral text with corporate/political overtones—referencing Google and President Barack Obama, neither present in the source—corresponding to the Case 3 findings. This transmediation across sensory domains suggests further research. Once a text begins its iterative journey it is never finished; through editing, recycling, and re-processing it takes on different readings—precisely what educators hope to encourage in critical digital literacies. The authors acknowledge that AI entities, once trained, begin to train the user: the more automation does for users, the less they know what it is doing, how well, and to what ends.

### Conclusion (p. 122–123)

Glitch pedagogy helps learners gain an overview—partial and biased—of the big data substrate on which contemporary voice and visual recognition systems are rooted, a substrate recycled by users and conditioning the language of the youngest populations as digital devices increasingly babysit children (Schaff & Mohan, 2014). When applications become agentive in the production and assembly of semiosis and psyche, a strong bond forms between the artificial other and those it serves; prescriptive social algorithms shape their users as genres do (Schryer, 1999), and glitch pedagogy interrogates that relationship dialogically and creatively, yielding surprising resonant clusters of meaning through collaborative human-robot semiosis—"poetry grows through the cracks in intended meaning." Like digital gaming, glitch pedagogy offers a safe environment where failure is a powerful learning experience without real-life stigma (Schaff & Mohan, 2014), celebrating mistakes and processing errors as central to creativity, inquiry, invention, and discovery of knowledge construction in the twenty-first century. The manifesto is offered as a provocation to curriculum thinkers and designers.

### Manifesto of Glitch Pedagogy (p. 124)

The twelve points, as given:

1. The movement of Glitch Pedagogy recognizes accident and error as inherent to all learning processes.
2. Glitch is understood to be the formative source of learning; glitch-free environments repulse rather than attract curiosity and inquiry.
3. Glitch is particularly effective as a means to convey meta-knowledge of systems and systems-thinking.
4. Glitch is caused by incommensurable input which is improperly digested by logical processes. The input is thus recombined and reconstituted irregularly producing new or unexpected results.
5. Glitch arises on a backdrop of programmatic consistency. It is therefore stigmatized.
6. Glitch interrupts the industrialist and the fascist. Glitch then interrogates power without cruelty or revenge.
7. Glitch is non-conformity.
8. Not until digital technology dominated the socius did glitch become paradigmatic and utilitarian.
9. Glitch provokes the hipster revolution. Without exemplary inconsistency, character is barren. Inconsistency gives character to ideas, charges them, puts them in motion.
10. Glitches are inherently amoral; they are neither good nor bad, but rather are the product of natural law in information environments.
11. Without glitch there is no evolution and no innovation. Glitch pedagogy is the best preparation for life.
12. Einstein's maxim that problems will not be solved by the same thinking that created them is our central tenet.

## GLOSSAR

- **glitch pedagogy** — a hands-on pedagogical approach that provokes and performs glitches as tools of inquiry, celebrating error and accident as central to creativity, learning, and critical digital literacy.
- **consummatory experience** — Dewey's principle, adopted here, that the interplay of means and ends in engaged inquiry is as significant as, or more significant than, the final product of learning.
- **glitch** — a non-moralizable, contextual event—an unexpected product of purposeful disruption that requires a norm to digress from, and whose status depends on the witness's prior knowledge.
- **stitch-skipping** — provoking glitches in panoramic photographs by purposefully digressing from the standard sweeping protocol, moving the camera or scene elements during capture.
- **voice-vaguing** — using improvised and extended vocal technique to create ambiguity and illogical interference in input to speech recognition software.
- **glot-swapping** — misrepresenting input to auto-captioning/translation software so that the vocal sounds of one language are homophonically translated or captioned in another.
- **iterative glitch** — a recombinant method that chains stitch-skipping, voice-vaguing, and glot-swapping (with OCR, text-to-speech, and translation software) so that a text is repeatedly glitched and never finished.
- **perceived affordances** — noticeable hints of an artifact's use or interaction possibilities, whose willing disruption can provoke glitches.
