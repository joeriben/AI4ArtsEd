## 6. What the help text should tell a user

The Help panel's own text, which opens on arrival, says: "Two sets of three stand to choose from: three of today's AI models, or the same family three years apart, 2022, 2023 and 2024. All three compute with the same prompt and the same seed. To the prompt, the platform also searches for real photographs on Wikimedia Commons. What separates the pictures lies in the differences between the AI models: those you can explore yourselves. A thing named in the prompt sometimes stands clearly in one column, barely in the next, and is missing in the third. Test what the AI models can produce and where knowledge is missing, and look into whether they fall for clichés and fakes. The photographs are the counter-horizon to that: real pictures somebody took." and, as its last line, "This help can support you with the comparative analysis of the results. Just ask it about them." What it should add, in this order:

- The three tests the text names have handles on the surface. What the models can produce: the note under the model fields says what each maker claims to have built the model for (SDXL several aspect ratios and a second text encoder; SD 3.5 “improving text comprehension, typography, and human preference ratings”, typography being the writing inside the picture; Flux 2 legible fine text and up to ten reference pictures; Gemini 3 Pro Image legible text in several languages and a Google Search query behind the picture). A prompt with a word to be written in the picture tests the claim column by column. Where knowledge is missing: a prompt that names a specific thing from a place (a dish, a garment, a festival, a building); the Commons row shows what the archive holds for the term. Clichés and fakes: an occupation, "a person in typical clothing", a place, and Gemini 3 Pro's claim to current information.
- Up to five models stand side by side; at least two stay selected, and a field that would go below two stays on without a message.
- The note describes the models of a chosen set, SD History or Current models, and leaves as soon as the selection is the reader's own. CommonCanvas XL C and Flux 3 Klein stand in no set and never get a note; what they are stands only in the build's code: CommonCanvas XL C shares SDXL's architecture and is trained on about 70 million Creative Commons-licensed pictures and on nothing else; Flux 3 Klein is Black Forest Labs' model of 2026, kept beside Flux 2.
- The row "Results for this prompt" is the Commons search the text names, not a result of the run; the sentence under it says that the pictures are "not the same subject and not a correct answer". The row is the comparison's second object: pictures that already exist, each with author, licence and date as the archive records them, and in several registers at once (in my run a hospital in Lagos under CC BY-SA 4.0, a uniform in a Serbian museum, a photograph of the 1910s in the public domain), where the three generated pictures share one register and name no source. At the kids and youth levels every found picture is checked before it is shown.
- The prompt field takes a dropped picture and turns it into a description (the field's own placeholder says so); Choose Perspective runs a Prompt Interception on the prompt before the comparison; the card back of every picture (long press) carries seed, size and computing time and the way on: favorite, forward to Flow, download, print; the Help can open a picture when asked, which is what "Interpretation help" offers.

## 7. Use possibilities, action-oriented

- Test the makers' claims with writing: a prompt with a word to be written in the picture ("a shop with a sign that says BREAD"), run with SD History, then with Current models; read in the note who claims legible text and count the legible letters per column.
- Run a prompt that names a specific thing from a place you know (a dish, a festival, a garment, a building) on five models; set the columns against the Commons row for the same term and against what you know.
- Run one occupation prompt ("a nurse at work", "a plumber at work") through three models and count who appears: gender, age, skin, setting; run it twice (two seeds) and separate what stays from what changes.
- Read the Commons row as a corpus: per picture the year, the kind (photograph, museum object, painting), the author and the licence as given; set the generated pictures beside it and name their register and their source line (there is none).
- Set SDXL and CommonCanvas XL C side by side (same architecture, same year, the second trained on Creative Commons pictures only) with prompts that name a known artwork, a brand or a famous building, and see which of the two knows it.
- Set Flux 2 and Flux 3 Klein side by side: one maker, one year apart.
- Write a plain prompt and run it; press Choose Perspective, pick one, and run the written prompt again; per model, compare the two pictures: what the perspective changed, what the model kept.
- Drag a photograph (objects and places, no people) onto the prompt field; the description it produces becomes the prompt; run it and set every column against the photograph.
- Open the card back of a picture: seed, size and computing time; forward the picture to Flow and continue there.
- Ask the Help about the finished pictures ("Interpretation help"): it can open them when asked.

## 8. Teaching scenarios

**S1 — Who is the nurse?** (formal, social studies or ethics, grades 8–10, 90 min). Pairs run three occupation prompts through the current models, record gender/age/skin/setting per picture in a table, and then do the same for the Commons row, adding per found picture the year, the kind (photograph, museum object, painting) and the author and licence the row gives. Sequence: 10 min prompts; 30 min runs (three per pair); 30 min both tables; 20 min plenary: which traits agree across models, in how many registers the archive holds the occupation, and what the generated pictures say about their source (nothing). Subject link: stereotypes, representation, provenance. Uncertainty: the Commons search term is automatic and can land beside the subject (a soldier named Nurse); the teacher should look at the row before the class does.

**S2 — Can it write?** (formal, art or computer science, grades 7–10, 90 min). Pairs write a prompt with a short word to be written in the picture (a shop sign, a jersey number, a street name) and run it with the SD History set; they count the legible letters per column, then read the note: which maker claims what, and in which year typography enters the claim. Second run with the Current models set, same prompt. Sequence: 10 min prompt; 20 min two runs; 30 min counting and table; 30 min the note against the table. Subject link: typography, what a claim in a paper means, reading a source. Uncertainty: I ran no prompt with writing; the claim stands in the note's text.

**S3 — What the model does not know** (formal, geography, ethics or an intercultural class, grades 8–12, 90 min; non-formal in a multilingual group). Each pair writes a prompt that names one specific thing from a place they know (a dish, a festival, a garment, a building, a game) and runs it on three or five models. Table per column: what the prompt named, what the model drew, what the Commons row holds for the term, what the pair knows. Then plenary: which models drew something generic in place of the named thing, and where the archive had more than the models. Sequence: 15 min prompts; 25 min runs; 30 min tables; 20 min plenary. Subject link: cultural knowledge, what training data hold and do not hold. Uncertainty: not run; in my run the Commons term was shortened automatically ("nurse at work"), so a long prompt can land beside its subject.

**S4 — One prompt, two readings** (formal, art or language teaching, grades 9–13, 90 min). The class writes one plain prompt and runs it on three models. Then one pupil presses Choose Perspective, picks a perspective (the overlay offers the platform's perspectives, the same set as in Flow, report 01), and the written prompt is run on the same three models. Six pictures: per model, what the perspective changed; per perspective, what each model kept doing its way. Sequence: 10 min prompt; 15 min first run; 15 min perspective and second run; 30 min comparison; 20 min plenary. Subject link: Prompt Interception, model against instruction. Uncertainty: I did not press Choose Perspective on this surface.

**S5 — The photograph and its re-drawings** (non-formal, photo workshop, age 12+, 2 h). Participants drag a photograph of their own (objects and places, no people) onto the prompt field; a vision model writes a description into the field, which they read and correct; five models draw from it. Each column against the photograph: what the description lost, what each model added. Then they change one sentence of the description and run again. Sequence: 15 min photographs and descriptions; 30 min first run; 30 min comparison; 30 min second run; 15 min round. Subject link: description, picture to text to picture, what a model is never shown. Uncertainty: I did not try the drop; the field's placeholder names it.

**S6 — Data you can name** (formal, ethics or art, grades 10–13, 45 min). Pairs run three prompts that name a known artwork, a brand and a famous building on SDXL and CommonCanvas XL C (same architecture, 2023; the second trained on about 70 million Creative Commons-licensed pictures and on nothing else) and record which of the two draws the named thing. Then the question what it means that one model's training pictures are all licensed for reuse and the other's are not disclosed in its paper. Subject link: training data, copyright, licence. Uncertainty: nothing on the surface says what CommonCanvas XL C is; the teacher brings the fact (it stands in the build's code, with the paper arXiv:2310.16825).
