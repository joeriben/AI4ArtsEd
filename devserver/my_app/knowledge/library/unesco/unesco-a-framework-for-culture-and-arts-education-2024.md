# A Framework for Culture and Arts Education

## PROFIL

The UNESCO Framework for Culture and Arts Education argues that culture and the arts must be systematically integrated across all learning contexts—formal, non-formal, and informal—as a transformative ecosystem advancing sustainable development, peace, equity, and inclusion. It positions itself against fragmented approaches that treat culture and education as separate domains, and against the context of rising inequalities, armed conflict, disinformation, racism, xenophobia, and discrimination that fuel social divisions and hamper sustainable development. Grounding its mandate in the UNESCO Constitution, UDHR Article 27, SDG 4 Target 4.7, and the MONDIACULT 2022 Declaration, the Framework defines "Culture and Arts Education" as encompassing teaching and learning about, with, and through the arts and culture, spanning lifelong and lifewide learning across physical, virtual, and blended spaces. For arts education, it provides a policy-level instrument for aligning curricula, teacher training, and professional development with principles of accessibility, cultural diversity, gender equality, and co-creation across all educational settings.

## WISSEN

### Preamble and Endorsement of the UNESCO Framework for Culture and Arts Education

Ministers ground the Framework in the UNESCO Constitution's mandate to diffuse culture and educate for justice, liberty, and peace, and in UDHR Article 27's right to participate in cultural life. Against a context of rising inequalities, armed conflict, disinformation, racism, and discrimination, they argue education systems must be transformed in purpose, content, and delivery to promote equity, inclusion, quality, and relevance, per SDG 4 Target 4.7. They assert culture and the arts are integral to holistic development, resilience, and well-being, providing the foundation of values, identity, critical thinking, creativity, and self-expression, while opening social and economic prospects. They commit to greater synergy between culture and education (MONDIACULT 2022), acknowledge prior instruments (Lisbon 2006 Road Map, Seoul 2010 Agenda), and endorse the Framework on a humanistic and empowering vision, entrusting UNESCO with implementation, operational guidance, policy dialogue, knowledge sharing, convening stakeholders, and monitoring progress.

### Introduction and Conceptual Clarification

Global challenges demand reinvesting in cultural capacities and rethinking education. Culture and arts enrich education—enabling diverse expression, ways of knowing, and cognitive, social-emotional, and behavioural skills. In March 2021, UNESCO Member States decided to elaborate a Framework for Culture and Arts Education to: promote access/inclusion; capture broadened understandings across formal, non-formal and informal settings; integrate into policies/curricula; widen cooperation; and support sustainable development. Definitions adopted: culture (MONDIACULT 1982/2022)—distinctive spiritual, material, intellectual and emotional features characterizing a society or social group; education (UNESCO 2023)—an inalienable human right, lifelong and society-wide. 'Culture and Arts Education' positions culture including the arts as an educational tool, an approach, and a field of study, research and practice—encompassing teaching and learning about, with and through the arts and culture.

### Guiding Principles and Objectives

Culture and arts education should be holistic, transformative, and impactful, guided by seven principles: (a) accessibility as a common good fostering well-being, requiring strengthened policy role and public investment; (b) grounding in human rights and fundamental freedoms per international instruments; (c) mainstreaming gender equality and countering gender discrimination, bias, harmful content and violence; (d) recognizing cultural diversity as common heritage, protected through mutual understanding, equitable access, and linguistic diversity; (e) ensuring inclusion, non-discrimination, and respect for diversity, countering bullying, stereotypes, racism, xenophobia; (f) enabling co-creation with active participation of learners, educators, cultural professionals, communities in design, implementation, and evaluation across formal, non-formal, and informal settings; (g) promoting lifelong and lifewide learning across physical, virtual, and blended spaces.

The Framework's objectives: (i) contribute to sustainable development and peace per the 2030 Agenda, especially SDG 4 and SDG 8; (ii) harness technology advances while mitigating risks in digital technologies and AI, promoting ethical and responsible use; (iii) integrate culture and arts education across culture and education policies, curricula, teacher training, and professional development for lifelong and lifewide development of knowledge, skills, attitudes, values, and behaviours.

### Scope of the Framework

The UNESCO Framework defines culture and arts education as an **ecosystem** spanning all learners, contexts, and settings—formal, non-formal, and informal. It encompasses diverse pedagogies (especially those embracing diverse cultural perspectives, activities, practices, expressions, materials and objects), all modalities (offline, online, distance, blended), and all levels and types of provision. Grounded in a broad understanding of culture, it incorporates processes such as intercultural dialogue and values such as cultural, linguistic and knowledge diversity. It further mandates multistakeholder collaboration and broad intersectoral partnerships among educational and cultural institutions, government bodies, cultural spaces and activities, memorial and heritage sites, artists and other cultural professionals and practitioners, researchers, local communities, the private sector, foundations and civil society organizations.

### Strategic Goals for Culture and Arts Education

The text advances five interlocking strategic goals. **Access, inclusion, and equity** (a) grounds culture and arts education in the right to education and cultural rights, demanding removal of all obstacles — infrastructure, vulnerability, exclusion on grounds of race, colour, descent, gender, age, language, religion, political opinion, national, ethnic or social origin, economic or social condition of birth, disability or any other grounds — alongside equitable digital access and explicit anti-colonial, anti-stereotype positioning. Priority learners include Indigenous Peoples, persons with disabilities, those in disadvantaged, impoverished and vulnerable situations, such as refugees, migrants, displaced persons, victims of armed conflict, and learners in crisis-affected and post-disaster contexts. **Contextual, quality, lifelong and lifewide learning** (b) calls for mainstreaming culture and the arts from ECCE onward through locally-relevant curricula, Indigenous ways of knowing, mother tongue languages, and intergenerational transmission. **Appreciation of cultural diversity and critical engagement** (c) positions culture as a dynamic, heterogeneous space for contestation and co-learning, equipping learners to challenge instrumentalization, bias, and stereotypes. **Skills for resilient, just and sustainable futures** (d) leverages arts education for creativity, STEAM approaches, social-emotional skills ranging from empathy and solidarity to pluralistic thinking, and decent work in cultural and creative industries. **Institutionalization and valorization** (e) demands curricular prominence, sustainable resources, certification of cultural/artistic competencies, and cross-ministerial cooperation overcoming siloed approaches.

### Implementation Modalities for Culture and Arts Education

Culture and arts education requires systemic transformation across eight interconnected domains. Governance must establish cross-ministerial cooperation—culture, education, health, science, environment, economy—to integrate culture and arts across formal, non-formal and informal settings, supported by evidence-based monitoring. Learning environments should expand beyond schools to museums, libraries, creative hubs, heritage sites, and UNESCO network cities as interdisciplinary spaces. Learning experiences must integrate local knowledge systems and engage artists, living heritage bearers, and community stakeholders in curriculum development. The teaching profession should diversify to include cultural practitioners, with revised teacher education and improved working conditions. Digital technologies and AI require ethical, equitable access and multi-stakeholder dialogue on risks and opportunities. Partnerships, financing, and research mechanisms must be mobilized at all levels for long-term sustainability.

### Monitoring, Follow-up and Review

Member States and Associate Members should support Framework implementation by sharing progress, good practices and challenges, submitting voluntary national progress reports every four years starting 2025 via existing reporting mechanisms (Global Report on Cultural Policies, Global Education Monitoring Report, SDG Target 4.7 mechanisms). They should explore feasibility of establishing Category 2 Centres under UNESCO auspices to strengthen research, training, data collection, monitoring and evaluation of the culture–education nexus, subject to UNESCO procedures and sustainable resources.

UNESCO shall, within existing resources:
- i. Develop a specific global monitoring mechanism, harnessing existing mechanisms at global, regional and national levels
- ii. Facilitate regular knowledge-sharing around good-practice case studies
- iii. Engage UNESCO's networks across Education and Culture Sectors to advance research and evidence-based analysis; encourage Member States to mobilize national networks including UNESCO Chairs and UNESCO Associated Schools
- iv. Build and expand partnerships with public and private stakeholders
- v. Collect and disseminate research, progress reports, data and diverse practices
- vi. Engage in extensive reflection to deepen synergies between relevant Conventions, Recommendations, programmes and actions across the Culture and Education Sectors

## GLOSSAR

- **humanistic and empowering vision of culture and education** — The foundational orientation of the Framework, positioning culture and education as mutually reinforcing forces for human dignity, agency, and development.
- **cross-cutting approach to public policies** — An integrative policy strategy linking culture and education sectors, established by prior arts education conferences and carried forward by the Framework.
- **Target 4.7** — The SDG 4 provision specifying that education must promote sustainable development, human rights, gender equality, peace, global citizenship, and appreciation of cultural diversity.
- **Culture and Arts Education** — Positions culture including the arts as an educational tool, an approach, and a field of study, research and practice.
- **about, with and through** — The three modalities of teaching and learning that define the scope of culture and arts education.
- **lifewide learning** — Learning that occurs across diverse settings and environments throughout life, including physical, virtual, and blended spaces.
- **co-creation** — Collaborative design, implementation, and evaluation of culture and arts education involving all actors and beneficiaries.
- **ecosystem** — The Framework's organizing metaphor for culture and arts education, treating it as an interconnected whole across all people, settings, pedagogies, modalities, and levels.
- **lifelong and lifewide learning** — A perspective encompassing formal, non-formal, and informal education across all life stages and settings, sustaining intergenerational transmission and community engagement.
- **place-based education** — Education anchored in local cultural heritage, environments, and community mediators to strengthen connections between learners, their communities, and environments.
