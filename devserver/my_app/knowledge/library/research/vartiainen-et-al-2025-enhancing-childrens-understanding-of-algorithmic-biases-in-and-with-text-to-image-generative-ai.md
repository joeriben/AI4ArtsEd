# Enhancing children's understanding of algorithmic biases in and with text-to-image generative AI

## PROFIL

The article is written by Henriikka Vartiainen, Juho Kahila, Matti Tedre, Sonsoles Lopez-Pernas and Nicolas Pope, and reports empirical workshops run in spring 2023 with 213 fourth- and seventh-graders across 12 classrooms in Finnish elementary and secondary schools, within a prolonged program of co-design and design-based research bringing AI into Finnish schooling. Its subject is how children's explanations of what causes algorithmic bias in text-to-image generative models evolve through a collaborative, hands-on intervention, guided by two questions: which biases children examine in open-ended learning tasks and what causal accounts they give, and how those accounts change through a workshop on data-driven AI. The authors' position, grounded in conceptual change research from cognitive and constructivist traditions and building on Mertala et al. (2022), is that children carry naïve ideas and misconceptions about AI, that everyday experience alone will not yield a scientific view of it, and that hands-on, experiential learning with organized evidence, dialogue and collaboration can support the required conceptual change. The article contains a theoretical background on children's conceptions of AI; an account of text-to-image generative models, diffusion models and their ethical concerns (uncurated training data, copyright and style imitation, reproduction of stereotypes); a description of three workshops built on design-oriented learning and a new educational tool, GenAI Teachable Machine, covering ML basics, data-driven design, and AI's impact and ethics, with algorithmic bias demonstrated through Midjourney prompts; the data collection and analysis (53 group assignments, pre-/post-tests, inductive qualitative content analysis with five explanation categories, chi-square tests and an alluvial plot); results showing data-based explanations rising from 7.1% to 43.8% with a statistically significant medium effect; a discussion; and a section on limitations and future research, noting the single-city sample and calling for varied cultural backgrounds, special-education support, a control group, and age-adjusted scaffolding. It is usable in arts education contexts involving creative making with generative AI, media and visual arts, and computer science and AI literacy education at primary and lower-secondary level, wherever the aim is to foster critical reflection on the ethical and social consequences of AI and on the imaginaries generative AI reflects and may reinforce.

## WISSEN

### Purpose and research questions (pp. 5342–5344)

The article tackles a neglected problem: although worries about algorithmic bias in generative AI are mounting, hardly any research examines how to build children's and young people's awareness and understanding of such bias. The authors report empirical workshops run in spring 2023 with fourth- and seventh-graders—213 students across 12 classrooms in Finnish elementary and secondary schools, 209 of whom handed in assignments—centering on how the students' explanations of what causes algorithmic bias in text-to-image generative models evolved. Two questions guided the study: (1) which biases do children examine when given open-ended learning tasks, and what causal accounts do they give for algorithmic bias? (2) how do those accounts change through a collaborative, hands-on workshop on data-driven AI? Children's data-based explanations improved to a statistically significant degree on a written reasoning test taken before and after the intervention, and likewise in the worksheets they completed during a lesson devoted to algorithmic bias.

### Theoretical background (pp. 5344–5345)

Grounded in conceptual change research from cognitive and constructivist traditions, the authors observe that children carry naïve ideas and misconceptions drawn from everyday life—for instance, that AI is free of bias or has no ethical dimension, or anthropomorphic beliefs that machines decide things the way humans do, thinking, acting, and feeling as people would. Because modern AI is opaque and pervasive, grasping what happens inside such systems is hard. Coming to understand algorithmic bias demands substantial conceptual change: children must grasp notions such as training rather than programming a system, curating and labeling examples for training sets, making sense of outputs from fragile, opaque systems, and seeing how embedded biases can damage individuals and societies. Earlier work (Mertala et al., 2022) indicates that primary-schoolers are unlikely to form a scientific view of AI through everyday experience alone, yet science-learning studies show reasoning responds to interventions—especially hands-on experiences where children face organized evidence and are pushed to account for it, supported by dialogue, argumentation, collaboration, and classroom discussion.

### Text-to-image generative models and ethical concerns (pp. 5346–5347)

Text-to-image generative models are software that turns a written description—a "prompt"—into a matching picture. They are trained on billions of image–text pairs scraped from public online repositories. State-of-the-art systems rest on diffusion models: neural networks fed millions of labelled images that learn the statistically likely pixel patterns tied to textual labels, letting them create entirely new pictures (e.g., a "depressed cartoon pug" in a space suit on the moon with a whiskey bottle) that appear nowhere in the training set. The authors illustrate Midjourney's quality gains between version 1 (February 2022) and version 5.1 (May 2023). Ethical worries include: training on public images without curation, consent, or any opt-out for owners; copyright and style imitation (fantasy artist Greg Rutkowski objected to tens of thousands of AI images copying his signature style); and—central to this study—the reproduction of societal stereotypes around ethnicity, gender, sexual orientation and other attributes, which can favor or marginalize particular individuals or groups.

### Methods and research context (pp. 5348–5352)

The study extends prolonged co-design and design-based research bringing AI into Finnish schooling, with researchers, developers, and teachers collaborating in iterative cycles of design, evaluation, and theory-building. Participants comprised 129 fourth graders (7 schools) and 80 seventh graders (5 schools). Finnish research-integrity guidelines were followed: municipal permits, guardian consent, voluntary participation, and pseudonymized names.

Data were gathered in school projects of three workshops (each roughly 1.5–2 hours, about a week apart, the whole project spanning about 3 weeks), built on design-oriented learning that cast students as innovators and ML designers instead of mere consumers. The team built a new educational tool, GenAI Teachable Machine, to introduce core ML ideas—training data, examples, classes, curating data, inputs, outputs, confidence—and the ML workflow: gathering relevant data, cleaning and filtering it, labeling it, training a classifier with it, connecting classifier outputs to intended behaviors, and assessing the model. Children personalized projects with their own photographs, recordings, music, drawings, texts, animations, and graphics, then iteratively tested, re-curated, re-trained, and refined their classifiers—experiencing directly how the data they include or exclude injects bias into their apps.

The three workshops:

1. **Workshop 1 – ML's basic concepts and mechanisms**: an introduction to ML use cases (recommender systems, autonomous vehicles, spam filtering, social-media advertising); examining how the social media apps children use gather data; a demonstration of the tool; each small group building its own open-ended classifier; homework to think up everyday problems ML could solve.
2. **Workshop 2 – data-driven design**: groups picked one app idea and used a design template to negotiate what the app would do, what data it would need and from where, how many classes it should recognize, and on what conditions training data would be supplied; teams then tested, explained, and refined their classifiers, producing 71 different apps (e.g., recognizing facial expressions of emotion, dance moves, brand logos, footballers, dog breeds).
3. **Workshop 3 – AI's impact and ethics**: teams presented their apps and weighed benefits, risks, and their own learning. Researchers then introduced algorithmic bias through generative AI, explaining: (1) how web scrapers harvest image–text pairs (e.g., Instagram photos tagged with users' hashtags) to build large image-recognition systems; (2) that such systems are trained much like the students' own classifiers; (3) that the same data sets can also train image generators; (4) that correlating image features with text labels enables prompt-based generation but imports biases rooted in the training data. Bias was demonstrated by prompting Midjourney for government leaders (exclusively elderly white men) and classroom teachers (exclusively young white women). Students then made their own images in small groups with Midjourney, hunted for biases, and recorded on a worksheet the bias found, its causes, and possible remedies.

### Data collection and analysis (pp. 5352–5354)

Two data sets: 53 written group assignments (two blank sheets discarded) and pre-/post-tests (196 pre-tests, 194 post-tests). The pre-test showed boys playing soccer; students were told that making a tournament poster with generative AI is difficult because every generated player is a boy and no girls appear, and were asked why this happens, what harm it causes, and to whom. The post-test mirrored this with gamers. Answers ran to a sentence or two. An inductive qualitative content analysis produced five coding categories; inter-rater reliability was almost perfect (89.1% agreement, Cohen's K = 0.852, SE = 0.022). Effectiveness was tested with chi-square (whole sample and by grade), effect sizes via Cramer's V (0.10 small, 0.3 medium, 0.5 large, per Cohen, 1988), and transitions visualized in an alluvial plot.

### Results: types of biases investigated (pp. 5355–5356)

In the open-ended task, teams' prompts spanned professions and roles (fireman, nurse, principal, scientist, soldier, teacher, bullier, criminal, molester, dancer, cheerleader, student), sports (basketball player, football player, ice hockey player, athletics), people and social categories (children, person, woman, poor, poor human), animals and objects (dog, hamster, horses, car, phone, apple, anime), and the religious figure Jesus. Many teams concentrated on gender bias—partly because the teacher demonstration featured it—but most went beyond the presentation. Examples:

- Fourth graders found every generated firefighter was male, reasoning that the web holds mostly pictures of male firefighters; their fix was data-driven: if more photographs showed women in firefighting gear, the AI would start depicting them too.
- Fourth graders on bullies: only boys appeared even though girl bullies exist; they put the fix on the user: "Write clearer what you seek."
- Seventh graders on dogs: every image showed a dark, large, floppy-eared dog, attributed to the abundance of such pictures online; remedy: upload more varied dog photos.
- Seventh graders on "woman": every image shared one skin tone and hair color, attributed to the web's surplus of images of a particular type of woman; harm identified as "body appearance pressure."
- Seventh graders on Jesus: only a white Jesus despite his Middle Eastern origin, explained by Christian iconography depicting him as white since most Christians are white.
- Seventh graders on scientists: gloomy elderly men with wild hair and glasses; remedy: upload images of female scientists.
- Fourth graders on "poor": only old, dirty men; seventh graders on "person": only elderly people; on "car": only American vintage cars.

Of the 53 completed worksheets, 41 proposed remedies grounded in data, acknowledging that the composition of the training set matters for correcting over- and under-representation.

### Results: development of explanations (pp. 5357–5361)

The content analysis produced five explanation categories (plus a sixth for missing submissions):

1. **Category 1 – no explanation given**: nothing about causes, only harms, or a simplistic attribution (e.g., "AI is not very advanced"). The most common pre-test response (76 of 196, 38.8%), falling to 46 of 194 (23.7%) afterwards.
2. **Category 2 – human thinking or behavior blamed**: societal prejudice or stereotyping, with no mention of training data (e.g., a fourth grader wrote that some people treat soccer as a boys' pastime even though anyone can play). 70 responses (35.7%) pre-test; 45 (23.2%) post-test.
3. **Category 3 – AI's creators or users blamed**: design choices or prompting habits (e.g., a seventh grader guessed "the maker of AI" assumed only boys play soccer). 10 responses (5.1%) pre-test; just 3 (1.6%) post-test, all about prompting.
4. **Category 4 – anthropomorphized AI blamed**: human-like cognition or feelings (e.g., "Because it [AI] doesn't like girls"). 26 responses (13.3%) pre-test; 15 (7.7%) post-test.
5. **Category 5 – training data blamed**: over- or under-representation of groups in the data the system learned from (e.g., a seventh grader reasoned that because the AI learned from web pictures, and most show boys playing, it generates boys). A mere 14 responses (7.1%) pre-test; 85 (43.8%) post-test, some students deploying ML vocabulary (training, examples) and explicitly naming "bias."

The alluvial plot showed that children in every category except the fifth mostly switched explanation types, with most movers landing in Category 5 (28 children moved from Category 1 to Category 5). The chi-square test (Χ²(1) = 66.72) gave p < .0001 with a medium effect (Cramer's V = 0.40; 95% CI [0.32, 1.00]). Grade-level tests yielded Cramer's V = 0.41 (fourth graders) and 0.39 (seventh graders), both p < .001. A clear pre-test gap separated the grades: 1.6% of fourth graders versus 15.0% of seventh graders initially gave data-based accounts. Overall, 40.7% of children demonstrated a genuine grasp of bias rooted in the training process after the workshops, up from 6.7% beforehand.

### Discussion (pp. 5361–5363)

Pre-test responses showed children's starting ideas were absent, partial, or at odds with the content to be learned. Data-based accounts were scarce at the outset (7.1%) and 38.8% of children offered no explanation, indicating everyday experience had not supplied them with ready accounts of algorithmic bias. A third of the children initially blamed human thinking—applying what they already knew about societal bias—which touches root causes but shows they did not yet grasp how AI learns from data and decides on the basis of training sets. Anthropomorphic phrasing suggests children lacked scientific frameworks for explaining the phenomenon.

The results document conceptual change through hands-on activity: data-based accounts climbed to 43.8% afterwards. Nevertheless, a sizeable share of students still gave no or partial explanations, showing that everyday conceptions resist change and can obstruct the take-up of a more scientific explanatory framework. The findings back research showing children's reasoning can be supported through experiential, hands-on learning and creative making, in which children jointly explore key computer science ideas in meaningful, contextualized ways. Open-ended tasks let teams follow their own interests—spotting gender, appearance, age, and physical attributes in depictions of professions, sports, and wealth—and, guided by teachers and peers, many proposed data-based remedies, recognizing that training sets matter for countering over-, mis-, and under-representation. Strikingly, children explained bias in their own words rather than parroting facts, signaling genuine processing; some folded ML concepts and technique names into their reasoning. The authors argue that grasping ML's basic mechanisms can open critical reflection on AI's ethical and social consequences and foster more critical attitudes toward the technologies and power structures shaping daily life.

### Limitations and future research (pp. 5363–5364)

Limitations include the single-city Finnish sample, which may have shaped the results; future work should cover varied cultural backgrounds, different school contexts, and children needing special-education support, and add a control group that skips the workshop. Fourth graders retained more absent or partial explanations afterwards, suggesting activities, scaffolding, materials, and tools need adjusting by age. Future research should examine which epistemic activities helped or obstructed collaborative learning and knowledge creation in both groups. The authors close by arguing that, as AI permeates daily life and decision-making, research-based models and pedagogies are needed that give students the capacity to spot and dissect problems in socio-technical systems and to question critically the imaginaries generative AI reflects and may reinforce. Funding came from the Strategic Research Council (SRC) within the Research Council of Finland (Grants #352859 and #352876).

## GLOSSAR

- **Algorithmic bias** — prejudice embedded in AI systems, originating in factors from how data are gathered and which algorithms are chosen to developers' own biases, producing outputs that are unfair or discriminatory toward particular groups.
- **Text-to-image generative models** — software that converts a written description (a "prompt") into a corresponding picture.
- **Diffusion models** — neural-network methods that, trained on millions of labelled images, learn the statistically likely pixel patterns tied to textual labels and can synthesize new images from prompts.
- **Prompt** — the written description supplied to a text-to-image model to produce an image.
- **Data-driven explanation / data-driven reasoning** — an account of algorithmic bias (or mode of reasoning) that locates the problem in the system's training data—for instance, some groups appearing too often or too rarely.
- **GenAI Teachable Machine** — the educational tool built for this study, letting novice learners create and deploy AI-driven apps while learning core ML concepts and workflows.
- **ML workflow** — the sequence of epistemic steps in data-driven problem-solving: gathering data, cleaning and filtering it, labeling it, training a classifier, linking outputs to intended behaviors, and evaluating the model.
- **Design-oriented learning** — an instructional approach casting students as innovators and ML designers instead of consumers of ready-made products, working on open-ended tasks in small groups.
- **Naïve conceptions / misconceptions** — belief systems drawn from everyday experience and lay culture that can conflict with scientific explanations and impede conceptual change.
- **Anthropomorphic conceptions of AI** — the assumption that machines decide things the way humans do—thinking, acting, feeling.
- **Category 5 explanation** — the study's strongest response type, locating algorithmic bias in how the system was trained, e.g., over- or under-representation of groups in the training data.
