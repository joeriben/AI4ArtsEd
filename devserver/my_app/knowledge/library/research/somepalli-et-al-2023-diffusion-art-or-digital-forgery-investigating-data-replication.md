# Diffusion Art or Digital Forgery? Investigating Data Replication in Diffusion Models

## PROFIL

- The paper is by Somepalli et al. (Gowthami Somepalli, Vasu Singla, Micah Goldblum, Jonas Geiping, Tom Goldstein; University of Maryland, College Park and New York University), a research contribution aimed at the machine-learning and computer-vision community investigating generative image models.

- Its subject is whether diffusion models create novel works or replicate content from their training data; the authors informally define "content replication" as reproduction of training images in part or in whole, deliberately setting aside the ethical/legal question of whether replication is acceptable, fair use, or "stealing." Their position: replication happens frequently in models trained on small and medium datasets, appears undetectable in a model trained on large and diverse ImageNet, yet occurs clearly in Stable Diffusion, which sometimes "blatantly" copies training images, including verbatim reuse of objects and backgrounds and reconstructive memory where recalled objects are semantically equivalent without being pixel-wise identical.

- The paper first reviews three literatures—image retrieval and copy detection (including DINO and the state-of-the-art copy-detection model SSCD), memorization in deep learning (distinguishing memorization from replication, membership inference, model inversion), and memorization in language models, drawing on GAN studies by Meehan et al., Feng et al., and Webster et al.

- It then builds detection infrastructure: 5 synthetic datasets (IN-Cutmix, IN-Dif-Patch, IN-Dif-Diagonal, MSCOCO Segmix, VOC Segmix), 5 real retrieval datasets (Oxford and Paris, INSTRE, GPR1200, CUB-200), a benchmark of 10 feature extractors, and a split-product similarity metric; DINO with split-product performs best on average, Swin wins on real datasets, SSCD on synthetic ones.

- Experiments follow on DDPMs trained on Celeb-A and Oxford Flowers (blatant copying at 300 and 3000 images, unlikely exact copies on the full dataset), a case study of the ImageNet Latent Diffusion Model (no significant copying), and a case study of Stable Diffusion v1.4 (significant copying among the top 1.88 percentile of generations, key-phrase and style dependence, and replicated content coming from heavily duplicated training images).

- The authors attribute replication to a complex interaction of text conditioning, a highly skewed distribution of image repetitions, and enough gradient updates to overfit on a subset of the data, and conclude that copies occur often enough to be ignored unsafely, while noting the study systematically underestimates true replication.

- The paper is usable in arts education contexts addressing generative AI, authorship, intellectual property, and the ownership of diffusion images, and in disciplines spanning machine learning, computer vision, and the study of memorization in generative models.

## WISSEN

### Purpose and Thesis (pp. 1–2)

This paper by Somepalli et al. investigates whether diffusion models—generative image models that convert Gaussian noise into natural images—create genuinely novel works or instead replicate content directly from their training data. The authors informally define "content replication" as the reproduction of training images, either in part or in whole. They stress that whether replication is acceptable, fair use, or "stealing" is an ethical/legal question they deliberately set aside; their scientific questions are whether replication happens in state-of-the-art diffusion models, and to what degree. Their central findings: replication happens frequently in models trained on small and medium datasets, appears undetectable in a model trained on large and diverse ImageNet, yet occurs clearly in Stable Diffusion, which sometimes "blatantly" copies training images, including verbatim reuse of objects and backgrounds, and even reconstructive memory where recalled objects are semantically equivalent to sources without being pixel-wise identical (p. 1).

### Background and Related Work (pp. 2–3)

The authors review three literatures. **Image retrieval and copy detection**: searching a database for images sharing features with a source image; relevant methods include self-supervised models (DINO is noted as competitive on instance retrieval), vision-transformer backbones, and the state-of-the-art copy-detection model SSCD, which optimizes a descriptor with entropic regularization and task-specific augmentations. **Memorization in deep learning**: memorization has no universal definition; related problems include membership inference (determining whether an image was in the training set) and model inversion (reconstructing training images from a model). Crucially, the authors distinguish memorization from replication: a model might memorize data yet never spontaneously generate it. **Memorization in language models**: replication risk grows with model size, data duplication, and prompting, even without overfitting. Related GAN studies (Meehan et al., Feng et al., Webster et al.) found replication inversely proportional to dataset complexity/size; FID scores favor memorizing models; large-scale diffusion authors have reduced replication via data de-duplication (p. 2–3).

### Definition of Replication (p. 3)

The paper's working (informal) definition: a generated image has replicated content if it contains an object (foreground or background) that appears identically in a training image, neglecting minor variations attributable to data augmentation. Object-level similarity is chosen because objects are the likely subject of intellectual-property disputes; style-wise or semantic similarity is excluded as too subjective and typically not an infringement claim (p. 3).

### Detecting Content Replication: Datasets, Models, and Metrics (pp. 3–5)

Because no labeled datasets capture this notion of replication, the authors create **5 synthetic datasets**: (1) IN-Cutmix (random square patches from one ImageNet image pasted into another); (2) IN-Dif-Patch (masking 80% of an image except a random patch, then outpainting via Lugmayr et al.'s method); (3) IN-Dif-Diagonal (masking a random triangular half and outpainting); (4) MSCOCO Segmix and (5) VOC Segmix (using segmentation masks to select an object or background, applying augmentations—flips, blur, autocontrast, solarize, colorjitter—before pasting into another image; foreground objects are also resized and repositioned). They also use **5 real retrieval datasets**: Oxford and Paris (geographic landmarks, cleaned versions), INSTRE (objects in varied conditions), GPR1200 (1200 classes from Google Landmarks V2, Stanford Online Products, IMDB-WIKI and others), and CUB-200 (fine-grained bird classes) (pp. 3–4).

They benchmark **10 feature extractors** in three categories: copy detection/instance retrieval (MultiGrain ResNet-50; SSCD ResNet-50), pre-trained (ViT-S/16 on ImageNet; ViT-B/16 on ImageNet-21k; CLIP ViT-B/32 on LAION; Swin Transformer on ImageNet), and self-supervised (MoCo v3 ViT-B/16; MoCo v3 fine-tuned with CutMix; VICRegL ResNet-50; DINO ViT-B/16) (p. 4).

For similarity computation, they note that standard inner products measure global similarity and can fail when a generated image steals content from two unrelated images (a triangle-inequality argument). They therefore implement a **split-product metric**: break each feature vector into chunks (vision-transformer tokens), compute inner products between corresponding chunks, and return the maximum. Qualitatively, split-product is more semantic; inner product enforces stricter pixel-wise similarity. Performance is measured with mAP, Recall@k, and MRR (p. 4).

**Results (Table 1)**: DINO with split-product performs best on average across all 10 datasets (average rank 4.1, tied with Swin Transformer); Swin wins on real datasets; SSCD wins on synthetic datasets (e.g., 99.91 mAP on IN-Dif-Diagonal, 96.11 on IN-Dif-Outpaint). The authors proceed with SSCD, Swin, and DINO split-product (pp. 4–5).

### Do Diffusion Models Copy? DDPM Experiments (pp. 5–6)

They train DDPMs (HuggingFace implementation) on Celeb-A (300 and 3000 training images, plus the official full-dataset checkpoint) and Oxford Flowers (100, 1083 [top 5 classes], and 8189 [complete dataset] images), with random horizontal flip and crop, trained at least 300k steps until FID < 50; analysis covers 10000 Celeb-A and 5000 Flowers generations. **Qualitatively**: models trained on 300 and 3000 images blatantly copy; the full-dataset model produces similar-but-not-identical images; the 1083-image Flowers model already creates unique minor variations. **Quantitatively** (histograms of top-1 similarity vs. a training-data self-similarity baseline): the 300-sample model's generations are extremely similar to training data; the histogram shifts drastically left with 3000 points; with the full dataset, generated and self-similarity histograms strongly overlap and the generated histogram loses its long right tail, indicating exact copies are unlikely. A small proportion of training self-similarity scores exceed 0.9, revealing repetitions in the training data itself (pp. 5–6).

### Case Study: ImageNet Latent Diffusion Model (p. 6)

Using the pre-trained class-conditional Latent Diffusion Model on ImageNet, 100 random classes with 1000 generations each, they find no significant copying: similarity scores never cross 0.65, and high-similarity examples are very similar but never exact copies, explainable by low intra-class diversity. Classes with higher self-similarity have higher maximum generation-match similarity (correlation 0.6, best-fit slope 0.39). Highest-similarity classes: theater curtain, peacock, bananas; lowest: sea lion, shopping cart, bee, swing (p. 6).

### Case Study: Stable Diffusion (pp. 7–10)

They evaluate Stable Diffusion v1.4, trained on over 2B LAION images then fine-tuned on 600M LAION Aesthetics v2 5+ images; for storage reasons they search only the 12M-image LAION Aesthetics v2 6+ subset. They sample 9000 source images/captions from LAION Aesthetics 12M and generate one image per caption (guidance scale 7.5, strength 0.5, 50 steps). Findings (pp. 7–9):

- Among ~170 images (top 1.88 percentile) with top-1 SSCD similarity > 0.5, significant copying occurs: verbatim reuse of object and background (e.g., a generation differing from its match only in the painting), and local copying where only a background is recycled.
- No generation matched its own caption source image; sometimes the source caption was unrepresentative of the source image, and occasionally the match image better represented the source caption.
- Replication depends on key phrases: the phrase "Canvas Wall Art Print" frequently (≈20% of the time) produced a particular sofa from LAION; prompts derived from "The Great Wave off Kanagawa by Katsushika Hokusai," even reduced to just "painting" and "wave," still produced wave structures resembling the original.
- Style copying: prompts of the form "style '<Name of the painting> by <Name of the artist>'" for ~20 classical and contemporary artists frequently reproduced known paintings with varying accuracy; content copying decreased left-to-right in their figure while style copying persisted (e.g., "The Scream" by Edvard Munch was generated from an image in the 600M Aesthetics 5+ split outside the searched 12M split).
- Caption sampling: mean generation similarity scores are much lower than source-image similarity scores, so LAION caption sampling does not typically produce replicas of the caption source—though key phrases in LAION captions may raise replication rates relative to other sampling methods, and experienced users exploit such phrases ("art station," "35mm," artist names).
- Duplicate training data: a typical random LAION image is duplicated 11.6 times (duplicate = SSCD score > 0.95), a typical matched image 3.1 times, but very close matches (> 0.5 SSCD) are replicated on average 34.1 times—replicated content tends to come from heavily duplicated training images (pp. 9–10).

### Possible Causes of Replication (p. 10)

Since ImageNet LDM and Stable Diffusion use similar update rules, training routines, and parameter counts, those factors are ruled out. Duplication alone is an incomplete explanation: the small-dataset models in Section 5 replicated strongly without duplicates, and a dataset with all images duplicated 1000 times should yield the same model as unique images given the same number of updates. The authors speculate replication arises from a complex interaction of factors: text (rather than class) conditioning, a highly skewed distribution of image repetitions in the training set, and enough gradient updates to overfit on a subset of the data (p. 10).

### Limitations and Conclusion (pp. 10–11)

Diffusion models are capable of reproducing high-fidelity training content. Typical images from large-scale models do not show detectable copied content, but copies occur often enough to be ignored unsafely: Stable Diffusion generations with dataset similarity ≥ 0.5 account for approximately 1.88% of random generations. The study systematically underestimates true replication because (1) only the 12M LAION Aesthetics v2 6+ split was searched—less than 0.6% of total training data—and replication from outside this split certainly exists (e.g., "The Scream" example), and (2) the retrieval method likely misses some replication (pp. 10–11).

## GLOSSAR

- **Content replication** — the reproduction of training images, either in part or in whole, by a generative model.
- **Replicated content (definition)** — a generated image contains an object (foreground or background) that appears identically in a training image, neglecting minor variations explainable by data augmentation.
- **Reconstructive memory** — behavior in which recalled objects are semantically equivalent to their source without being pixel-wise identical.
- **Split-product metric** — a similarity measure that breaks feature vectors into chunks (e.g., vision-transformer tokens), computes inner products between corresponding chunks, and returns the maximum, enabling local rather than global similarity.
- **Source images** — the 9000 random images sampled from LAION Aesthetics 12M whose captions are used to generate synthetic images.
- **Match images** — the top-1 training-set matches retrieved for each generated image.
- **IN-Cutmix** — synthetic dataset built by pasting random square patches from one ImageNet image into another.
- **IN-Dif-Patch** — synthetic dataset created by masking 80% of an image except a random square patch and outpainting the rest with diffusion.
- **IN-Dif-Diagonal** — synthetic dataset made by masking a random triangular half of an ImageNet image and outpainting it.
- **MSCOCO Segmix / VOC Segmix** — synthetic datasets using segmentation masks to select an object or background, augmenting it, and pasting it into another random image.
- **Key phrases** — caption phrases closely associated with dataset images that conjure memorized images (e.g., "Canvas Wall Art Print," "art station," "35mm").
- **Duplicate** — a training image appearing multiple times, defined as having SSCD score > 0.95 with another image.
