# Social Biases through the Text-to-Image Generation Lens

## PROFIL

- Written by Ranjita Naik and Besmira Nushi (Microsoft), the article "Social Biases through the Text-to-Image Generation Lens" is a research study aimed at technology and policy makers concerned with the responsible development of generative models.
- Its subject is representational social bias in two Text-to-Image generation models, DALLE-v2 and Stable Diffusion v1, and its central claim is that, because these models are trained on massive, non-curated web data, harmful biases leak into the generation process itself, constituting a "major setback on representational fairness" compared even to image search.
- The paper opens with purpose, thesis, and a motivating "CEO or housekeeper problem"; a related-work section covering social bias in image search (Kay et al.), Text-to-Image generative architectures, and prior bias studies of T2I models (Cho et al., Bianchi et al., Zhang et al.), against which it positions its broader four-dimension, four-subject design; a methodology section defining bias dimensions (perceived gender, race, age, geographical location), prompt types (person, occupations, traits, situations), and both crowdsourced (Mechanical Turk) and automated (FairFace, Azure Cognitive Services, CLIP) evaluation; a results section covering neutral and expanded prompts, image quality via Fréchet Inception Distance, personality traits, everyday situations, and human-versus-automated agreement; a limitations section; and a conclusion, followed by an appendix with occupation and trait lists, correlation tables, distribution figures, heat maps, and the annotation interface.
- Headline findings include near-0% women for "CEO" and "computer programmer" in DALLE-v2, near-100% women for "nurse" and "housekeeper" in SD, competence traits attributed primarily to men and warm traits to women, and lowest country representation for Nigeria, Ethiopia, and Papua New Guinea; prompt expansion is shown to be an imperfect mitigation that can introduce new biases and quality discrepancies.
- For arts education, the article is usable in media and digital arts, visual culture, and critical AI literacy contexts, wherever students and educators examine how generative image systems construct representations of gender, race, age, and place, or compare machine-generated imagery against real-world references such as labor statistics and image search results.

## WISSEN

### Purpose and Thesis (p. 1)

This paper, by Ranjita Naik and Besmira Nushi (Microsoft), systematically quantifies representational social biases in two popular Text-to-Image (T2I) generation models, DALLE-v2 and Stable Diffusion (SD) v1. The authors argue that because these models are trained on massive, non-curated web data, harmful biases leak into the generation process itself, creating a "confirmatory process that can propagate known issues in ways that can erase or undo previous mitigation efforts." The study examines how occupations, personality traits, and everyday situations are depicted across four social bias dimensions: (perceived) gender, race, age, and geographical location, using both automated and crowdsourced human evaluation. A key motivating example is the "CEO or housekeeper problem": occupations stereotypically associated with men (CEO) or women (housekeeper). The authors compare three views of such occupations: real-world distributions from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), search engine results (Google Image Search), and image generation results. Their headline finding: image generation models are a "major setback on representational fairness" compared even to image search — e.g., DALLE-v2 shows almost 0% women for "CEO" and "computer programmer," while Stable Diffusion shows almost 100% women for "nurse" and "housekeeper." Results are intended to inform technology and policy makers.

### Related Work (p. 2–4)

The paper situates itself in three strands of research:

1. **Social bias in image search.** Prior work (Kay et al., cited as [13]) compared gender representation in image search results for occupational queries against U.S. BLS 2015 data, finding that results slightly magnified gender stereotypes (an occupation with 50% women in BLS would show about 45% women in results on average) and portrayed the less-represented gender less professionally. A follow-up study ([19]) found women still underrepresented in 2020 and individuals of color also underrepresented. The present work shows T2I models are "a step back" from even image search.
2. **Text-to-Image generative models.** DALLE-v2 uses CLIP embeddings to bridge text and visual domains, with denoising in pixel space (computationally intensive); Stable Diffusion runs denoising in latent space, enabling high-quality generation on low-cost GPUs. The paper studies both as representatives of pixel-space and latent-space approaches.
3. **Social bias in T2I models.** Cho et al. [5] evaluated gender and skin-tone skews from neutral occupation prompts, finding Stable Diffusion more prone than minDALL-E to producing a single gender or skin tone. Bianchi et al. [3] showed Stable Diffusion perpetuates racial, ethnic, gendered, class, and intersectional stereotypes, exhibits stereotype amplification, and links specific groups to negative associations (malnourishment, poverty, subordination) that neither model "guardrails" nor carefully expanded prompts lessen. Zhang et al. [29] studied gender presentation differences (e.g., "a shirt" vs. "a dress") across DALLE-v2, CogView2, and Stable Diffusion, finding DALLE-v2 presents genders more similarly than the others.

The authors' contribution beyond prior work (summarized in Table 2): studying two models across four bias dimensions (gender, race, age, location), four bias subjects (person, occupations, traits, situations), and quantifying the impact of expanded prompts, using both human and automated evaluation.

### Methodology (p. 4–6)

**Social bias dimensions.** Because images are computer-generated, the study annotates *perceived* attributes:
- **Gender:** a deliberately simplistic binary specification (male/female), acknowledged as not capturing non-binary definitions.
- **Age:** four groups — "Child or minor," "Adult 18-40," "Adult 40-60," "Adult over 60."
- **Race:** the seven FairFace classifications — White, Black, Indian, East Asian, Southeast Asian, Middle Eastern, and Latino. The paper distinguishes race (a social construct based on physical characteristics) from ethnicity (cultural background, traditions, language, history).

**Bias subjects and prompts.** Four prompt types were used: person, occupation, traits, and everyday situations. Prompt prefixes were tuned per model: "a portrait of a [prompt]" for DALLE-v2 and "a photo of a [prompt]" for SD, chosen for generating salient human faces. Expanded prompts added explicit gender (e.g., "a female doctor"), race (e.g., "a black author"), or age (e.g., "a junior biologist") to occupation prompts to test prompt expansion as a mitigation strategy.
- **Occupations:** compared against U.S. BLS 2022 labor statistics as a real-world reference (noting this reflects U.S. representation only and that matching BLS does not imply fairness, since real-world distributions are themselves biased). The list (appendix Table 6) was adapted from previous work [18]; abbreviations used: CP (computer programmer), PST (primary school teacher), CSR (customer service representative).
- **Person:** the prompt "person."
- **Personality traits:** trait adjectives from Abele et al. [1], uniform in valence and frequency across languages, partitioned into positive and negative traits (appendix Table 7).
- **Everyday situations:** default and location-specific prompts across six categories — events, food, institutions, clothing, places, and community — using the two most populous countries per continent (excluding Antarctica): The United States, China, India, Nigeria, Ethiopia, Russia, Germany, Mexico, Brazil, Colombia, Australia, and Papua New Guinea. Template: "a [situation] in [country]" (e.g., "a library in Brazil," "breakfast in Ethiopia").

**Model and data.** Images came from OpenAI's DALLE-v2 API and the Stable Diffusion v1 repository. The study used the first 50 images featuring humans (detected via Azure Cognitive Services Analyze Image API and FairFace) for person, occupation, and traits prompts, and 250 images for everyday situations and expanded occupation prompts (evaluated automatically).

**Evaluation.**
- *Human evaluation:* Amazon Mechanical Turk, three annotators per image, average wage $12/hour, annotators with Master's qualification (low-quality annotators excluded in a pilot). Majority vote (2 or 3 agreeing) determined the label; full disagreement yielded "unclear" and exclusion. Annotators indicated whether they saw cartoons, humans, or no humans, plus gender, race, and age; 37 example images with FairFace ground-truth annotations were provided for guidance.
- *Automated evaluation:* FairFace (108,501 images, balanced race composition, sourced from YFCC-100M Flickr) with a ResNet-based pre-trained model; faces detected via dlib's CNN-based face detector. Azure Cognitive Services checked for non-empty "faces" features or human-referencing "tags" (e.g., "man," "woman," "girl," "child").
- *Everyday situations:* average CLIP embeddings computed across default and location-specific generations; the distance between them measures country representation (lower distance = closer to default representation), visualized as heat maps.

### Results (p. 6–11)

**What does a "person" look like? (p. 6–7).** For the prompt "person": DALLE-v2 generated 70% male vs. 30% female images; SD generated 66% female vs. 28% male. Both models showed racial bias toward white individuals (minimum 70% of images). DALLE-v2 failed to represent East Asian, Southeast Asian, or Middle Eastern individuals; SD did not portray Latino or Middle Eastern individuals. SD showed more varied age representation, while DALLE-v2 depicted 76% of images as adults aged 18-40.

**Occupations — neutral prompts (p. 7–8).** After filtering out prompts that generated too few individuals (e.g., "a garbage collector," "a truck driver" yielding trucks/containers), obstructed faces (e.g., photographers behind cameras), or caricatures (e.g., "a tax collector"), 44 occupations were identified for analysis (results text refers to 43 analyzed).
- *Gender:* DALLE-v2 reinforced under-representation of women in technical writer, optician, bartender, and bus driver, and over-representation in customer service representative, primary school teacher, and telemarketer; only 8 of 43 occupations fell within ±5% of BLS proportions. SD reinforced under-representation in technical writer, bartender, telemarketer, and custodian, and over-representation in PR person, pilot, police officer, and author; only 7 of 43 occupations fell within ±5% of BLS. Correlations with BLS on the proportion of women: 0.84 (DALLE-v2) and 0.87 (SD).
- *Race:* For DALLE-v2, white individuals were under-represented by more than 50% in childcare worker, announcer, nurse, and housekeeper; only Pilot and Primary School Teacher matched BLS for white workers. 72% of DALLE-v2 occupations had zero representation of black individuals; for SD, 37% of occupations had zero black representation, with childcare worker over-represented by 48% and telemarketer under-represented by 21%. For SD, white individuals were under-represented by more than 50% in construction worker, childcare worker, and housekeeper; nurse was the only occupation matching BLS.
- *Age:* For DALLE-v2, administrative assistant, customer service representative, receptionist, electrician, and nurse were dominated by ages 18-40 (minimum 96%); truck driver and CEO were dominated by ages 40-60 (minimum 78%); over-60 individuals were prominent in clergy member and tax collector. For SD, bartender, computer programmer, telemarketer, and electrician were dominated by ages 18-40 (minimum 98%); CEO, custodian, and clergy member by ages 40-60 (minimum 60%); over-60 individuals prominent in bus driver.

**Expanded prompts (p. 8–9).** Prompt expansion is an imperfect mitigation:
- *Gender:* Even with specific prompts, 5% of DALLE-v2 images for "male childcare worker" or "male primary school teacher" were female, and at least 5% of images for "female security guard" or "female custodian" were male. For SD, "a female police officer," "a female roofer," "a female cook," and "a drafter" contained 24%, 19%, 10%, and 10% male images respectively; "a male administrative assistant," "a male receptionist," "a male housekeeper," and "a male paralegal" generated 60%, 53%, 25%, and 18% female images.
- *Race:* At least 9% of DALLE-v2 images for "black mail carrier" and "black crane operator" were of white individuals; "East Asian garbage collector" images were mostly of individuals from Southeast Asia. For SD, "a Middle Eastern special ed teacher" produced 17% white and 21% Indian individuals.
- *Age:* 4% of DALLE-v2 images for "a junior receptionist" and "a junior childcare worker" depicted seniors. For SD, "a junior crane operator," "a junior electrician," and "a junior plumber" generated over-60 individuals at least 10% of the time.
- Overall, SD ignores gender, race, and age specific prompts more frequently than DALLE-v2. The authors recommend complementary mitigation strategies such as duplicate prompting and output filters.

**Image quality evaluation (p. 9).** Using Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) against curated real image search results for gendered occupational prompts, the study found that gender-skewed representations exhibit greater similarity with real-world images: quality is higher when gendered prompts use the gender most represented in neutral prompts, and lower otherwise. For DALLE-v2, male-dominated occupations (CEO, crane operator, roofer, bus driver) had better FID scores for male prompts, and female-dominated occupations (nurse, childcare worker, primary school teacher, administrative assistant) had better FID scores for female prompts. The illustrative case of "female announcer" shows prompt expansion addressing gender bias but introducing racial bias (images dominated by East Asian individuals) with worse FID (237 vs. 164 for "male announcer" and 201 for neutral "announcer"); lack of output diversity may drive DALLE-v2's worse quality scores.

**Personality traits (p. 9).** Because SD generated non-human images for over 50% of certain trait prompts, this analysis was limited to DALLE-v2 with human evaluation. Competence-related traits — "intelligent," "strong-minded," "rational" — were primarily attributed to men; women were most associated with warm traits — "affectionate," "warm," "sensitive" (Table 3 lists top male traits including boastful, energetic, egoistic, dogmatic, decisive, rational, strong-minded, striving, industrious, intelligent, gullible, moral, reliable, self-critical; top female traits: sensitive, affectionate, harmonious, supportive, warm). Racially, "ambitious" and "determined" associated most strongly with the black race, "vigorous" and "detached" with East Asian. The white race was more associated with positive traits ("competent," "active," "rational," "sympathetic") but lower on "ambition," "vigorous," and "striving"; it was strongly linked with negative traits "dominant" and "egoistic" but less represented for "detached" and "hardheaded." By age: caring and altruistic behaviors generated more over-60 individuals; rationality and tolerance associated with ages 40-60; laziness, ambition, and perfectionism with ages 18-40.

**Everyday situations (p. 9–10).** Using CLIP-embedding distances across the six categories and 12 countries: Nigeria, Ethiopia, and Papua New Guinea had the lowest representation in both models; Australia, Germany, and the United States were most represented. Germany was the most represented country for DALLE-v2; the United States for SD. DALLE-v2 images were generally more representative of all countries studied. The paper also illustrates (Figure 4, "Office in Ethiopia") that both models depict Ethiopia in poor economic conditions compared to image search results.

**Human vs. automated evaluation (p. 10).** Correlations between human and automated evaluation were greater than 0.9 for all groups except white individuals in the occupation category: for neutral occupations, gender 1.0 (DALLE-v2) / 0.99 (SD); race-white 0.87 / 0.89; race-black 0.99 / 0.98; age 18-40 0.95 / 0.91; age 40-60 0.95 / 0.91. For DALLE-v2 traits: gender 0.99, race-white 0.93, race-black 0.98, age 18-40 0.91, age 40-60 0.9. Correlations could not be meaningfully computed for significantly under-represented groups (under 18, over 60, and races other than black and white), so automated evaluation effectiveness for these groups is inconclusive.

### Limitations (p. 11)

The authors note the work does not address non-binary gender definitions or under-represented communities such as individuals with disabilities, smaller countries, and religious groups; example-based evidence suggests such groups are poorly represented in neutral prompts, but deeper analysis is needed on whether prompt expansion can mitigate this and at what cost. Prompt expansion may not solve biases leading to image quality discrepancies or complex associations requiring qualitative evaluation (e.g., prior work [3] showed country prompts represent some countries in poor economic status, as in Figure 4). Risks exist that generations for non-binary or religious groups could carry harmful stereotypes. Only two models (DALLE-v2 and SD v1) were evaluated; further work is needed on proprietary models (e.g., Imagen) and newer releases (e.g., Stable Diffusion v2).

### Conclusion (p. 12)

The study measured biases in DALLE-v2 and Stable Diffusion v1 across gender, race, age, and geographical location using prompts for occupations, personality traits, everyday situations, and "person." Both models exhibited significant biases across all dimensions and even exacerbated them relative to BLS labor statistics. Prompt expansion can diversify generated content but introduces variations in image quality. Some countries were under-represented in everyday situations while others were over-represented. The authors plan to explore more mitigation strategies and intend the results and method to inform the evaluation and responsible development of new generative models with increased focus on representational fairness.

## GLOSSAR

- **Social bias dimensions** — the four axes along which bias is measured — gender, race, age, and geographical location.
- **Bias subjects** — the four prompt types studied — person, occupations, personality traits, and everyday situations.
- **Neutral (default) prompts** — prompts that do not specify gender, age, race, or location, used to analyze bias without the interference of prompt crafting.
- **Expanded prompts** — prompts augmented with explicit gender, race, or age specifications (e.g., "a female doctor," "a black author," "a junior biologist"), used to estimate the effectiveness of mitigation techniques for generation or search.
- **Prompt expansion mitigation strategy** — the technique of diversifying outputs by adding demographic specifications to prompts; the paper shows it may not fully mitigate bias, can introduce new biases, and can cause image-quality discrepancies.
- **Perceived attributes** — discrete demographic labels (gender, race, age) assigned to computer-generated people, since the images do not involve actual individuals.
- **Everyday situations** — prompts describing common scenes across six categories — events, food, institutions, clothing, places, and community — generated both by default and with country locations ("a [situation] in [country]").
- **Race vs. ethnicity** — race is defined as a social construct based on physical characteristics (skin color, hair texture, facial features); ethnicity refers to cultural background including traditions, language, and history — related but not interchangeable.
- **FID (Fréchet Inception Distance)** — the metric used to compute the similarity between AI-generated and real-world image datasets, using Inception V3 features trained on ImageNet; lower FID indicates better resemblance to real images.
- **Unclear label** — the label assigned to an image when all three human annotators produced different responses; such images were excluded from the study.
- **Confirmatory process** — the cycle by which biases from web data resurface in AI-generated content, propagating known issues in ways that can erase or undo previous mitigation efforts.
