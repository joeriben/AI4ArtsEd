# AI competency framework for students

## PROFIL

UNESCO's *AI competency framework for students* (AI CFS), issued in 2024 as the first framework of this type at global level, was produced together with a companion framework for teachers and is written for policymakers, curriculum developers, providers of AI education programmes, school leaders, teachers and educational experts, to be adapted to local conditions. It responds to the finding that, as of 2022, only around 15 countries had incorporated AI learning objectives into national curricula, and that private companies' training frequently shapes how student AI competencies are defined while public frameworks are largely absent; it offers an international reference point, guided by a vision of students as co-creators of AI and responsible citizens and anchored in human rights, inclusion and equity. It builds on UNESCO's ICT competency framework for teachers, *AI and education: Guidance for policy-makers*, the *Guidance for generative AI in education and research*, the Beijing Consensus on AI and Education, and the Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence. After an introduction and a chapter of key principles — fostering a critical approach to AI, prioritizing human-centred interaction, encouraging environmentally sustainable AI, promoting inclusivity, and building core AI competencies for lifelong learning — the framework sets out 12 competencies in a matrix of four aspects (Human-centred mindset, Ethics of AI, AI techniques and applications, AI system design) crossed with three progression levels (Understand, Apply, Create), resting on the pillars of knowledge, skills and values. Chapter 4 specifies each competency block through student competency, curricular goals, suggested pedagogical methods and learning environments, including unplugged settings; Chapter 5 addresses application: aligning AI competencies with national AI strategies (with the Republic of Korea's strategy as an example), building interdisciplinary core and cluster AI curricula (with the United Arab Emirates' Computing, Creative Design and Innovation curriculum as an example), framing future-proofing and locally feasible AI domains as carriers of the curriculum, tailoring age-appropriate spiral curricular sequences under a paradigm shift to competency-based education, and building enabling learning environments. The framework is usable for schooling, technical and vocational education and training, higher education, employee upskilling and reskilling, and lifelong learning, across disciplines including mathematics, science, engineering, languages, social studies, art, civic and citizenship education and history, with the AI system design aspect aimed at students with a particular interest in the field.

## WISSEN

### Publication context and short summary (p. 3–4)

This document is UNESCO's *AI competency framework for students* (AI CFS), issued in 2024 (ISBN 978-92-3-100709-5) and the first framework of this type at global level. It was produced together with a companion framework for teachers and draws on earlier UNESCO publications: the ICT competency framework for teachers, *AI and education: Guidance for policy-makers*, and the *Guidance for generative AI in education and research*. The framework sets out 12 competencies organized under four aspects — Human-centred mindset, Ethics of AI, AI techniques and applications, and AI system design — each developed through three levels of mastery: Understand, Apply, and Create. Its guiding vision sees students as co-creators of AI and responsible citizens. A motivating statistic: as of 2022, just 15 countries had incorporated AI learning objectives into national curricula.

### Foreword (p. 6)

UNESCO Assistant Director-General for Education Stefania Giannini frames the framework against the backdrop of AI's rapid spread, accelerated when generative AI tools became publicly available in November 2022. She contends that schooling must equip learners to act as "active co-creators of AI, as well as future leaders who will shape novel iterations of the technology." The framework is anchored in human rights, inclusion and equity, and was shaped by input from Member States, an international working group, three international consultation meetings, and several rounds of online consultation.

### Chapter 1: Introduction — why an AI competency framework for students? (p. 12–13)

Education's role is not to serve as a passive proving ground for AI; rather, it must empower young people to help build sustainable futures. The 2019 UNESCO Beijing Consensus on AI and Education already recognized the need for AI literacy, yet a survey covering 190 countries found only around 15 countries developing or implementing AI curricula in schools, with definitions of AI literacy, skills and competency varying widely (UNESCO, 2022b). Frequently, private companies' training — oriented toward technical operation of commercial platforms — shapes how student AI competencies are defined, and public frameworks are largely absent. The AI CFS addresses this gap by offering an international reference point.

It pursues three aims: (1) to serve as a global reference on core student AI competencies that can guide national or institutional frameworks; (2) to describe typical attitudinal and behavioural performance at each mastery level to support curriculum design; and (3) to suggest an open-ended roadmap for sequencing AI learning across grades. It is written for policymakers, curriculum developers, providers of AI education programmes, school leaders, teachers and educational experts, and is meant to be adapted to local conditions — curricula, learning environments, teacher preparedness and students' starting knowledge.

### Chapter 2: Key principles (p. 14–17)

**2.1 Fostering a critical approach to AI.** Learners should confront basic questions: does AI help solve real human problems, or does it threaten humanity irreversibly? Do the climate costs of building and running AI outweigh the expected gains? Which social, economic, political and demographic consequences of AI deserve scrutiny? Rather than assuming AI can fix anything, students must learn to judge for themselves when AI is appropriate, when it is not, and what it can and cannot solve. At a level suited to their age, they should also take part in shaping AI regulation.

**2.2 Prioritizing human-centred interaction with AI.** UNESCO's human-centric position holds that AI should strengthen human capabilities, safeguard dignity and agency, and advance justice and sustainability throughout the AI life cycle, with human rights principles and respect for linguistic and cultural diversity as guides. AI must never displace critical thinking. Students should learn what data AI gathers about them, how such data trains models, and what this cycle means for privacy — so they neither become addicted to nor dependent on AI, and so humans stay answerable for consequential decisions.

**2.3 Encouraging environmentally sustainable AI.** Students must grasp how profit-driven approaches to designing, training and deploying AI damage the environment. Sustainability is frequently sidelined, or even deliberately hidden behind assertions that AI will solve climate change. Learning should involve project-based investigation of the environmental costs of AI use and training, and exploration of mitigation options.

**2.4 Promoting inclusivity in AI competency development.** Access to AI and AI competencies together form citizens' basic rights in today's world. Inclusivity must run through the whole AI life cycle: choosing representative data, selecting bias-agnostic algorithms and anti-discrimination training methods, building accessible functionalities, testing outputs for inclusiveness, and assessing effects on social inclusion. Any AI tool recommended for education must pass rigorous public validation to screen out biases tied to gender, ability, socio-economic status, language, ethnicity and/or culture; absent such validation, no specific tool should be recommended at scale. Governments should guarantee inclusive access to connectivity, up-to-date devices, and open-source or affordable AI programmes and software, paying special attention to students with disabilities and those from linguistic or cultural minorities.

**2.5 Building core AI competencies for lifelong learning.** Core competencies rest on values of an ethical, human-centred mindset; understanding of specific rights — equality, non-discrimination, privacy, plural expression; and hands-on skills for countering bias, safeguarding privacy, upholding transparency and accountability, and working in an ethics-by-design mode. Because the competencies are brand-agnostic and product-agnostic, they prepare students for any current tool and for future AI generations, linking AI concepts to real-world problems.

### Chapter 3: Structure of the framework (p. 18–26)

The framework takes the form of a matrix: four aspects crossed with three progression levels, yielding twelve competency blocks. AI competency rests on three pillars — knowledge, skills and values.

**The four aspects:**

1. **Human-centred mindset** — values, beliefs and critical thinking about whether AI is fit for purpose, comprising: *Human agency* (recognizing that AI is human-led and that human control matters, including what happens when it is absent), *Human accountability* (creators' and providers' legal obligations; humans must not hand consequential choices over to AI), and *Citizenship in the era of AI* (critical awareness of AI's societal effects; civic responsibility; lifelong learning for self-actualization).

2. **Ethics of AI** — comprising: *Embodied ethics* (grasping key ethical debates and internalizing six principles: do no harm — rejecting, for instance, facial recognition used for surveillance or social-status scoring, or exam-grading by predictive algorithms; proportionality; non-discrimination — spotting gender, ethnic, cultural and other biases as well as AI divides within and between countries; sustainability; human determination in human-AI collaboration; transparency and explainability — users' right to explanatory information from designers and providers), *Safe and responsible use* (acting in line with ethical principles and applicable regulations; giving deliberate, informed consent for every stage of data handling — collection, use, sharing, archiving, deletion), and *Ethics by design* (checking AI tools against ethical regulations and reviewing regulations across every step of the AI life cycle, beginning at conceptualization).

3. **AI techniques and applications** — comprising: *AI foundations* (basic knowledge of data and algorithms; interdisciplinary grounding), *Application skills* (age-appropriate grasp of data, AI algorithms and programming; critical evaluation of free and/or open-source AI tools, programming libraries and datasets), and *Creating AI tools* (adapting existing AI toolkits into task-based tools; bringing human-centred and ethical thinking to the assessment of available resources; social and emotional skills such as adaptivity, complex communication and teamwork).

4. **AI system design** — aimed at students with a particular interest in the field, comprising: *Problem scoping* (judging from legal, ethical and logical standpoints whether AI belongs in a situation; setting a problem's boundaries, goals and constraints before any model is trained), *Architecture design* (building scalable, maintainable, reusable architectures spanning data, algorithms, models and application interfaces; constructing prototype systems), and *Iteration and feedback* (judging a model's appropriateness, robustness and impact on users, societies and the environment; improving datasets and reconfiguring algorithms; rehearsing decisions about shutting a system down and mitigating harm; growing an identity as a co-creator within the AI community).

**The three progression levels:**

- **Level 1: Understand** — intended for every student; building age-appropriate interpretations of the values, ethical questions, concepts, processes and technical methods behind AI tools.
- **Level 2: Apply** — also relevant for all students; extending, transferring and adapting what has been learned to more complex contexts. For many learners this is where school-based AI competency development ends.
- **Level 3: Create** — students work as conscientious AI co-creators, building human-centred solutions and new AI tools using open-source and/or customizable datasets, programming tools or AI models. This level demands substantial curriculum time (e.g. one or more full semesters) and, especially for the AI system design aspect, should be elective rather than compulsory for students not pursuing deeper study.

The matrix serves four functions: (1) scoping focus areas and target mastery levels according to local AI readiness and instructional time; (2) identifying AI content that can be woven into existing curricula, subjects and grades; (3) setting proficiency levels and assessment criteria; and (4) inspiring age-appropriate, domain-specific, agile teaching methods.

### Chapter 4: Specifications of AI competencies — Level 1: Understand (p. 27–36)

The specifications assume that competencies emerge from combined influences: national curricula, extracurricular programmes, informal learning via media including the internet, and family and community engagement. AI-related learning — whether a standalone subject or modules within computer science or ICT — needs sufficient instructional time within a semester, ideally spanning several semesters. Each competency block is described through student competency, curricular goals (CG), suggested pedagogical methods, and learning environments.

**4.1.1 Human agency (p. 29–30).** Curricular goals: CG4.1.1.1 establish that AI is human-led (covering data ownership and privacy, protection of human rights in data processing, explainability of AI methods, human control in deployment, human determination in decision-making; AI cannot substitute for human thinking or intellectual growth); CG4.1.1.2 build understanding of why humans must retain control over AI (through real-world scenarios such as weak regulation permitting harmful AI tools, institutions delegating high-stakes decisions to AI, and unchecked AI outputs); CG4.1.1.3 develop critical thinking about how human and machine agency interact (including cases where some machine agency helps: detecting medical patterns beyond human doctors' reach in diagnosing rare diseases, spell check and autocorrection, auto captioning and automated video production for course materials, automatic translation). Pedagogical methods include concept maps of human agency across the AI life cycle; a simulated courtroom debate in which students act as jurors on AI systems banned under the European Union's AI Act; scenario work on human-controlled interaction with AI; and debates on the shifting boundary between human and machine agency. Learning environments: unplugged settings (paper-based articles, printed materials, worksheets), locally available AI tools including mobile phones, pre-downloaded videos, search engines and online courses.

**4.1.2 Embodied ethics (p. 31–32).** Curricular goals: CG4.1.2.1 present AI-related dilemmas and uncover the roots of ethical conflicts (e.g. scaling up data collection versus protecting data ownership; recording users' private data for model training versus safeguarding privacy; favouring machine control for profit versus preserving human agency's primacy; AI safety versus speed of iteration); CG4.1.2.2 build scenario-based understanding of the six core ethical principles — (1) do no harm, (2) proportionality, (3) non-discrimination, (4) sustainability, (5) human determination, (6) transparency and explainability; CG4.1.2.3 guide embodied reflection on and internalization of these principles (their implications for human rights, data privacy, safety, human agency, equity, inclusion, social justice and environmental sustainability; reflecting on personal attitudes such as advocating inclusive interfaces and reporting discriminatory biases found in AI tools). Pedagogical methods: case studies of controversial AI scenarios; individual or group reflection on dilemmas' personal dimensions (e.g. whether large language models should train on local communities' data; whether AI harms or helps the climate; how much privacy users should trade for AI services), expressed through essays, posters, drawings or storyboards; and searching for and validating examples of "AI for the public good" (advancing equity and inclusion for people with disabilities, preserving linguistic and cultural diversity, strengthening social justice and environmental sustainability).

**4.1.3 AI foundations (p. 32–34).** Curricular goals: CG4.1.3.1 illustrate what AI is and is not through examples (facial recognition, social media recommendations, pattern analysis of scientific data, medical diagnoses, self-driving cars, loan-default prediction); CG4.1.3.2 build conceptual knowledge of how AI is trained on data and algorithms — the three algorithm types: supervised learning, unsupervised learning and reinforcement learning, including how data are acquired and labelled — while debunking the claim that AI will write its own algorithms so humans need not learn them; CG4.1.3.3 encourage open-minded thinking and interdisciplinary grounding (artificial neural networks; the distinction between strong and weak AI; links to STEM, languages and social studies); CG4.1.3.4 give concrete form to human-centred considerations (the human roles across the AI life cycle: researchers, architecture engineers, data engineers, data workers, beta testers, ethics and safety regulators, human-AI interface specialists, compliance auditors). Pedagogical methods include example-based investigation (e.g. supervised learning with image classification for cancer diagnosis; natural language processing and generative AI for automated minute-taking and literature-review drafting); spiral learning moving from examples to abstract concepts (problem definition, data collection, data processing, training, evaluation, deployment, iteration); case analysis of innovative AI tools and uses; and strengthening the multidisciplinary foundation, especially mathematics — algebra, probability and statistics, data structures and algorithms such as K-nearest neighbours, K-means clustering, linear regression and CART/decision trees, plus linear algebra for complex data representation and matrix work, and calculus for back propagation and gradient descent in machine learning and neural networks.

**4.1.4 Problem scoping (p. 35–36).** Curricular goals: CG4.1.4.1 build critical thinking about when AI should not be used (e.g. where non-AI alternatives match its performance with less ethical risk and environmental harm, or where AI would dull human awareness or manipulate behaviour); CG4.1.4.2 develop skills in defining problems suitable for AI systems through simulation projects (e.g. training a model on a minority language to serve its community; automated tracking of migration across target regions), with problem statements that prevent wasted effort on ill-defined problems; CG4.1.4.3 build skills in assessing an AI system's requirements for data, algorithms and computing resources, including feasibility under regulatory and ethical constraints and total costs of processing, engineering, computing and hardware. Pedagogical methods: simulated review of project proposals, debating whether AI is warranted (weighing training-data availability, ethics, environmental impact and non-AI alternatives) and drafting a review checklist; simulated problem-scoping for everyday problems (automatically watering the school garden; helping a hard-of-hearing grandparent detect alarms); and a data preprocessing lab — training a model on dataset variations (e.g. classifying mystery images) using techniques such as data augmentation, handling outliers and analysing dataset skew/imbalance, then observing how preprocessing changes model performance. Learning environments: unplugged settings (worksheets, paper-based case studies, printouts of prototypes or design plans), digital devices with internet connection, and selected online AI systems.

### Level 2: Apply (p. 37–44)

At the 'Apply' level, the overarching aim is that learners build a firm, transferable body of conceptual understanding and skills around AI, and make a habit of applying human-centred thinking and ethical principles whenever they evaluate, study or work with AI tools. Table 3 sets out curricular goals that steer the definition of core value orientations, workable ethical principles and methodological knowledge; the recommended teaching approaches are meant to spark problem-driven inquiry into concepts and task-driven appreciation of hands-on skills while sustaining curiosity for further study; and suitable learning environments require hardware, software and applications that enable AI operation and co-creation, including open-source possibilities.

**4.2.1 Human accountability (Human-centred mindset) (p. 37–39).** Learners should come to see that accountability for AI rests legally with its creators and service providers, grasp which obligations fall to them personally when they design or use AI, and hold to the principle that high-stakes decisions must not be handed over to AI. They should also grow resistant to misleading claims that AI outputs and predictions can take the place of human thought and judgement. Curricular goals:
- CG4.2.1.1: Building on what students already know about the human-led AI life cycle and on actual lawsuits, show that those who create AI, provide it as a service, or deploy it institutionally must answer for any legal problems, violations and infringements the system causes. Explain the mechanisms for making them answer for safety incidents, ethical hazards in how AI is designed and trained, and exploitative misuse of the service.
- CG4.2.1.2: Accountability is both a legal and a social duty whenever AI informs decisions about people. Students examine what AI tools used for decision support can genuinely do, puncture exaggerated claims about AI's decision-making powers, and weigh the consequences when institutions let AI decide in sensitive situations—for instance, when profiling learners' suitability for further study or screening job applicants. AI must not be used to give students marks (as occurred during COVID-19) or to select university entrants, nor to judge a person's values, read their emotions or forecast their aptitudes.
- CG4.2.1.3: Cultivate the conviction that being accountable for AI demands personal capability to direct its purposeful use; have students question whether automating literature reviews, writing or artistic work erodes human thinking and intellectual growth, and have them work out concrete steps to shield themselves and others from letting AI outputs or predictions displace human thought and ongoing capability-building.

Recommended methods: having students take the parts of AI creators and data owners and draft self-discipline guidelines covering human oversight of data collection and processing, model training, the design of functions and interfaces, deployment, and monitoring/feedback loops; examining real cases where AI determined or heavily shaped decisions about people and checking redress options under applicable rules (e.g. the EU AI Act)—examples include a bank's AI-supported system approving or rejecting student loans, and a hotel predicting a guest's socio-economic status from their location and booking device; and purposeful, scenario-based AI practice, including discussion of how unaccountable use (such as submitting an essay written by AI) can stunt intellectual growth. Environments: offline materials such as printed case studies, role-play scripts, videos, worksheets and flipcharts; and online tools including learning management systems, social media and generative AI platforms.

**4.2.2 Safe and responsible use (Ethics of AI) (p. 39–41).** Learners should practise AI responsibly, following ethical principles and the regulations in force where they live; they should recognize privacy risks and act so that their personal data is gathered, used, shared, stored and erased only when they have knowingly and deliberately agreed; and they should know typical AI incidents and the particular dangers of certain systems, protecting themselves and their peers. Curricular goals:
- CG4.2.2.1: Build self-awareness and routine adherence to ethical principles—safeguarding personal data and privacy, honouring copyright, labelling AI-generated content clearly, and refusing to feed disinformation, misinformation, hate speech or sensitive details about identifiable people into AI systems—supported by a checkbox of principles that students build and revise over time.
- CG4.2.2.2: Strengthen self-discipline through age-appropriate insight into one's personal, legal and ethical duties and the price of breaking the rules, with special attention to sensitive personal data, copyrighted material, images showing identifiable people, AI-generated or digitally synthesized content, and the circulation of misinformation, disinformation and hate speech.
- CG4.2.2.3: Deepen practical safety knowledge and awareness of local rules; students sort general AI risks, risks tied to particular tools and typical incidents into categories, rehearse consent-protecting strategies for their data, and get to know the regulations that can shield them or soften the fallout of incidents.

Recommended methods: creating an "ethics kit" for self-disciplined use, developed through simulated ethical dilemmas (revealing private data or protected content in chats with AI, passing off AI text as one's own assignment, making videos with other people's images, spreading misinformation, disinformation or hate speech) and stocked with items taken from local regulations; rehearsing typical AI incidents and risk management; having students obtain AI creators' privacy policies, test them against data-owners' rights and creators' legal duties, and, where they find breaches, write complaints to regulators or improvement recommendations for the creator; and staging a debate on who owns AI-generated content and human–AI co-produced outputs, looking at whether copyright rules recognize such content and intellectual work that blends it in varying degrees. Environments: offline materials (worksheets, posters, principle checklists); pre-downloaded privacy policies, AI regulations and sample legal or ethical cases; locally available AI tools such as smartphone apps; and online platforms, particularly those with recommender algorithms and content generators.

**4.2.3 Application skills (AI techniques and applications) (p. 41–42).** Learners should assemble an age-appropriate understanding of data, AI algorithms and programming, gain transferable practical skills, and learn to judge and make use of free and/or open-source AI tools, code libraries and datasets. Curricular goals:
- CG4.2.3.1: Reinforce understanding and ability in data modelling, engineering and analysis, including the use of suitable tools or programming languages to obtain, clean and reformat data so it can be stored, processed and analysed in databases (e.g. SQL, NoSQL, SparkSQL or Apache Flink).
- CG4.2.3.2: Build age-appropriate programming technique, scaffolding comprehension of supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning—how these families of algorithms gather and process data, how training works, how they operate, and which concrete algorithms sit beneath each category.
- CG4.2.3.3: Grow analytical and synthesis abilities for exploiting open-source datasets (e.g. MNIST, CIFAR or ImageNet) and free/open-source libraries (e.g. Teachable Machine, PyTorch or Keras) on authentic tasks, with practice transferring data and algorithm skills to more complex settings.

Recommended methods: a data biases lab, where students experiment hands-on with how outliers distort models (e.g. regression or clustering) and how class imbalance (far more data in one class than another) degrades per-class image-classification performance, then learn data-engineering techniques to strip identifiable biases and compare outcomes; optional modular courses on different AI algorithms, tailored to each cohort's age and prior knowledge; hackathons built on varied authentic tasks that exercise transferable programming skills; and countering the claim that AI will write all code so students need not learn programming—researching the expertise real AI development demands and reflecting on how replacing human programmers would deepen the divide between those who do and do not understand AI. Environments: internet-connected computers; sample datasets or locally reachable public datasets; applications for AI programming or accessible online open-source libraries; and computer-based or online AI tools.

**4.2.4 Architecture design (AI system design) (p. 43–44).** Learners should acquire foundational methodological understanding and technical capability for setting up an AI architecture that scales, is maintainable and reusable, and spans data, algorithm, model and application-interface layers; they should also develop the cross-disciplinary abilities to draw on datasets, programming tools and computing resources to build a prototype AI system, bringing deepened human-centred values and ethics to its configuration, construction and optimization. Curricular goals:
- CG4.2.4.1: Scaffold engineering thinking and the operational capability to compare AI architectures and pick a fitting solution for a stated problem, open-source options included; support project work in which students design a prototype architecture that features an anti-bias data structure, an energy-efficient model that limits environmental harm, human-centred performance and service design, and metrics for testing and refining the configuration.
- CG4.2.4.2: Prepare advanced technical and project-management abilities for building a prototype for a simple, well-defined task (e.g. a chatbot that mimics an experienced teacher's replies): normalizing datasets, assembling virtual computing resources, choosing and improving models (e.g. hyperparameter optimization), simulating model training, and managing the project—balancing scope against available resources, dividing responsibilities, and critically appraising AI resources.

Recommended methods: simulated evaluation of frameworks and components for architectural configuration (e.g. TensorFlow, PyTorch or Scikit-learn), choosing solutions for the data, algorithm, model and interface layers and communicating the design via flowcharts, diagrams or pseudocode; simulated resource use on locally hosted machines or accessible cloud platforms (e.g. Hadoop or Spark), with operating systems (e.g. GNU) and the software for training machine-learning models; weighing cost against computing power, and model robustness against environmental cost, to use resources efficiently; and simulated enhancement through hyperparameter tuning and/or fine-tuning (e.g. transfer learning from an existing model, novel neural networks or non-trivial changes to foundational models). Environments: videos and metrics demonstrating ethical and technical model evaluation; computer-based or online AI system examples; dataset samples or public datasets; AI programming applications or open-source libraries; and locally hosted or open-source cloud resources shared between institutions.

### Level 3: Create (p. 45–52)

The 'Create' level pushes and equips students to reach advanced competence in configuring AI solutions or building new AI tools from adaptable datasets, programming tools or AI models, open-source options included. Students also strengthen their membership in a wider community of AI co-creators and engage intellectually with the social duties of citizenship in AI societies. Table 4's curricular goals sketch high-level competencies: advanced methodological knowledge of AI, engineering capability in system design, and flexibility in honouring personal and corporate social responsibility when creating and testing AI systems. The pedagogical approaches target ill-structured problems and higher-order thinking via project work, problem-driven exploration of methodology and multi-faceted ethical assessment; the learning environments cover the arrangement of datasets, programming tools and computing devices, with resource sharing and critical use of open-source options.

**4.3.1 AI society citizenship (Human-centred mindset) (p. 45–47).** Learners form critical perspectives on how AI reshapes human societies, extend human-centred values towards inclusive and sustainable development, consolidate civic values and social responsibility as AI-society citizens, and keep an open, lifelong curiosity about using AI to reach self-actualization. Curricular goals:
- CG4.3.1.1: Awaken the critical AI citizen—grounded insight into AI's spread as infrastructure underpinning social activity; critical perspectives on dilemmas such as speeding AI innovation at the cost of safety and inclusivity versus putting safety and inclusive access first; the ability to challenge AI-amplified prejudice against women, marginalized ethnic groups and the socio-economically disadvantaged, and AI's effects on social relationships, norms and structures; and judgement on how legal, ethical and social rules must evolve in response.
- CG4.3.1.2: Cultivate personal and social duty—students articulate their visions of desirable AI societies, map the obligations citizens must shoulder to build inclusive, sustainable and just ones (as both AI users and designers), keep refining their own responsibilities, and test their ethical commitments against complex real-world situations to make the human-centred mindset resilient.
- CG4.3.1.3: Foster self-actualization and lifelong learning—students keep reviewing how AI adoption changes sectors and the competencies life and work in an AI society demand, weigh AI's role in their personal goals, and build an adaptive, persistent disposition toward studying AI throughout life in service of their own fulfilment and society's sustainable development.

Recommended methods: case studies of tensions between an inclusive, just AI society and AI's threats to inclusion, justice and sustainability—analysing how deeply embedded AI can magnify bias, deepen economic and social divides, weaken human agency and accelerate climate change, then taking and defending positions on regulating today's AI and steering tomorrow's designs; group inquiry into citizens' rights and obligations, weighing global and local contexts, inclusion, equity, social justice, human-centred purposes and effects on the environment and ecosystems, with humans retaining control and accountability at every key step of the AI life cycle; and case studies linking AI adoption in work, life and social practice to personal goals, careers and self-actualization, and to lifelong learning. Environments: offline materials (worksheets, flipcharts, reports or videos on jobs and careers in AI societies, printed case studies on societal and environmental effects); and online AI systems or local tools for experiential and analytical testing, including smartphone apps offering personal assistants, chatbots and intelligent tutoring systems.

**4.3.2 Ethics by design (Ethics of AI) (p. 47–48).** Learners take an ethics-by-design stance toward designing, assessing and using AI tools and toward reviewing and revising AI regulations; they understand that vetting and ratifying a design's intent must begin at conceptualization and run through the entire AI life cycle; and they apply parameters to test a tool's conformity with ethical rules and employ a multi-stakeholder ethical matrix to review regulations and propose changes. Curricular goals:
- CG4.3.2.1: Instill awareness and understanding of ethics by design through conflict-based learning—judging ethical soundness at the conceptual stage, anti-bias safeguards in data collection and engineering, non-discriminatory training methods, human-centred 'guardrails' on generated outputs, and inclusive testing and auditing.
- CG4.3.2.2: Sharpen critical perspectives on the ethics-by-design commitments embedded in existing systems and algorithms—holistic evaluation, plus recommendations to developers for correcting violations and remedying harm their tools have done.
- CG4.3.2.3: Build the social responsibility to defend ethics by design within AI regulation—assessing how well chosen regulations embody the approach and whether their measures adequately monitor and control typical ethical risks in algorithms and systems, then recommending amendments to local rules or drafting proposals for new community regulations.

Recommended methods: role-playing the 'chief ethics officer' of an AI development team, including writing an audit checklist of ethical criteria for key design steps and defining due-diligence procedures for overseeing a team's or company's system; mock audits using an 'ethics label' (comparable to a nutrition label on food) to probe designers' true intent beyond their public statements—for example, a shopping-recommendation platform that claims to help customers find suitable products while covertly aiming to foster dependence or addiction; and constructing an ethics matrix—core ethical principles as columns, stakeholders (AI creators, regulators, institutional deployers, individual users) as rows—to analyse a chosen regulation and draft reviews with adaptation recommendations, or, where no local regulation exists, to propose a new one with drafted articles. Environments: offline materials (worksheets, flipcharts, printed due-diligence checks and reports, ethics labels and matrices, privacy policies, regulations); locally available AI tools including smartphone apps; online AI systems for ethical analysis; and websites hosting AI regulations and lawsuits or court cases.

**4.3.3 Creating AI tools (AI techniques and applications) (p. 49–50).** Learners extend and deploy their data and algorithm knowledge to adapt existing AI toolkits into task-specific tools; they bring their human-centred mindset and ethical judgement to bear when appraising available AI resources and testing their own creations, and they develop the social-emotional skills creation demands—adaptivity, complex communication and teamwork. Curricular goals:
- CG4.3.3.1: Stretch students toward advanced task-based tool development—translating their values, knowledge and skills into tools built on existing models or toolkits; judging whether available tools truly fit a task, sizing up data collection and processing requirements, choosing between a low-code route and full algorithms/programming, and executing the customization or coding.
- CG4.3.3.2: Stimulate creativity in adapting toolkits and code—working with AI-development platforms or toolkits, enriching datasets and modifying code, open-source variants included; and exploring and testing inventive designs against problem variations.
- CG4.3.3.3: Train students to test and refine their own tools—adapting assessment methods and instruments for robustness and usability, running peer assessments, exchanging feedback and growing collaborative skill as co-creators.

Recommended methods: task-based work on datasets and code to craft a tool around an authentic problem—monitoring how much energy local schools or households consume, predicting weather for a given place or route, or following an epidemic—using automatic collection tools (e.g. BeautifulSoup for scraping webpages), programming to clean, encode and preprocess the data, then feeding it into customized models or new tools; an AI application performance test lab, where students find and adapt free/open-source performance matrices (e.g. accuracy, precision, F-1 score, confusion matrices and ROC curves), test their application's performance and robustness, simulate user feedback on ethical compliance, and generate automated visual reports with optimization advice; and a comparison between creating tools by customizing datasets and code versus building on low-code platforms, discussing the two routes' differences in human agency and determination, whether local-community data and cultural diversity are reflected, and the scalability and reusability of the results, and how to choose between them. Environments: locally reachable free/open-source online datasets, AI tools and programming libraries; free/open-source data analytics tools; and cloud-based computing, locally hosted resources (e.g. a school server), or resources shared by trusted institutions or industry agencies.

**4.3.4 Iteration and feedback (AI system design) (p. 50–52).** Learners extend and apply cross-disciplinary understanding and practical methods to judge whether an AI model is humanistically appropriate and methodologically sound, and what it does to individual users, societies and the environment; they gain age-appropriate technical capability to upgrade datasets, rework algorithms and improve architectures in light of test results and feedback; they bring human-centred and ethical thinking to simulated decisions about shutting a system down and limiting its harm; and they grow into co-creators within the wider AI community. Curricular goals:
- CG4.3.4.1: Train students to critique AI systems—auditing whether a model strengthens or erodes human capacities, agency and consciousness; verifying its explainability and privacy protection; measuring performance; and studying user feedback to gauge wider social and environmental effects.
- CG4.3.4.2: Build technical skill and social responsibility for three iteration scenarios: (1) optimization—improving datasets, algorithms, the model, functionalities and/or the interface; (2) reconfiguration—returning to problem scoping and rebuilding the system; and (3) shutdown—when a system is shown to breach human rights or damage vulnerable groups, learning to decide to halt the model and rapidly activate remedies.
- CG4.3.4.3: Develop students' self-image as co-creators and 'drivers' of next-generation AI design, their belonging to the larger AI community, their critical analysis of AI's long-term effects on social relations and individual behaviour informed by real design and building experience, and their thinking on adapting or creating regulations and policies for better AI governance.

Recommended methods: simulated performance testing of an AI system, using adapted metrics (e.g. the F1 score, confusion matrices and ROC curves) and open-source visualization tools to weigh whether a model strengthens or weakens human capacities, agency and consciousness and how explainable its method is; gathering age-appropriate qualitative and quantitative market data, including (simulated) end-user feedback, on societal and environmental implications, then synthesizing and visualizing the findings; role-playing AI engineers' corporate decisions on a model's iteration—optimization when problem scoping holds, reconfiguration when tests or feedback expose fundamental flaws, shutdown when rights violations or harm to vulnerable groups are proven—while integrating corporate social responsibility, including negotiation and remedial strategies; and joining local or online communities of AI co-creators, taking part in discussions and collaborative development, and sharing open-source datasets and algorithm or toolkit examples. Environments: locally reachable online free/open-source AI tools, including data analytics tools and programming libraries; locally hosted or accessible cloud computing; downloaded and adapted instruments for ethical auditing and performance testing; access to applicable AI regulations or governance frameworks; and accessible online collaborative platforms for resource sharing, peer learning and joint design and creation (e.g. GitHub, arXiv or forum groups).

### Chapter 5: Applying the framework (p. 53–56)

**5.1 Aligning AI competencies as the foundation for national AI strategies (p. 53–55).** Some 70 countries have published AI strategy documents, which typically cast education as the means of growing domestic AI talent and human resources. Where such strategies are firmly established, the AI CFS can be mapped onto existing policy frameworks to cultivate the human-centred outlook and values required to give effect to AI-ethics regulation, to form prudent AI users and citizens, and to grow local co-creator communities at scale. Box 1 reproduces passages from UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (2022a, pp. 33–34): Member States should promote 'prerequisite skills' for AI education—basic literacy, numeracy, coding and digital skills, media and information literacy, plus critical and creative thinking, teamwork, communication, socio-emotional and AI ethics skills—above all where these are badly under-taught; and they should run general awareness programmes on AI developments, data, AI's opportunities and challenges, and its effects on human rights, children's rights included, open to non-technical as well as technical audiences. Where a national AI strategy exists and is well executed, rolling out the AI CFS and student curricula should be planned and resourced—administratively and financially—inside that strategy. Such strategies typically answer AI's disruption of work, both the jobs it displaces and those it creates, and the new skills the labour market will need; the leading response is nationwide AI competency development combining funding and incentives with dedicated AI courses that differentiate pathways per sector: schooling, technical and vocational education and training (TVET), higher education, employee upskilling and reskilling, and lifelong learning for citizens. Where no strategy has been adopted, the AI CFS itself can prompt awareness of the need for national AI policies and competency development. Rollout should begin by assessing readiness and programme gaps, with monitoring built in early and regularly; evaluation criteria for AI curricula should span: student and teacher readiness; shortfalls in teacher training and professional-development support; curricular goals and content needing repair to serve the national AI vision; content additions required by present and near-future market needs; mechanisms for mobilizing and validating cross-sector support; how far the curriculum is integrated; the preparedness of learning environments; and implementation quality. Box 2 summarizes the Republic of Korea's National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence (Ministry of Science and ICT, 2019), organized around: (1) reliable AI infrastructure, covering talent and technology; (2) spreading AI use across industry and society; and (3) getting ahead of social change, labour market needs included. It aims at an ecosystem of full-scale AI use and global leadership in people-centred AI. Measures include regulatory reform for an industry-friendly climate and productive data and AI innovation, AI-assisted governance, AI-ethics regulation, and human capital building starting in primary school. The strategy defines AI competencies for four groups: (1) the general public, who must be able to use AI and gain basic AI and data literacy, AI ethics included; (2) AI practitioners, who deploy AI and software tools in 'AI + X' settings in the labour market; (3) AI professionals, who build AI platforms and systems; and (4) AI talents, who will tackle AI problems and invent new models and algorithms. Matching measures raise industry professionals to professorship level in AI, expand AI departments and recruit more departments into AI-related programmes, widening master's and doctoral education and creating interdisciplinary AI majors. In schools: early primary pupils get experiential AI engagement to spark interest and curiosity; later primary pupils apply AI within core subjects; secondary students may attend AI-centred schools offering a more advanced curriculum; and teachers receive AI content in initial training plus new degrees on AI pedagogy integration.

**5.2 Building interdisciplinary core and cluster AI curricula for AI competency (p. 56).** Developing students' AI competency must draw on multiple channels: formal courses inside the national curriculum, extracurricular programmes, and informal learning via families and local communities. While a national AI curriculum remains the primary vehicle for the AI CFS, privately provided or non-governmental study programmes should be checked for conformity with the human-centred vision and ethical principles, and informal channels, digital platforms included, should be reviewed and steered—holding providers accountable for safety and ethics whenever their programmes target students, children above all. AI is intrinsically interdisciplinary, entwined with mathematics, science, engineering, languages, social studies, art, civic and citizenship education, history and blends of these, and it marks both a step and a leap within the continuum of digital technologies. The AI CFS accordingly rests on multidisciplinary knowledge of data, programming, computing structures and the internet, together with computing and engineering thinking and scientific reasoning, while the human-centred mindset and AI ethics are rooted in students' broader social and emotional skills. Countries should therefore fit the AI CFS to their general student competency frameworks and ask whether those frameworks need updating for the AI era; where national digital or ICT competency frameworks already operate, an adaptive route can fold AI in, redefining digital competencies to encompass the values, knowledge and skills that new AI iterations or domains demand and their links to earlier digital technologies. Core AI curricula within formal schooling are generally the main route to inclusive opportunity, especially for students whose only access to AI is at school; this means reconfiguring national curricula to make room for AI course time. Cluster curricula touching AI should be adjusted or reformed to connect more strongly with AI competencies while preserving their own core aims; such interdisciplinary core and cluster curricula can be organized in agile structures suited to national or local contexts. The UNESCO report K-12 AI curricula: A mapping of government-endorsed AI curricula (2022b) found four main integration strategies: AI as a discrete subject; AI folded into existing subjects (usually ICT); cross-curricular designs embedding AI outcomes across several cluster subjects; and AI as an optional, extracurricular or co-curricular activity (e.g. a club). A discrete subject may be compulsory for all and backed by companion courses in science, technology, mathematics, engineering and design, serving diverse abilities, backgrounds and needs. Whichever route is chosen, interdisciplinarity cuts both ways: the core AI curriculum should draw on students' multidisciplinary values, knowledge and skills across relevant subjects—science, technology, engineering, arts and mathematics (STEAM) above all, as in the United Arab Emirates' Computing, Creative Design and Innovation curriculum (UNESCO, 2024)—while the cluster AI curriculum should weave key AI competency aspects organically into learning outcomes and navigate them at the matching progression levels.

### 5.3 Framing future-proofing and locally feasible AI domains as carriers of the curriculum (p. 58–59)

To embed the AI competency framework for students (AI CFS) in national or institutional core curricula, developers must work out three interlocking elements: the key aspects of AI competency, the subdomains falling under each aspect, and the concrete AI systems that will serve as carriers of the curriculum. Whether a domain is compulsory or elective hinges on at least three considerations: how foundational each aspect is, how well the knowledge and skills will hold up over time, and whether local schools can realistically deliver them. Feasibility depends on how ready teachers and students are for AI, and on whether most schools can locally obtain and afford generic AI systems along with the particular hardware, software, programming languages and essential applications they need.

As Chapter 4 argued, the first three aspects—mindset, ethics, and techniques/applications—matter for every student's life and work in the AI era and therefore belong in any curriculum. Other domains, AI system design among them, may fit students with particular interest and aptitude. Gauging local readiness shows whether system design should be treated as a set of thinking skills folded into other aspects, or taught separately where databases, computing resources and AI models are actually accessible.

Scoping comes next: choosing subdomains from a very wide field of techniques. These range from logic systems and algorithms derived from general deductive principles (human-coded decision trees, alpha-beta pruning, minimax) to models that learn from large datasets (deep learning, generative AI), and developers must spell out how the chosen subdomains relate—classical or 'rule-based AI', machine learning, deep learning, generative models. On the technology side, the options multiply across computer vision, natural language processing, automated speech recognition, and automated planning and scheduling (AI planning). When picking example systems and tools, developers should stay as brand-neutral as possible; following Principle 5 of Chapter 2, rigorous public validation must screen out systems that discriminate against marginalized groups or carry biases tied to gender, ability, socio-economic status, language and/or culture. Inclusivity governs tool selection.

National context—policy ambitions as well as readiness—determines what is compulsory versus elective, and the depth and breadth of domain content should match the typical abilities of the target cohorts. Every student should attain the first two levels of the three universal aspects, while the third level, 'Create', is less essential—especially for system design—so an agile or contextualized strategy mixing compulsory and elective courses is advisable. Because tools and innovations will keep emerging after publication, the curriculum must be adjustable to stay 'future-proof'. A modular design is recommended: modules built around AI domains or specific systems and tools can be added or dropped without overhauling the whole curriculum. Future-proofing can also take the form of schools and students co-designing the AI curriculum, through school-based drafts and teachers' contextual adaptations of the domains or tools chosen for general competency building.

### 5.4 Tailoring age-appropriate spiral curricular sequences (p. 59–60)

The AI CFS implies a paradigm shift to competency-based education: away from fixed time with flexible learning (instruction completed on a set schedule whether or not everyone has mastered the material) and towards flexible time with fixed learning (schedules flex so that students of all abilities reach the expected mastery). Students demonstrate the knowledge, skills and values that make up a competency, and anyone falling short of the minimum receives extra support until they get there (Patrick and Sturgis, 2017).

The framework deliberately avoids prescribing progression by grade, concentrating instead on the exit-level outcomes systems should secure for all students. Developers must therefore build a scaffolded spiral learning pattern across all four aspects, letting students begin at the difficulty level matching their abilities and their school's readiness. Spaced, repeated encounters with foundational AI knowledge support memory retrieval and cyclically upgraded practice, moving information from working into long-term memory and letting students use existing schemas to absorb new AI content or adapt skills to unfamiliar problems. By contrast, one-off short bursts such as hackathons or bootcamps may kindle interest but seldom produce lasting competency. Developers' task is to lay out the main elements—AI ethics, foundational knowledge and skills, system design thinking—then calibrate their difficulty, breadth and depth for each grade and build progressively advancing iterations of lessons and project-based tasks.

**Box 4: The spiral curricular sequence of 'Day of AI' courses (p. 60).** The Day of AI curriculum from MIT's RAISE initiative clusters its content around questions such as 'What AI is', 'How AI works', 'How a machine learns' and 'How a machine creates'. Learners of different ages keep revisiting topics like 'What is AI?' while meeting steadily more advanced material—algorithms and AI programming, teachable machines, generative AI. Cross-cutting ethical themes (AI biases, human rights, human–AI interaction, AI's social impact) were pitched to each age group.

### 5.5 Building enabling learning environments for AI curricula (p. 61–62)

What resources are needed varies with curricular goals and local digital readiness, but a basic environment is indispensable for reaching minimum mastery. UNESCO's report *K-12 AI curricula: A mapping of government-endorsed AI curricula* (2022b) ranks the essential conditions for school implementation as follows: (1) teacher training and support, (2) teaching resources on AI, (3) needs analysis and school-based research, (4) updated digital infrastructure in schools, and (5) provision of AI resources—procuring hardware and software and working with the private or third sector so that AI devices and systems are shared. Without these, the curriculum is unlikely to be delivered as intended or to hit its learning and competency targets.

**Box 5: Typical enabling learning environments set up by governments' AI curricula (p. 61).** Drawing on the 11 countries with governmental K-12 AI curricula as of 2022:
- Hardware and robotics: computers, tablets, laptops and internet access; where robotics is taught, free online virtual applications or locally affordable kits can serve, and low-cost devices such as Raspberry Pi let students build and test programs.
- Software: some curricula used the Ubuntu open-source operating system as a cheaper option.
- Programming languages: freely available languages drawn on include HTML, Javascript, Python, Micropython, NumPy, R and Scratch.
- Tools for learning AI techniques: MachineLearningForKids (children train a computer to recognize text, pictures, numbers, sounds or other inputs), Teachable Machine (a Google platform for training a computer on one's own images, sounds and poses), TensorFlow (an end-to-end machine-learning platform) and Keras (deep learning for humans).

Governments should guarantee universal internet connectivity for every school and student, including agile 'online + offline' solutions, so learners can reach online or mobile AI systems, customizable applications, basic and extendable resources, and peers or co-creators. The infrastructure baseline also means a modest stock of well-functioning devices with basic connectivity and at least a minimum of software for learning operational skills, practising programming, and training virtual machine or AI models.

Where these conditions are missing but a government wants to start an AI curriculum anyway, alternatives exist. Most objectives under the first two aspects—Human-centred mindset and Ethics of AI—can be engaged, at least partly, through offline or 'unplugged' solutions; for AI techniques and applications, academic and non-profit organizations have produced unplugged activities (Everyday AI, AI Unplugged, and the International Society for Technology in Education). Even in fully connected settings, unplugged work has value: it lets students step back from the algorithm-curated information cocoons and platform interactions to practise independent, autonomous contemplation, which is essential for building and deepening conceptual knowledge of AI.

### 5.6 Promoting the professionalization of AI teachers and streamlining their support (p. 62–64)

Teacher training and support, together with AI teaching resources, are the single most important preconditions. Achieving the AI CFS goals requires teachers—especially in ICT or AI—to keep updating their subject knowledge and their pedagogical capacity to design and facilitate age-appropriate AI activities. Strategists should pursue an integrated approach: reforming pre-service programmes to produce qualified AI teachers; offering competency-based training and sustained support to in-service ICT or AI teachers; and upskilling teachers of other core subjects to spread interdisciplinary AI competency. This push towards professionalization means establishing frameworks dedicated to AI teachers (or more agile equivalents) that define and develop the professional competencies the curriculum requires. Because ICT and AI are often treated as marginal subjects, professionalization also entails classifying AI as a core subject, with AI teachers granted the same professional standing, and their teaching hours and performance equally recognized, as teachers of other core subjects.

**Box 6: An AI competency framework for AI subject teachers in China (p. 63).** The National Institute for Education, East China Normal University and Tencent jointly developed a competency framework for AI subject teachers. Though not government-driven, it signals the professionalization trend. It sets out six dimensions: (1) understanding and awareness, (2) basic knowledge, (3) basic skills, (4) problem-solving capability, (5) teaching practices, and (6) ethics and security. Teachers are expected to master AI's underlying conceptual logic and its effects on society, to appreciate how human and machine intelligence differ, and to value human–machine collaboration in view of AI's educational roles. Unlike the UNESCO AI competency framework for teachers, it targets AI teachers specifically, leaves out human-centred mindset and professional development, and offers no progression levels.

Where public teacher education cannot keep pace with AI's rapid change, public-private partnerships are often mobilized, drawing on the human and material resources of AI companies or NGOs to substitute partly or fully for public curricula and ICT/AI teachers. Because such companies and NGOs are keen to entrench their own brands in AI teaching, this carries a risk of de-professionalizing public AI teachers. Partnerships should therefore be purpose-built to prepare and upskill public AI teachers and support their continuing development. Comprehensive AI-teacher competency frameworks should supply rigorous criteria for judging whether industry-developed courses and trainers are trustworthy, free of bias, relevant to competency development and sufficiently brand-agnostic, and for checking that such courses supplement rather than supplant the public curriculum. Schools' accountability for steadily improving teachers' implementation capacities must be protected, not weakened. The AI CFS should also serve as a benchmark for aligning pre-service and in-service training and continuing support with clearly defined competencies, so that they complement one another in scaffolding teachers' growth across their careers, with particular attention to reviewing and adapting continuing education and school-based professional development in line with the values, knowledge and practical skills the national AI curriculum demands.

### 5.7 Guiding the cohort-based design and organization of pedagogical activities (p. 64–65)

AI competency develops as a "three-helix bundle": the social and emotional learning of values and ethical principles; the construction of conceptual AI knowledge, both self-directed and collaborative; and the practical skills to apply and co-create AI tools. Innovative pedagogies must be combined so students can advance along all three helixes at once, linking what they know to what they can do and carrying prior knowledge into new concepts and unfamiliar problem contexts in future AI-rich workplaces and social spaces.

Pedagogical innovation can be unlocked by organizing learning scenarios or projects around cohorts—students enrolled in the same AI course or sharing an interest in a domain, possibly drawn from different classes and grade levels. This cohort-based approach is not tied to any single learning theory; it typically mixes interactive activities, collaborative projects and peer support. Students form a community of practice, share accountability, motivate and coach one another, and seek feedback from teachers, deepening understanding, tackling hard questions together, collaborating on hands-on projects, and debating AI's societal impact and ethical questions to strengthen social construction.

Pedagogical choices must also respect domain-specific needs:

- Nurturing human-centred values and mindset rests inherently on social and emotional learning and calls for conflict-based opinion-taking, social construction and social interaction.
- Learning ethics means grasping abstract principles and regulatory rules through practical case work, critical evaluation of scenarios, application in context, and rule-making done collaboratively.
- AI techniques and applications blends practice-oriented construction of conceptual knowledge with authentic, task-based application; it needs real AI tools as the basis for knowledge schemas, problem-based learning, transferable application and scenario-based inquiry, plus a deepened grasp of the values and ethics embedded in AI tools and their uses.
- AI system design mirrors real engineering projects across the life cycle of creating, realizing and iterating AI systems, exercising engineering thinking and integrated problem-solving. Teachers must organize project-based learning in which students identify and delineate the problems AI can and should solve; assess data needs and plan collection; configure model architecture; and train models or build, test and iterate prototypes.

Because competency is a three-helix bundle, a single lesson or unit can touch several aspects, so instructional planners must weave together multiple methods. Real-world AI development relies on intensive, continuous conceptualization and iterative programming, configuration and optimization—a prerequisite validated by the pedagogical record of hackathons and bootcamps. Schools should therefore schedule more intensive units of lessons or activities aligned with the formal curriculum. Curricula should frame pedagogical guidance around shared accountability, peer learning within the target cohort, and the specificity of the AI domain and expected outcomes. When new methodologies are introduced, teachers need training, practical guidance and instantly responsive services (e.g. online chatbots), and locally relevant incentives should be planned to review, validate and recognize innovations during piloting and scale-up.

**Box 7: Pedagogical methodologies in the MIT curriculum on the ethics of AI for middle school students (p. 66–68).** Blakeley H. Payne, supported by the MIT Media Lab Personal Robots Group led by Cynthia Breazeal, created an AI ethics curriculum for students aged 12–14 early in their AI learning, deliverable online and/or offline (Payne, 2019). Parts fed into the MIT DAILy Curriculum and into *How to Train Your Robot: A Middle School AI and Ethics Curriculum*; research on the latter showed it can be taught even by teachers with little computer science background (Williams et al., 2021). This student-centred, inquiry-based curriculum runs a cycle of orientation and information-gathering (building knowledge schemas), conceptualization (forming hypotheses about AI's purposes), investigation (examining perspectives, benefits, values and risks for different populations and designing solutions), and finally a project-based prototype of a solution, with discussion and reflection throughout. Its six core goals, with example activities:

1. Understand the basic mechanics of AI systems—recognizing everyday AI uses, seeing algorithms as input, changes to input and output, and AI as an algorithm with a dataset, learning and prediction. Activities: 'AI Bingo', where each student finds a classmate who has used various AI applications (e.g. an emoji-suggesting tool or a route-mapping app) and the pair works out the dataset and prediction behind each system until five in a row—gamification that builds interest, motivation and recall; writing an algorithm for the 'best' peanut butter and jelly sandwich (or a noodle, rice or tamale dish, or another familiar local food), individually or in groups, exercising recall in a familiar mandated problem; identifying as a group the AI systems on YouTube, laying foundational schemas for later reflective and creative problem-solving; and building a classifier in Google's Teachable Machine to sort cat and dog pictures using a biased dataset that produces inconsistent results—facilitated experiential learning in which students reflect on and diagnose the bias, with "confirmation design" (a given question and methodology to confirm a known result) as one format; more advanced students explain their results.
2. Understand that all technical systems are socio-technical, serve political agendas and are not neutral—engaging the stated and hidden goals of algorithms, algorithmic bias and human agency. Activities: an ethical matrix of the stakeholders and values invested in the sandwich (or other food item), moving from recall to reflection and early critical analysis of conflicting interests, building procedural knowledge for harder, even ill-defined problems; and an ethical matrix built around the YouTube Recommender Algorithm, connecting classroom learning to students' lived realities.
3. Recognize that a socio-technical system has many stakeholders who are affected differently—identifying stakeholders, their values, and the goals systems should have to meet their needs. Activity: reflecting on stakeholders for technologies such as generative adversarial networks (GANs), emotional recognition and speech-to-text software, transposing procedural knowledge from the earlier matrices and building translational skills.
4. Apply technical understanding and stakeholder knowledge to determine a just goal for a socio-technical system. Activity: brainstorming and redesigning the YouTube algorithm around new goals, identifying the datasets and design features needed—a constructivist, project-based approach running design thinking up to the prototype stage; sharing designs enables co-learning and reflection, and a second iteration lets students use peer feedback.
5. Consider the impact of technology on the world. Activity: students try out different technologies and respond to creative writing and/or discussion prompts about direct and extended impacts, engaging experientially and fostering debate on the interactions among technology, its users, society and the environment.

### 5.8 Constructing competency-based assessments on the progression of key AI aspects (p. 69–77)

Assessing AI competencies calls for competency-based instruments matched to the specificity and integration of the multiple aspects—tools to benchmark students' starting points, gauge mastery of the key aspects, and appraise teaching and curriculum implementation. Since hardly any instruments exist for assessing comprehensive AI competencies across progression levels, implementing the AI CFS or a local curriculum must include building an assessment system covering its purpose and objectives, authentic tasks and methodologies, benchmarking standards or indicators, and domain-appropriate criteria with a matching grading scale.

**Criterion-referenced assessments (p. 69).** The core aim is to measure mastery against predefined standards or benchmarking frameworks. Under competency-based education, students who fall short within a given timeframe receive extra support until they reach the minimum. Criterion-referenced ipsative assessment—of an individual student or a target cohort—should diagnose the gap between current mastery and the standard and track progress over time, extending to self-assessment and personal curricular goals. The AI CFS translates competencies into measurable learning outcomes and exit-level behavioural performance for each competency block, which can anchor the benchmarking standards and a repository of assessment items showing which aspects, domains or topics are mastered and which need work. Norm-referenced assessment (comparing students with peers) is not the main focus, but agencies may build dynamically adjusted norms by tracking performance over the long term, benchmark local students against same-age peers abroad, check whether the group mean exceeds or falls short of the minimum standards, and disaggregate results by age, gender or demographic background to ground remedial support for students disadvantaged in learning AI.

**Adapting performance scenarios (p. 70–71).** Because AI addresses real-world problems, assessment should use real-world scenarios and authentic tasks to gauge how students apply mindset, ethical principles, skills and knowledge, and to psychometrically validate their development across aspects. Beyond observable 'performance-based assessment', four adaptations are recommended:

1. Assess both observable performance and latent competencies: move from measuring what students already do to psychometrically testing or validating their latent knowledge schema of AI techniques and application abilities, their human-centred critical thinking, and their ethical evaluation and selection of AI tools for specific purposes.
2. Shift from rote learning to transferability, adaptivity and creativity: replace fixed, repetitive operations with varied tasks probing how students carry knowledge and skills into new contexts, adapt to novel situations, critically evaluate existing tools, and collaboratively craft or co-create new ones.
3. Balance domain-specific and integrative assessment: on top of domain-specific checks of mindset, ethics, knowledge, skills and design, use authentic project-based testing of students' ability to combine ethical principles, AI knowledge and skills, and computational and engineering thinking to critically evaluate tools, redesign algorithms or co-create systems—open tasks whose required breadth is calibrated to the progression level, with grading scales suited to open, multilayered competencies.
4. Configure authentic tasks and grading scales: task design should follow the competency-block specifications in Chapter 4, tailored to each domain (mindset; ethics; conceptual knowledge; operational skills; comprehensive system design). 'Understand'-level tasks emphasize comprehension of the concepts and ethical principles beneath performance; 'Apply'-level tasks centre on problem-based practical skills and adaptivity to task variation; 'Create'-level tasks involve synthesis and algorithmic programming on new ideas, designing virtual or physical prototypes of new tools or systems, testing and optimizing AI models, the computational and engineering skill shown in co-creation, and the human-centred mindset and ethics underlying design and testing.

**Recommended domain-specific assessment forms (p. 72).** Twelve forms across four aspects and three levels:

1. Human-centred mindset: 1.1 Conflict-based opinion taking; 1.2 Conflict-based critical evaluation; 1.3 Conflict-based social actions.
2. Ethics of AI: 2.1 Scenario-based ethical value orientation; 2.2 Scenario-based ethical behaviour; 2.3 Scenario-based rule-making.
3. AI techniques and applications: 3.1 Problem-based AI knowledge and understanding; 3.2 Tool-based conceptual insights and transferable operation; 3.3 Task-based tool crafting.
4. AI system design: 4.1 Project-based design thinking; 4.2 Project-based system configuration; 4.3 Project-based iteration.

The AI system design forms rest on the virtual environment of Teachable Machine and a simulation project in which students design, train, test and optimize an AI system, themed around real-world needs for social inclusion and trained with data on local languages or cultural features. Because iterating systems from feedback is a critical integrated competency, traditional paper-based testing must be widened with metrics capturing technological conceptualization, prototype and improvement-process creation, and the technical expertise demonstrated in projects.

**Table 5: Examples of assessment tasks (p. 73–76).** Each level of each aspect opens with an integral paper and/or computer-based test on its main points (labelled 'Human agency', 'Human accountability', 'Social responsibility', 'Ethical principles', 'Safe and responsible use', 'Co-creating ethical rules'), followed by example items:

- Human-centred mindset. Understand: whether AI can support human decisions on values and social issues, and a weakness of current AI in doing so; what happens if humans shirk accountability when conceiving and designing AI systems; whether machine agency will grow beyond human agency. Apply: weighing media claims that artificial general intelligence will arrive by 2030 and overpower humans against experts who say AGI may never emerge, and evaluating whether selected media reports exceed AI's genuine capabilities; whether the next generation still needs to synthesize materials and draft reports if AI writes all meeting minutes and administrative reports; judging whether a given life or subject-learning problem can and/or should be tackled with AI. Create: analysing how AI systems affect environments and climate change and how their methods could be optimized, against claims that AI will remove climate-change drivers or that unlimited model training has caused irreversible impacts; reflecting on whether AI will be an indispensable, trustworthy co-worker or a threat to safety, inclusion, equity, justice and other social norms; evaluating whether AI creates jobs equally or deepens inequality, and its effects on the local economy and job market; critically evaluating, against AI companies' claims to build tools for all, what wide AI adoption means for inclusion and equity locally.
- Ethics of AI. Understand: describing how personal online data are collected and used without consent; how sensitive personal data may be captured through prompts or interactions with AI (e.g. asking a 'trustworthy' generative system to draft a recommendation letter); identifying ethical issues in the video-recommendation algorithms of platforms such as YouTube and TikTok. Apply: explaining why data security and data privacy must be considered when developing and using AI applications, including whether enjoying an AI system's services requires sacrificing some privacy; evaluating the claim that no explanation of how AI models work is needed because platforms always exceed expectations, and describing explainable AI; evaluating uses of AI-generated content—posting a realistic video generated from a friend's photo for fun, or publishing AI-authored essays under one's own name—and the potential legal problems. Create: if the country or school district has AI (or generative AI) regulations, critically evaluating them against UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of AI and/or the EU AI Act; if not, drafting a proposal justifying regulations and outlining their main points; co-creating ethical guidance for oneself and peers on video-recommendation platforms or generative AI; co-creating rules for safe, responsible AI use at school and home; co-creating regulatory rules for brain-computer interface (BCI) technology.
- AI techniques and applications. Understand: describing or exemplifying (with tools) what AI is and is not, or AI-supported personal, school-based or public tools; distinguishing strong from weak AI; outlining the basic concept of big data with examples of misuse; explaining how machine-learning models are trained, tested and optimized and why data matter throughout; relating deep learning to machine learning; defining 'artificial neural network' (or other key concepts for this level). Apply: exemplifying applications of natural language processing, computer vision, speech recognition, image recognition, autonomous agent systems, emotion detection, data-based prediction or generative AI; explaining supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning at a basic level; exemplifying typical algorithms in each category and tools that use them; explaining which algorithms a given generative AI system integrates; exemplifying two to three open-source datasets and algorithm libraries, with their advantages and limitations. Create: explaining how sensors, crawling software and other tools gather data for model training; explaining or demonstrating how to find and reuse open-source datasets and libraries, weighing benefits and risks against proprietary options; drafting an age-appropriate design-and-development plan for a task-based AI tool addressing real-world needs in and beyond the local context, covering critical analysis of existing tools, assessment of data needs, methods of collection and processing, suitable algorithms and programming languages, open-source tools to customize or fine-tune, and parameters for testing.
- AI system design. Understand (4.1 project-based design thinking): simulated problem-scoping tests with a report and/or oral defence, evaluated on why AI should be used (via checklist) and a problem statement covering key requirements or features such as algorithms, datasets and functionalities; explaining why a teacher-given real-world challenge should not be solved by AI; computer-based tests on data preprocessing with open-source datasets—adjusting data augmentation, handling outliers, analysing dataset skew or imbalance, retraining on modified datasets, and observing how preprocessing changes performance. Apply (4.2 project-based system configuration): computer-based tests on architectural configuration, evaluated on assessing and selecting frameworks, choosing solutions for the architecture's layers and components, configuring a prototype architecture, and presenting it; explaining how open-source datasets and AI programming libraries, plus locally accessible cloud platforms or operating systems and training software, can be leveraged to build an AI system; the criteria for optimizing efficiency and minimizing computational waste; calculating a chosen model's resource consumption and designing strategies to improve efficiency and reduce environmental impact. Create (4.3 project-based iteration): computer-based simulated optimization of a simple AI model—datasets, algorithms, parameter adjustment, functionality and interface design, and/or reconfiguration including modified problem-scoping; designing metrics for performance-testing an exemplar system, including metrics to measure performance and gather end-user feedback on societal implications and environmental impact, and exemplifying open-source tools that conduct and report such testing; drafting a report explaining the decision on an AI system—optimize, reconfigure or shut down—based on simulated tests and user feedback, with mitigation strategies where harm is possible; exemplifying locally accessible online communities of AI co-creators and what a student can do there.

**Agile formats and AI in assessment (p. 77).** Concrete formats and grading scales should be designed, tested and optimized, including formative and peer assessments (reflective essays, oral presentations, reports of users' tests of AI tools) and summative examinations, on paper and/or computer or unplugged—prototypes of AI tools, drawings of algorithms, essays on AI ethics case studies, technical reports on tool or system design, fine-tuning or simulated training of AI models, and assembling or creating hardware. This array should be matched nuance by nuance to each aspect's needs and flexibly infused in implementation. AI tools themselves emerge as a supplementary assessment method—automating the collection of learning-process and formative-mastery data from students or learning management systems, personalizing assessments to ability or linguistic and cultural background, and supporting teachers' strategic decisions. These opportunities should be dynamically reviewed and leveraged, but ethical issues must be examined and regulated: the collection and use of students' data, the risks of AI recommendations and predictions in assessments (especially high-stakes ones), and the erosion of teachers' agency, particularly their opportunities to gain insight from analysing learning processes.

### Conclusion (p. 78)

The framework is an action-oriented programme built on three assumptions about education's role amid pervasive AI adoption. First, the education sector must not merely adapt to AI systems and tools but proactively develop the competencies needed to shape AI that is ethical and environmentally friendly. Second, students should be equipped to act as critical and responsible users of AI, as co-creators of it, and as leaders in defining and designing the next generation of AI technologies. Third, students' AI competencies should form where a human-centred mindset and attitudes, internalized AI ethics, transferable conceptual knowledge and skills, and future-proof thinking about AI system design converge. Because competency development goes well beyond coding or tool operation, integrating AI-related learning demands an interdisciplinary approach spanning science, technology, engineering, art and mathematics as well as social studies and citizenship education. This is the first attempt at a global blueprint steering a human-centred integration of AI-related learning into curricula; informed by international expertise and consultation, it is a reference to be adapted across diverse local contexts and refined only through adaptation and testing among teachers and teacher educators. As a living document, it will be continuously reviewed in light of practice across contexts and of the new AI iterations yet to emerge.

### The AI competency framework for students (p. 81)

The framework rests on an ambitious vision reaching beyond popular notions of AI literacy: students should grow into not just effective, ethical users of AI tools but co-creators of more inclusive and environmentally sustainable AI. It defines the values, foundational knowledge and transferable skills needed to understand and use AI systems critically, safely, effectively and meaningfully at different levels of mastery; specifies which AI topics to cover and which pedagogical methods to deploy for understanding, applying and creating AI; and guides curricular integration, the organization of learning sequences, and the design of competence-based assessments. Treating these competencies as integral to responsible citizenship in the AI era, the framework is grounded in inclusivity, the centrality of human agency, non-discrimination, and respect for linguistic and cultural diversity.

## GLOSSAR

- **AI CFS (AI competency framework for students)** — UNESCO's global reference framework defining the values, knowledge and skills for students to understand, use and create AI at three progression levels (Understand, Apply, Create) across four aspects.
- **Human-centred mindset** — the aspect covering students' values, beliefs and critical thinking on whether AI is fit for purpose and what responsibilities individuals and institutions bear for safe, inclusive and just AI societies.
- **Ethics of AI** — the aspect covering ethical value judgements, embodied reflections, and social and emotional skills needed to navigate, practise and contribute to principles and regulations across the AI life cycle.
- **AI techniques and applications** — the aspect linking conceptual AI knowledge with operational skills on concrete AI tools and authentic tasks.
- **AI system design** — the aspect covering systemic design thinking and engineering skills for problem scoping, architecture building, training, testing and optimization of AI systems.
- **Understand** — progression level 1, for all students — constructing age-appropriate interpretations of the values, ethical issues, concepts, processes and technical methods underlying AI.
- **Apply** — progression level 2 — enhancing, transferring and adapting learned values, knowledge and skills to new, more complex contexts; the exit level for many students.
- **Create** — progression level 3 — becoming conscientious AI co-creators who design, implement and test human-centred AI solutions addressing real-world challenges.
- **Human agency** — the competency of recognizing that AI is human-led and that human control and determination must be protected throughout AI's design, provision and use.
- **Human accountability** — the legal and social responsibility of AI creators, service providers and institutional users for the consequences of AI systems, and the personal capability users need to direct AI purposefully without surrendering high-stakes decisions to it.
- **Embodied ethics** — the internalization of six core AI ethical principles — do no harm, proportionality, non-discrimination, sustainability, human determination, transparency and explainability — in reflective practice.
- **Ethics by design** — adopting an ethics-by-design approach to the design, assessment and use of AI tools and to the review and adaptation of AI regulations across the AI life cycle.
- **Problem scoping** — the competency of examining whether AI should be used from legal, ethical and logical perspectives, and defining a problem's boundaries, goals and constraints before training an AI model.
- **AI literacy** — the foundational values, ethical principles, knowledge and understanding that ensure proper and effective use of AI by students.
- **Proportionality** — the capacity to examine whether using a specific AI system is advantageous for a justified aim and whether the AI method is appropriate to the context.
- **Apply (Level 2)** — the competency level at which students build a firm, transferable knowledge and skill base in AI and make a habit of human-centred, ethical assessment and practice of AI tools.
- **Create (Level 3)** — the competency level at which students reach advanced ability to configure AI solutions or build new AI tools from adaptable datasets, programming tools or AI models, and consolidate their identity as AI co-creators.
- **Ethics kit** — a self-discipline checklist of ethical principles, drawn from locally applicable regulations, that users habitually consult when using AI.
- **Ethics label** — an audit instrument for AI systems, comparable to a nutrition label on food, used to probe the intent of AI designers beyond their published statements.
- **Ethics matrix** — an adaptive review tool with core ethical principles as columns and stakeholders (e.g. AI creators, regulators, institutional deployers, individual users) as rows, used to analyse regulations and recommend adaptations.
- **AI society citizenship** — the civic values, critical perspectives and personal and social duties citizens need to build inclusive, sustainable and just AI societies, including lifelong learning for self-actualization.
- **AI CFS** — the AI Competency Framework for Students, the UNESCO framework that can be aligned with national AI strategies and student competency frameworks.
- **Core AI curricula** — formal education AI courses offering inclusive learning opportunities to all students, especially those with no AI access outside school.
- **Cluster curricula** — existing school subjects related to AI that should be adapted or reformed to strengthen their links with AI competencies while keeping their own focus.
- **'AI + X' environments** — labour-market settings in which AI practitioners deploy AI and software tools in combination with other domains (term from the Republic of Korea's national AI strategy).
- **CCDI (Computing, Creative Design and Innovation)** — the UAE's interdisciplinary K-12 curriculum integrating AI with computer science, engineering, design, sustainability and visual communication.
- **Competency-based education** — a model shifting from fixed time with flexible learning to flexible time with fixed learning, so all students reach expected mastery levels with additional support as needed.
- **Spiral curricular sequence/pattern** — a scaffolded design giving spaced, repeated engagement with foundational AI knowledge across grade levels, deepening understanding through memory retrieval and cyclically upgraded practice.
- **Modular curriculum design** — structuring the curriculum in modules based on AI domains or systems/tools so content can be added, removed or updated dynamically without changing the whole curriculum.
- **Unplugged solutions** — offline (non-digital) activities enabling partial engagement with AI competencies where digital infrastructure is lacking, and fostering autonomous contemplation even in connected settings.
- **Three-helix bundle** — the conception of AI competency development as spanning social and emotional learning of values and ethics, self-directed and collaborative construction of conceptual knowledge, and practical skills to apply and co-create AI tools.
- **Cohort-based approach** — organizing learning scenarios or projects for groups of students drawn from different classes and grade levels who share a course or AI-domain interest, building a community of practice with shared accountability and peer learning.
- **Professionalization of AI teachers** — recognizing AI as a core subject and AI teachers as having equal professional status, supported by dedicated competency frameworks and streamlined pre-service/in-service training.
- **Criterion-referenced assessment** — assessment measuring students' mastery against predefined standards or benchmarking frameworks rather than against peers.
- **Ipsative assessment** — assessment of an individual student's or cohort's progressive performance over time and gaps against minimum standards, supporting personalized learning experiences.
- **Norm-referenced assessment** — assessment comparing individual students to their cohort or to same-age students in other countries, used secondarily and monitored against predefined standards.
- **Performance-based assessment** — assessment using real-world scenarios and authentic tasks to measure students' overt behavioural performance in applying mindset, ethics, skills and knowledge.
- **Confirmation design** — an activity format in which students are given a question and methodology to confirm a known result (from the MIT ethics curriculum).
- **Carrier of the curriculum** — a specific AI system or tool selected to carry the learning of AI domains and competencies, chosen brand-agnostically and validated for inclusivity.
