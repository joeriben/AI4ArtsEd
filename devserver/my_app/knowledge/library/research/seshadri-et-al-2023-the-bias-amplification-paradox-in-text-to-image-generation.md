# The Bias Amplification Paradox in Text-to-Image Generation

## PROFIL

- Written by Preethi Seshadri, Sameer Singh, and Yanai Elazar, this scholarly article investigates "bias amplification" — a model exacerbating biases present in its training data — in the text-to-image domain, studying gender-occupation bias in Stable Diffusion (versions 1.4 and 1.5) and its training dataset LAION-5B (the 2.3-billion English portion).
- Its central claim is paradoxical: although the model appears to amplify gender-occupation bias considerably under naive evaluation, most of this apparent amplification stems from distributional discrepancies between training captions and generation prompts; once these are accounted for, amplification decreases drastically. The title alludes to Simpson's Paradox, as the aggregate trend largely disappears when subgroups are examined separately.
- The paper measures perceived binary gender at scale via an automated pipeline (face detector plus CLIP classification, with a 0.9 probability filter), analyzing 62 occupations across four gender-neutral prompts, and quantifying amplification following Zhao et al. (2017).
- It proceeds from a naive comparison showing 12.57% average amplification, to two corrective approaches — excluding captions with explicit gender indicators, and selecting nearest-neighbor captions via Sentence-BERT embeddings — which, combined, reduce amplification to 4.35%; using original training captions verbatim as prompts establishes a lower bound of 0.68%.
- It situates itself against and within work on pretraining data and model behavior (Carlini et al., 2023; Biderman et al., 2023), bias amplification research (Zhao et al., 2017; Hall et al., 2022; Friedrich et al., 2023), and bias in text-to-image models (Basu et al., 2023; Fraser et al., 2023; Bianchi et al., 2023).
- It closes with recommendations for data curators and evaluators (multiple prompts, removal of prompt-specific confounders), stated limitations (binary, appearance-based, English-only gender analysis), and an appendix with per-occupation tables; the authors release their code publicly.
- Usable in arts education wherever generative AI, dataset curation, and the ethics of representation in computational image-making are taught or researched — for instance in digital art, media studies, and critical data-literacy contexts examining how training data and prompts shape the imagery models produce.

## WISSEN

### Purpose and Thesis (p. 1–2)

This paper investigates "bias amplification" — the phenomenon in which a model exacerbates biases or stereotypes present in its training data — in the text-to-image domain. The authors study gender-occupation bias in Stable Diffusion (versions 1.4 and 1.5) and its training dataset LAION-5B (the 2.3-billion English portion), comparing gender ratios in training images versus generated images. Their central thesis is paradoxical in nature: although the model appears to amplify gender-occupation bias considerably under naive evaluation, most of this apparent amplification can be attributed to distributional discrepancies between training captions and the prompts used for generation. Once these discrepancies are accounted for, amplification decreases drastically. The paper's title alludes to Simpson's Paradox, since a trend observed in aggregate (substantial amplification) largely disappears when subgroups of the data are examined separately. The authors argue that any comparison of dataset and model biases must factor in distribution shifts, and they release their code publicly.

### Experimental Setup (p. 2–3)

The study uses Stable Diffusion, a text-to-image model trained on image-caption pairs from LAION-5B. To measure perceived binary gender in images at scale, the authors use an automated pipeline: a face detector first checks that an image contains a single face, then CLIP (a multimodal zero-shot classifier, whose text encoder Stable Diffusion itself uses) predicts gender. Images with predicted gender probability below 0.9 are excluded to avoid ambiguous cases; the same filtering is applied to training and generated images. The authors acknowledge the limitations of binary gender classification from appearance, noting that their analysis excludes non-binary individuals. They analyze 62 occupations that vary in bias direction — male-skewing (e.g., CEO, engineer), balanced (e.g., attorney, journalist), and female-skewing (e.g., dietitian, receptionist) — drawn from prior work (Rudinger et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2018; De-Arteaga et al., 2019).

### Methodology (p. 3)

Model bias is measured by generating 500 images per occupation and prompt using four prompts that deliberately omit gender information: (1) "A photo of the face of a/an [OCCUPATION]", (2) "A portrait photo of a/an [OCCUPATION]", (3) "A photo of a/an [OCCUPATION] smiling", and (4) "A photo of a/an [OCCUPATION] at work". G_P_o is defined as the percentage of females in generated images for prompt P describing occupation o. Data bias is measured by querying LAION with WIMBD (a tool for exploring large text corpora) to find captions mentioning an occupation; T_S_o is the percentage of females in images for a training subset S corresponding to occupation o. Bias amplification follows the formulation of Zhao et al. (2017): for each occupation, amplification is computed by comparing G_P_o against T_S_o, aggregated across occupations and averaged over the four prompts. Bias is defined as deviation from a 50% balanced binary gender ratio; occupations that switch bias direction between training and generation are excluded from analysis.

### Naive Approach and Apparent Amplification (p. 3–4)

Selecting all training captions containing a given occupation (500 randomly sampled per occupation), the authors find that Stable Diffusion 1.4 amplifies bias by 12.57% on average across occupations and prompts (10.24% for Prompt #1). However, qualitative inspection reveals discrepancies between captions and prompts: (1) captions frequently contain explicit gender indicators (e.g., "female mechanic"), which the authors hypothesize are used more often for underrepresented groups, thereby shifting the training gender distribution toward balance and inflating amplification; (2) prompts are intentionally underspecified while captions contain rich contextual detail (person descriptions, activities, locations — examples include "Portrait of smiling young female mechanic inspecting a CV joint on a car in an auto repair shop"); and (3) occupations with multiple interpretations diverge — training captions for "president" include company or organization presidents, whereas the model interprets the prompt as a national leader (often a US president); similar differences appear for "teacher". The authors conclude that naive comparisons cannot distinguish genuine model amplification from confounding distribution shift.

### Reducing Discrepancies (p. 4–7)

The authors propose two approaches that restrict the training-example search space while keeping prompts fixed:

1. **Excluding captions with explicit gender indicators** (p. 4–5). On average 59.5% of captions contain explicit gender information; for mechanics, 68% of female-mechanic examples contain indicators versus only 30% for male mechanics. The Pearson correlation between percentage of females in training images and percentage of captions with female indicators is -0.458 (statistically significant at < 0.05), confirming that indicators mark underrepresented genders. Gender indicators are detected via gender words, binary pronouns, and names (using spaCy named entity recognition plus the gender-guesser library). Amplification drops to 8.66% (a 31% reduction).

2. **Nearest neighbors (NN) on text embeddings** (p. 5–6). Using Sentence-BERT (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) embeddings, the authors select the top-500 captions most similar to each prompt by cosine similarity. Average caption-prompt cosine similarity rises from 0.385 to 0.704 (an 83% increase), and CLIP image-embedding similarity also rises (13%). Qualitatively, NN captions for "president" depict world leaders and for "teacher" depict classroom settings. Amplification drops to 6.76% (a 46% reduction). The authors chose Sentence-BERT over CLIP embeddings to avoid leaking biases from Stable Diffusion's text encoder into example selection.

3. **Combining both approaches** (p. 6–7) yields the largest reduction: average amplification of 4.35%, with only 1.11% for Prompt #1. A one-sample t-test fails to reject the null hypothesis of zero expected amplification for prompts #1 and #3, but rejects it for prompts #2 and #4 (significance level < 0.05), indicating some unexplained amplification remains, possibly from subtler confounding factors.

### Removing Distributional Differences: A Lower Bound (p. 7)

To eliminate caption-prompt mismatch entirely, the authors use the original training captions verbatim as prompts, generating 10 images per caption. Amplification drops to 0.68%, with most occupations lying along the no-amplification diagonal, even for highly imbalanced occupations (though the null hypothesis of zero amplification is still rejected). For captions with explicit gender indicators, the model matches the training image gender with 98.41% accuracy; on the no-indicator subset, amplification is 2.05% (an 84% reduction relative to keyword querying). The authors note this setup is not a practical recommendation but establishes a lower bound and demonstrates the impact of distributional similarity.

### Related Work (p. 7–8)

The paper situates itself in: (1) work relating pretraining data to model behavior (Carlini et al., 2023; Kandpal et al., 2023; Razeghi et al., 2022; causal studies by Biderman et al., 2023; Elazar et al., 2022; Longpre et al., 2023); (2) bias amplification research, especially Zhao et al. (2017), who showed structured prediction models amplify data biases but focused on mitigation rather than analysis; Hall et al. (2022) on how data and training choices influence amplification; and Friedrich et al. (2023), who compared LAION and Stable Diffusion and proposed "fair diffusion" for mitigation; and (3) bias in text-to-image models, including geographical disparities (Basu et al., 2023; Naik and Nushi, 2023), intersectional biases (Fraser et al., 2023; Luccioni et al., 2023), and Bianchi et al. (2023) on stereotype persistence under counter-stereotypical prompting.

### Discussion and Recommendations (p. 8–9)

The authors note that small prompt changes have large effects: "A portrait photo of an attorney" skews heavily male while "A photo of an attorney at work" skews female; reductions in amplification also vary by prompt (89% for Prompt #1 versus 49% for Prompt #2), suggesting prompt-specific sources of distribution shift. They distinguish their amplification baseline — model bias relative to training data — from real-world amplification (Kirk et al., 2021; Bianchi et al., 2023), which answers a different question. Recommendations include: data curators should consider how practitioners actually write prompts; crowdsourcing or automatically rewriting training captions to reflect real-world usage may lower amplification; and evaluations should use multiple prompts and remove prompt-specific confounders (e.g., via NN selection).

### Conclusion, Limitations, and Ethics (p. 9)

The paper concludes that naive evaluations of bias amplification are incomplete and misleading, and strongly recommends accounting for distribution shifts when comparing dataset and model properties. Limitations include: biases arising from CLIP's text embeddings cannot be disentangled since CLIP's training data is not public; automated gender classification inherits CLIP's biases, though human evaluation with 7 participants on 200 images showed high agreement (Krippendorff's α = 0.948, with 98% of CLIP predictions matching majority-vote annotations). Ethical caveats: the analysis covers only gender-occupation stereotypes among many bias types, uses only binary gender, relies on appearance-based gender inference, and is English-only; the gender-guesser library (covering 40,000+ names, mostly European) likely performs worse on non-Western names.

### Appendix (p. 12–15)

The appendix lists the 62 occupations (Table 4) and notes 5 occupations (assistant, author, dentist, painter, supervisor) that consistently switch bias direction across all prompts and both models, which are excluded. It details the explicit gender indicator detection method and provides full per-occupation tables of percentage-female in training images and generated images (SD 1.4 in Table 5, SD 1.5 in Table 6), including per-prompt results and standard deviations across prompts.

## GLOSSAR

- **Bias amplification** — the phenomenon in which models exacerbate biases or stereotypes present in the training data, measured here as the change in gender ratio from training to generated images.
- **Bias (as defined here)** — a deviation from the 50% balanced binary gender ratio; distinct from performance gaps between groups in classification setups.
- **De-amplification** — bias decreasing from training to generation (the opposite of amplification).
- **G_P_o** — the percentage of females in generated images for a prompt P describing occupation o.
- **T_S_o** — the percentage of females in images for a training subset S corresponding to occupation o.
- **Naive approach** — selecting training examples by keyword querying (all captions containing the occupation), which yields inflated amplification of 12.57%.
- **No-gender-indicator subset** — training captions without explicit gender words, pronouns, or gendered names, used to reduce distribution shift; yields amplification of 8.66%.
- **Nearest neighbors (NN) approach** — selecting the top-500 training captions most similar to prompts via Sentence-BERT embedding cosine similarity; yields amplification of 6.76%.
- **Combined approach** — NN selection plus exclusion of gender indicators, yielding the lowest subset-based amplification of 4.35%.
- **Lower bound setup** — using training captions verbatim as prompts to eliminate caption-prompt mismatch, yielding amplification of 0.68% (2.05% on the no-indicator subset).
- **Underspecification** — the deliberate vagueness of prompts (lacking adjectives, surroundings, activities) that forces the model to generate from open-to-interpretation inputs.
- **Explicit gender indicators** — gender words (e.g., male, woman), binary gender pronouns, or names from which gender is inferred.
- **Switching behavior** — occupations exhibiting different directions of bias at training versus generation, excluded from amplification analysis.
