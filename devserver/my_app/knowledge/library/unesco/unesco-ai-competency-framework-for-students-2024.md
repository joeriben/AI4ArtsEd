# AI competency framework for students

## PROFIL

- The document presents UNESCO's AI competency framework for students, which aims to support the development of core competencies for students to become responsible and creative citizens equipped to thrive in the AI era.
- It charts an action-oriented programme asserting that the education sector must be proactive in developing competencies required to shape ethical and environmentally-friendly AI, positioning students as critical users, co-creators, and future leaders.
- The framework defines twelve competencies across four dimensions—Human-centred mindset, Ethics of AI, AI techniques and applications, and AI system design—articulated at three progression levels: Understand, Apply, and Create.
- The publication builds on UNESCO's previous work, explicitly naming the ICT competency framework for teachers, AI and education: Guidance for policy-makers, and Guidance for generative AI in education and research.
- It also references the 2019 UNESCO Beijing Consensus on AI and Education and was developed alongside a companion competency framework for teachers.
- For an arts-education platform, the text is applicable to policymakers, curriculum developers, and educational experts designing interdisciplinary curricular integration and age-appropriate agile teaching methodologies.
- It provides guidance for project-based learning activities, such as those evaluating the social, economic, and environmental impacts of AI use, and emphasizes an ethics-by-design approach to AI co-creation.

### AI competency framework

- Students are expected to move beyond the misconception that AI is a solution to everything.
- Students should make meaningful use of AI for self-actualization; evaluate its social, economic and environmental impacts; and contribute, at a level appropriate for their age or grade, to the development of AI regulations.
- A human-centred approach requires that AI be used in ways that ensure transparency and explainability, as well as human control and accountability.
- The AI CFS guides students to design and implement project-based learning activities on the environmental impacts of AI use and training.

### AI competency framework

- Inclusivity across AI life cycle: representative data selection, bias-agnostic algorithms, anti-discrimination training methods, accessible functionality design, testing inclusiveness of outputs, social-inclusion impact assessment.
- Public validation must screen AI tools for bias by gender, ability, socio-economic status, language, ethnicity, culture. Tools supporting disabilities and linguistic/cultural diversity prioritized. Without validation, no at-scale recommendation.
- Governments commit to: basic internet connectivity, updated devices, open-source/affordable AI software, essential AI devices.
- Core competencies are brand-agnostic and product-agnostic; cover AI data, algorithms, models, system design.
- Human rights focus: equality, non-discrimination, privacy, plural expression. Ethics-by-design approach to AI co-creation.

### AI competency framework

The AI CFS defines twelve competency blocks from a matrix of two dimensions: four aspects and three progression levels. The four aspects are: human-centred mindset, ethics of AI, AI techniques and applications, and AI system design. The progression levels are Understand, Apply, and Create.

Matrix intersections:
- Human-centred mindset: Human agency, Human accountability, Citizenship in the era of AI
- Ethics of AI: Embodied ethics, Safe and responsible use, Ethics by design
- AI techniques and applications: AI foundations, Application skills, Creating AI tools
- AI system design: Problem scoping, Architecture design, Iteration and feedback loops

The matrix guides:
- scoping focus areas and mastery levels
- identifying learning content
- defining proficiency levels and assessment criteria
- designing age-appropriate agile teaching methodologies

Level 1 (Understand) is for all students. Level 2 (Apply) is relevant for all school students and is the exit point for many. Level 3 (Create) is specialized.

### AI competency framework — Level 3: Create and Aspects

### Level 3: Create
Students become AI co-creators, building tools using open-source and/or customizable datasets, programming tools, or AI models. Full implementation requires sufficient curriculum allocation (e.g. an entire semester or multiple semesters). The 'AI system design' aspect should be offered as elective, not compulsory, for students without strong interest.

### Aspects
Four aspects structure AI competencies across three progression levels (Understand, Apply, Create).

**Human-centred mindset** — competency blocks: Human agency; Human accountability; Citizenship in the era of AI. Students recognize AI is human-led; understand human accountability as legal obligation; recognize human choice should not be ceded to AI in high-stakes decisions.

**Ethics of AI** — competency blocks: Embodied ethics; Safe and responsible use; Ethics by design. Embodied ethics principles:
- Do no harm
- Proportionality
- Non-discrimination
- Sustainability
- Human determination in human-AI collaboration
- Transparency and explainability

### AI competency framework

Two aspects × three progression levels (Understand, Apply, Create):

**AI techniques and applications**
- AI foundations: data, algorithms; interdisciplinary areas: science, technology, engineering, mathematics, arts, languages, social studies
- Application skills: evaluate free/open-source AI tools, programming libraries, datasets
- Creating AI tools: customize existing AI toolkits for task-based tools
- Data collected, used, shared, archived, deleted only with deliberate, informed consent
- Ethics by design: assess AI tool compliance with ethical regulations; examine AI life cycle from conceptualization

**AI system design**
- Problem scoping: examine whether AI should be used from legal, ethical, logical perspective; define boundaries, goals, constraints before training a model
- Architecture design: configure scalable, maintainable, reusable architecture covering data, algorithms, models, application interfaces
- Iteration and feedback: evaluate robustness; improve datasets, reconfigure algorithms; simulate AI system shutdown decisions

### AI competency framework

- **Level 1: Understand**: Goal is acquiring "AI literacy" — age-appropriate interpretations of AI values, ethical issues, concepts, processes, and technical methods. Connect AI knowledge to real-life experiences and related learning areas.
- Pedagogical methods: age- and domain-appropriate, using concrete tools, personal experiences, and real-world scenarios.
- Learning settings: include practising with "unplugged and low-tech options".

### AI competency framework: Level 1 Understand

### Competency block: Human-centred mindset
**Student competency: 4.1.1 Human agency**

**Curricular goals:**
- CG4.1.1.1: AI is human-led. Facilitate stepwise comprehension of human agency covering principles on data ownership and data privacy, protection of human rights in collecting and processing data, explainability of AI methods, human control in deployment, and human determination in using AI for decision-making. AI cannot replace human thinking or intellectual development.
- CG4.1.1.2: Expose students to consequences of human oversight in controlling AI: weak regulations failing to prevent the design and production of harmful AI tools, the institutional use of AI to substitute for humans when making high-stakes decisions, and the absence of human validation of the accuracy of AI outputs.
- CG4.1.1.3: Nurture critical thinking on the dynamic relationship between human agency and machine agency. Examples of machine agency needed: detecting medical patterns that are undetectable for human doctors in diagnosing rare diseases, auto spell check and autocorrection when humans draft reports, auto captioning or automating video-production in the development of course materials, automatic language translation.

**Suggested pedagogical methods:**
- Visualizing the abstract concept of human agency throughout the AI life cycle: concept maps of human agency in key steps (data ownership, respecting data privacy when collecting and processing data, explainability of AI algorithms and AI models, human-controlled evaluation of AI outputs, and human determination in AI-assisted decision-making).
- Simulating an AI Act courtroom debate to evaluate creators' intents underlying prohibited AI systems: Based on an age-appropriate interpretation of the definition of AI systems prohibited under the European Union's AI Act. Example: an AI system may deploy techniques to weaken a person's awareness or purposefully impair their ability to make an informed decision.
- Scenario-based understanding of human-controlled interaction with AI: Select examples or scenarios in which AI tools are used in workplaces or daily life.
- Debating the dynamic boundary between human agency and machine agency.

**Learning environments:**
- Unplugged learning settings like paper-based articles, printed reading materials and worksheets.
- Locally available AI tools including mobile phones with AI applications.
- Predownloaded or recorded videos and other resources related to specific case studies, or scenarios that present a dilemma.
- Search engines, online videos and supplemental online learning courses.

### 4.2 Level 2: Apply

### 4.2 Level 2: Apply

**4.2.1 Human accountability**

**Student competency:**
- Recognize human accountabilities as legal obligations of AI creators and AI service providers.
- Understand human accountabilities to assume during the design and use of AI.
- Humans should not cede determination to AI when making high-stakes decisions.
- Enhance judgement on illusive claims about AI outputs and predictions.

**Curricular goals:**
- **CG4.2.1.1:** Develop the view that human accountability is a legal obligation of AI creators and service providers. Leverage real-world lawsuits to show accountability for legal issues, violations, infringements, safety incidents, ethical risks in designing/training AI, and misuses to control users.
- **CG4.2.1.2:** Generate understanding that human accountability is a legal and social responsibility when using AI in decisions about humanity. Evaluate consequences of institutional AI use in complex situations (e.g., profiling the aptitude of students, determining the employability of job candidates). Do not use AI to replace humans in high-stakes decisions (e.g., assess values, infer emotions, predict aptitudes).

**Suggested pedagogical methods:**
- **Writing guidelines on human accountability for AI creators and service providers:** Students play roles of AI creators and data owners, discussing key legal and ethical accountabilities in maintaining human control of data collection/processing, training AI models, designing functionalities/interfaces, deployment, and monitoring/feedback loops. Write self-discipline guidelines.
- **Investigating the impact of AI-assisted decisions on humans and avenues of redress within AI regulations:** Find examples where decisions about humans are determined or influenced by AI (e.g., an AI-assisted assessment system used by a bank to approve or deny a student loan application; a profiling system used by a hotel to predict a person's socio-economic background based on location and device). Check compliance with locally applicable or international regulations (e.g., the EU AI Act).
- **Scenario-based practices of using AI with purpose:** Use AI tools to practise writing skills and foster inquiry-based learning, higher-order thinking, and creativity. Discuss how using AI without human accountability (e.g., handing in an essay produced by AI) reduces human intellectual development. Outline concrete actions to protect against misuse of AI outputs.

**Learning environments:**
- Unplugged and/or offline learning settings and resources: print-based case studies, role-play scripts, videos, worksheets, and flipcharts.
- Online AI tools: learning management systems, social media platforms, and generative AI platforms.

### 4.3 Level 3: Create

Level 3: Create targets advanced competencies to configure AI solutions or craft new AI tools using customizable datasets, programming tools, or AI models, including open-source options. Pedagogical approaches include project-based learning, problem-based exploration, and multi-faceted ethical assessments. Learning environments require datasets, AI programming tools, and computational devices.

**Human-centred mindset: 4.3.1 AI society citizenship**

Curricular goals:
- CG4.3.1.1: Foster awareness of being a critical AI citizen. Gain evidence-based insights into AI as social infrastructure. Critique AI-amplified biases against females, marginalized ethnic groups, and socio-economically disadvantaged people. Assess adaptation of legal, ethical, and social rules.
- CG4.3.1.2: Nurture personal and social responsibilities. Delineate obligations for building an inclusive, sustainable, and just AI society from user and designer perspectives. Examine challenges in upholding ethical principles in complex authentic situations.
- CG4.3.1.3: Nurture self-actualization and lifelong learning. Dynamically review the impact of AI adoption across sectors.

Suggested pedagogical methods:
- Case studies on conflicts between an inclusive AI society and AI threats: Analyze cases of pervasive AI embedding. Interrogate how AI amplifies biases, widens economic and social inequality, undermines human agency, and worsens climate change. Defend positions on AI regulation and design.
- Inquiry on personal social responsibilities: Group discussions on citizen rights. Outline obligations considering global and local contexts, inclusion, equity, social justice, human-centred purposes, and impacts on environment and ecosystems. Ensure humans have control and accountability over all key steps of the AI life cycle.
- Case studies on self-actualization: Review implications of AI adoption for personal goals and career development.

Learning environments:
- Unplugged settings: Worksheets, flipcharts, reports, or videos on jobs and career development; print-based analytical case studies on societal implications and environmental impact.
- Online AI systems or locally available AI tools: Experiential and analytical tests, including smartphone applications providing personal assistants, chatbots, and intelligent tutoring systems.

### Aligning AI competencies as the foundation for national AI strategies

Around 70 countries have AI strategies. UNESCO (2022a) recommends prerequisite skills:
- basic literacy, numeracy, coding, digital skills, media and information literacy
- critical/creative thinking, teamwork, communication, socio-emotional and AI ethics skills

Evaluation criteria for AI curricula must cover:
- readiness of students/teachers; teacher training deficiencies
- curricular goals/content gaps; market needs additions
- intersectoral support; curriculum integration degree
- learning environments readiness; implementation quality

Republic of Korea (2019) defines four populations:
- general public (basic AI/data literacy, AI ethics)
- AI practitioners (apply AI in "AI + X" environments)
- AI professionals (develop platforms/systems)
- AI talents (develop new models/algorithms)
Schools: lower primary (experiential), higher primary (applied in core subjects), secondary (AI-centred schools).

### Building interdisciplinary core and cluster AI curricula for AI competency

- AI competency development spans formal national curriculum courses, extracurricular programmes, and informal learning via families/communities; private-sector programmes must comply with human-centred vision and ethical principles; providers targeting students, especially children, must be held accountable for safety and ethics.
- AI CFS builds on multidisciplinary knowledge: data, programming, computing structures, internet; computing/engineering thinking; scientific reasoning; human-centred mindset anchored in social and emotional skills.
- Countries with existing digital/ICT competency frameworks can adopt an adaptive approach, redefining digital competencies to cover AI-specific values, knowledge and skills.
- UNESCO report *K-12 AI curricula: A mapping of government-endorsed AI curricula* (2022b) identifies four integration strategies: AI as discrete subject; AI integrated into existing subjects (usually ICT); cross-curricular approaches across cluster subjects; AI as optional/extracurricular/co-curricular activity.
- Core AI curriculum should mobilize STEAM multidisciplinary foundations; cluster AI curriculum should integrate key AI competencies into learning outcomes at corresponding progression levels.
- **United Arab Emirates — Computing, Creative Design and Innovation (CCDI)**: established 2016 as technology-focused subject; revised 2020 to cover five domains: (1) computer science (computer systems, networks/internet, data/analysis, algorithms/programming, impacts of computing); (2) engineering principles and systems (electricity/electrons, robotics/systems, embedded systems); (3) design and innovation (entrepreneurship, engineering design process); (4) sustainability (sustainable society); (5) visual communication (graphics for design, CAD, design realization). Progressively integrated robotics, programming, 3D-modelling, electronics (UNESCO, 2024).

### Framing future-proofing and locally feasible AI domains as carriers of the curriculum

- Compulsory/elective decisions based on: foundational value, futureproofing potential, local feasibility.
- All curricula must include: humancentred mindset, AI ethics, AI techniques and applications.
- All students must reach the first two levels of these aspects; reaching the third level, 'Create', is less necessary, especially for AI system design.
- Exemplar AI techniques: human-coded decision trees, alpha-beta pruning and minimax, deep learning, generative AI.
- Subdomains: classical AI or 'rule-based AI', machine learning, deep learning, generative AI models.
- AI technology categories: computer vision, natural language processing, automated speech recognition, automated planning and scheduling (AI planning).
- AI tools must be agnostic to commercial brands and prevent bias against marginalized groups (gender, ability, socio-economic status, language, culture).
- Modular curriculum design recommended for dynamic updates.
- Competency-based education shifts from fixed time/flexible learning to flexible time/fixed learning (Patrick and Sturgis, 2017).
- Framework targets exit-level outcomes, not grade levels, using a scaffolded spiral curricular pattern.

### AI competency framework

- China's AI competency framework for AI subject teachers: developed by National Institute for Education, East China Normal University and Tencent (not government-driven).
- Six dimensions: understanding and awareness; basic knowledge; basic skills; problem-solving capability; teaching practices; ethics and security.
- Unlike UNESCO framework: targets AI teachers specifically; omits human-centred mindset and professional development; no progression levels.
- AI competency is a **three-helix bundle**: (1) social/emotional learning of values and ethical principles; (2) self-directed and collaborative construction of conceptual knowledge; (3) practical skills to apply and co-create AI tools.
- **Cohort-based** design groups students from different classes/grade levels sharing an AI course or interest domain.
- Domain-specific pedagogical needs: human-centred values require conflict-based opinion-taking and social construction; ethics learning uses case studies, scenario-based critical evaluations, collaborative rule-making; AI techniques require real AI tools, problem-based learning, scenario-based inquiry; AI system design simulates engineering projects (data planning, model architecture, training, prototyping, iteration).
- Hackathons and bootcamps validated as effective pedagogical methodologies.

### Pedagogical methodologies in the MIT curriculum on the ethics of AI for middle school students

Curriculum created by Blakeley H. Payne (2019), MIT Media Lab Personal Robots Group, directed by Cynthia Breazeal. Target: students aged 12–14, early in AI learning. Implemented online and/or offline. Integrated into the MIT DAILy Curriculum and *How to Train Your Robot: A Middle School AI and Ethics Curriculum*; research showed deliverability by teachers with limited CS background (Williams et al., 2021).

Learning cycle: orientation/information-gathering → conceptualization → investigation → prototype development (project-based). Discussion and reflection throughout.

Six core goals with example activities:

- **Understand basic mechanics of AI systems** (recognize AI in everyday life; algorithms as input–change–output; AI as algorithm with dataset, learning, prediction): *AI Bingo* worksheet (gamification for recall); write algorithm for peanut butter and jelly sandwich; identify AI systems on YouTube; build a classifier in Google's Teachable Machine using a biased dataset (cats/dogs) to experience bias.
- **Understand all technical systems are socio-technical, serving political agendas, not neutral**: create an ethical matrix of stakeholders and values for the sandwich; construct an ethical matrix around the YouTube Recommender Algorithm.
- **Recognize multiple stakeholders affected differently by a system**: reflect on stakeholders for GANs, emotional recognition, and speech-to-text software.
- **Apply technical understanding and stakeholder knowledge to determine a just goal**: redesign the YouTube algorithm for new goals, identifying datasets and design features (project-based, design thinking up to prototype stage).
- **Consider technology's impact on the world**: interact with technologies, respond to creative writing/discussion prompts on direct and extended impacts.

### Constructing competency-based assessments on the progression of key AI aspects

A competency-based assessment system for AI requires purpose and objectives, authentic tasks, benchmarking standards, and domain-appropriate criteria with a grading scale.

- **Criterion-referenced assessments** measure mastery against predefined standards, focusing on minimum mastery levels and using ipsative assessment to track progress.
- **Norm-referenced assessments** compare students to cohorts; performance should be disaggregated by age, gender, and demographic background for remedial support.
- **Performance-based assessment** measures overt behavioural performance, but must also assess **latent competencies** (human-centrality of mindset and ethics, transferability of knowledge, adaptivity of skills, creativity in AI system design).
- Assess both observable performance and latent competencies.
- Shift from rote learning to testing transferability, adaptivity, and creativity.
- Balance domain-specific and integrative AI competency assessments using authentic project-based testing.
- Configure tasks and grading scales aligned with progression levels: 'Understand' (comprehension of concepts/ethics), 'Apply' (problem-based practical skills, adaptivity), and 'Create' (synthesis, algorithmic programming, virtual/physical prototypes, testing/optimizing AI models, co-creation of AI).

### AI competency framework

Framework comprises three domains:

- **Ethics of AI**: scenario-based ethical value orientation; scenario-based ethical behaviour; scenario-based rule-making
- **AI techniques and applications**: problem-based AI knowledge and understanding; tool-based conceptual insights and transferable operation; task-based tool crafting
- **AI system design**: project-based design thinking; project-based system configuration; project-based iteration

AI system design assessment uses the virtual environment of **Teachable Machine** for a simulation project on design, training, testing and optimization. Projects must address real-world needs of promoting social inclusion and use data on local languages or cultural features when training AI models. Assessment must extend beyond paper-based testing to capture technological conceptualization, prototype creation, improvement processes, and technical expertise demonstrated in projects.

### AI Competency Framework — Assessment Tasks

The framework organizes assessment tasks along two competency aspects — **Human-centred mindset** and **Ethics of AI** — each mapped across three progression levels: **Understand**, **Apply**, and **Create**.

### Human-centred mindset

**Understand (1.1 Conflict-based opinion taking)**
- 1.1.0: Paper/computer test on 'Human agency'
- 1.1.1: Name a weakness of current AI in supporting decisions on values, social issues, and personal emotional reactions
- 1.1.2: What happens if humans don't take accountability in AI conceptualization and design?
- 1.1.3: Will machine agency surpass human agency?

**Apply (1.2 Conflict-based critical evaluation)**
- 1.2.0: Test on 'Human accountability'
- 1.2.1: Evaluate whether selected media reports of AI exceed genuine capabilities; AGI arrival by 2030 vs. experts saying it may never emerge
- 1.2.2: Assess whether a particular problem can/should be solved with AI methods

**Create (1.3 Conflict-based social interactions)**
- 1.3.0: Test on 'Social responsibility'
- 1.3.1: Analyse how AI systems affect environments and climate change; whether unlimited AI model training is justified
- 1.3.2: Critically reflect on AI's potential impact on safety, inclusion, equity, justice
- 1.3.3: Evaluate AI's effect on local economy and job market; inequality across global markets
- 1.3.4: Critically evaluate implications of wide AI adoption for inclusion and equity in local context

### Ethics of AI

**Understand (2.1 Scenario-based ethical value orientation)**
- 2.1.0: Test on 'Ethical principles'
- 2.1.1: Describe how personal online data are collected/used without consent
- 2.1.2: Describe how sensitive personal data may be collected through prompts or interactions with AI systems
- 2.1.3: Identify ethical issues around video-recommendation algorithms (YouTube, TikTok)

**Apply (2.2 Scenario-based ethical behaviours)**
- 2.2.0: Test on 'Safe and responsible use'
- 2.2.1: Explain why data security must be considered in AI development/use
- 2.2.2: Explain why data privacy must be considered
- 2.2.3: Describe the concept of explainable AI
- 2.2.4: Evaluate legal problems arising from AI-generated content or claiming it as one's own work

**Create (2.3 Scenario-based rule-making)**
- 2.3.0: Test on 'Co-creating ethical rules'
- 2.3.1: Evaluate existing AI regulations against UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of AI and/or the EU AI Act; or develop a proposal
- 2.3.2: Co-create ethical guidance for use of video-recommendation platforms or generative AI
- 2.3.3: Co-create ethical rules for safe/responsible AI use in schools and at home
- 2.3.4: Co-create regulatory rules for brain-computer interface (BCI) technology

### AI competency framework for students

The framework rests on three assumptions: education proactively shapes ethical, environmentally-friendly AI; students act as critical users, co-creators, and leaders in AI design; competencies converge human-centred mindset, internalized ethics, transferable knowledge, and future-proof thinking. Curricular integration spans science, technology, engineering, art, mathematics, social studies, and citizenship education. The framework specifies AI topics, pedagogical methods, learning sequences, and competence-based assessments.

## GLOSSAR

- **Human-centred mindset** — An aspect focusing on values, beliefs, and critical thinking applied to whether AI is fit for purpose and what responsibilities individuals and institutions should assume.
- **AI co-creators** — The framework's vision of students as active participants who shape AI technology rather than merely consume it.
- **progression levels** — The three-stage competency development sequence—Understand, Apply, Create—through which student AI competencies advance.
- **AI CFS** — AI Competency Framework for Students, structured around three aspects: Human-centred mindset, Ethics of AI, and AI techniques and applications.
- **UNESCO Beijing Consensus on AI and Education** — A 2019 agreement underlining the need to equip people with AI literacy across all layers of society.
- **human-centric approach** — A UNESCO approach advocating that AI design and use should serve human capabilities, protect dignity and agency, and promote justice and sustainability.
- **knowledge commons** — The shared knowledge resources defined by linguistic and cultural diversity that AI must respect.
- **ethics-by-design** — An approach integrating ethical considerations into the development and co-creation of AI systems from the outset.
- **bias-agnostic algorithms** — Algorithms selected to avoid favouring particular demographic groups in AI system outputs.
- **Ethics by design** — An approach requiring students to embed ethical considerations across all stages of the AI life cycle, from conceptualization through design, assessment, and use.
- **Embodied ethics** — A competency block in which students internalize ethical principles—do no harm, proportionality, non-discrimination, sustainability, human determination, and transparency—in their reflective practices and uses of AI.
- **AI problem scoping** — The starting point for AI innovation where students assess whether AI should be used and define boundaries, goals, and constraints before training a model.
- **AI literacy** — The ability to ensure proper and effective use of AI, based on foundational values, ethical principles, knowledge, and understanding.
- **unplugged and low-tech options** — Basic learning settings recommended for practising AI concepts without advanced technology.
- **Human agency** — The capacity of humans to control, determine, and make decisions in the design, provision, and use of AI systems.
- **Machine agency** — The extent to which AI systems autonomously perform tasks or make decisions, which may be necessary in specific contexts but must be balanced with human control.
- **European Union's AI Act** — A regulatory framework by the European Union that includes definitions of AI systems prohibited due to their potential to harm humans or undermine human agency.
- **Human accountability** — The legal and social obligation of AI creators, providers, and institutional users for the consequences and ethical risks of AI systems.
- **High-stakes decisions** — Determinations significantly affecting a person's life, such as assessing values, inferring emotions, or predicting aptitudes, where AI should not replace human determination.
- **AI society citizenship** — The capacity to build critical views on AI's impact on human societies and promote its design for inclusive and sustainable development.
- **AI life cycle** — The sequence of steps in creating and maintaining an AI system, over which humans must retain control and accountability.
- **AI + X** — Interdisciplinary environments where AI and software tools are applied in the labour market.
- **TVET** — Technical and vocational education and training, a sector targeted by AI competency development strategies.
- **core AI curricula** — A set of formal education AI courses providing inclusive learning opportunities for all students, particularly those without AI access outside school.
- **cluster AI curricula** — Existing subject curricula adapted or reformed to enhance their connections with AI competencies while retaining focus on other core competencies.
- **adaptive approach** — A strategy for countries with existing digital or ICT competency frameworks to integrate AI aspects by redefining digital competencies to cover AI-specific values, knowledge and skills.
- **humancentred mindset** — A core aspect of the AI curriculum deemed crucial for all students' lives and work in the AI era.
- **spiral curricular pattern** — A scaffolded learning design providing spaced and iterated engagement with foundational AI knowledge across all aspects.
- **exit-level outcomes** — The expected mastery levels that educational systems should seek to achieve for all students, rather than breaking down progression by grade level.
- **spiral design approach** — Curricular design clustering content around key topics revisited at increasing depth across age levels.
- **unplugged solutions** — Offline activities for teaching AI concepts without digital devices, developed by academic and non-profit organizations.
- **three-helix bundle** — The document's model of AI competency as three intertwined strands: values/ethics learning, conceptual knowledge construction, and practical skills.
- **cohort-based approach** — A pedagogical design organizing learning scenarios around a group of students from different classes or grade levels who share an AI course or domain interest.
- **ethical matrix** — A pedagogical tool for identifying stakeholders in a socio-technical system and their potentially conflicting interests and values.
- **confirmation design** — An activity structure where students are given a question and methodology to confirm a known result, used in guided experiential learning.
- **Criterion-referenced assessments** — Assessments measuring students' mastery level against predefined standards or benchmarking frameworks.
- **Ipsative assessment** — Assessment comparing a student's or cohort's performance against their own previous performance over time.
- **Latent competencies** — Underlying abilities such as the human-centrality of mindset and ethics, transferability of knowledge, adaptivity of skills, and creativity.
- **Teachable Machine** — A virtual environment used for simulation projects on AI system design, training, testing and optimization.
- **Conflict-based** — An assessment design approach that presents competing claims or tensions (e.g., media vs. expert views) to elicit critical evaluation and opinion formation.
