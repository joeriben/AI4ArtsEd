# Bias Analysis in Unconditional Image Generative Models

## PROFIL

The article "Bias Analysis in Unconditional Image Generative Models" was written by Xiaofeng Zhang, Michelle Lin, Simon Lacoste-Julien, Aaron Courville, and Yash Goyal, affiliated with Mila, Université de Montréal, and Samsung AI Lab, Montréal. It is a research paper aimed at the machine-learning research community and addresses how bias arises in unconditional image generative models—models that produce images without text prompts or class labels—by measuring the bias shift between training and generated distributions. Its central claims are that attribute bias shift does arise between training and generation distributions and that its size tracks how "spectrum-based" an attribute is; that BigGAN models show larger shifts than diffusion models even with comparable generation metrics; and that the density of the classifier's decision boundary predicts shift sensitivity well. The paper positions itself against three research strands: studies of social bias in text-to-image systems (minDALL-E, Karlo, Stable Diffusion v1.4/v2, DALL-E 2), which it finds confounded by pipeline components such as classifier-free guidance; works on bias shift between train and generation that rely on public Stable Diffusion models and LAION-5B subsets; and attribute-labeling approaches using CLIP, BLIP/BLIP-2 with VQA, or ViT with captioning, which it replaces with a classifier trained on the dataset under analysis. It contains a bias definition and evaluation framework, experiments on CelebA and DeepFashion with ablated diffusion models and BigGAN, an analysis of average attribute bias shift and its sensitivity to decision-boundary density, per-attribute results, conclusions with limitations and broader impact, and appendices with a mathematical proof, dataset details, training configurations, and a threshold justification building on Wu et al.'s study of inter-human annotation agreement. The findings are usable in machine learning and AI education concerned with algorithmic fairness, generative modeling, and model evaluation, particularly in situations where bias must be assessed separately from generation quality and diversity.

## WISSEN

### Abstract and Introduction (p. 1–2)

This paper by Xiaofeng Zhang, Michelle Lin, Simon Lacoste-Julien, Aaron Courville, and Yash Goyal (Mila, Université de Montréal; Samsung AI Lab, Montréal) investigates how bias arises in **unconditional** image generative models—models that produce images without text prompts or class labels. The authors define an attribute's bias as the gap between how often the attribute appears in the observed distribution and the share it should have in an ideal reference distribution. They train a set of unconditional generative models (diffusion models and GANs) and apply a common evaluation framework to measure the **bias shift** separating training and generated distributions. Their experiments reveal that the detected attribute shifts are small. They find that these shifts are sensitive to the attribute classifier used to label generated images, especially when its decision boundaries fall in high-density regions—a sensitivity typically seen in attributes whose values lie on a spectrum rather than being binary. The authors conclude that this highlights the need for more representative labeling practices, greater scrutiny of the shortcomings of evaluation frameworks, and recognition of the socially complex nature of attributes when evaluating bias.

The motivation is that prior literature is inconclusive: some studies report bias amplification in Stable Diffusion relative to its LAION-5B training data, while others trace bias shift to mismatches between the captions used in training and the prompts given to the model. By restricting attention to unconditional models, the authors remove confounding factors such as classifier-free guidance (CFG), which curtails diversity in outputs and may itself affect bias. Their central question is how bias shift behaves between the training and the generation distributions.

Their main findings are: (1) attribute bias shift does arise between training and generation distributions, and its size tracks how "spectrum-based" the attribute is; (2) BigGAN models show larger attribute bias shifts than diffusion models even when generation metrics such as FID are comparable; (3) the density of the classifier's decision boundary predicts shift sensitivity well.

### Related Works (p. 2–3)

The authors position the work against three research strands:

- **Bias in image generation**: earlier studies of social bias in text-to-image (T2I) systems (examining minDALL-E, Karlo, Stable Diffusion v1.4/v2, DALL-E 2) often conclude that models fail to mirror U.S. labor statistics. Because T2I pipelines contain many components that could contribute to bias, this paper isolates the unconditional generator itself.
- **Bias shift between train and generation**: few works compare generated outputs against training data; existing efforts rely on public Stable Diffusion models and on subsets of LAION-5B chosen via image-prompt similarity or caption keywords. The authors instead train generative models on datasets whose attributes are labeled, which makes bias estimates dependable on both sides.
- **Bias-related attribute label prediction**: generated images must be labeled before bias can be computed. Prior work uses CLIP representation space, BLIP/BLIP-2 with visual question answering (VQA), or ViT with image captioning—but pre-trained models carry biases of their own. Here, the classifier is trained on the very dataset under analysis.

### Bias Evaluation Method (p. 3–4)

**Bias definition.** Following prior work on T2I systems, bias is present when the share of an attribute among generated images deviates from real-world statistics. Formally, for a binary attribute C, bias is the difference between the probability of C appearing in the observed distribution and its expected proportion in an ideal reference distribution, P_ideal(C). The data probability P_data(C) can be estimated by P_train(C) or P_val(C). (K-way attributes can be handled by converting each into K one-vs-all binary attributes.)

**Bias shift evaluation framework.** The pipeline runs as follows: (1) train unconditional generative models using only training-set images, without ground-truth labels; (2) generate 10,000 images per checkpoint during training; (3) fine-tune an attribute classifier on the training split and validate it against ground-truth labels on the validation split; (4) apply this classifier to label the training, validation, and generation sets; (5) compute bias shift metrics from the classifier-predicted labels. Using one classifier on both data and generation keeps the classification protocol consistent, and estimating the data distribution with P_val(C) avoids overfitting to the training set.

**Attribute bias shift** is the per-attribute difference separating generation from training data; the ideal reference probability cancels out, so it equals the change in attribute frequency from training data to generated data. When it is near 0, the two distributions carry the same degree of attribute bias.

**Average attribute bias shift (ABS)** is proposed as the mean of the per-attribute bias shifts over all attributes considered, expressed as a percentage. The authors acknowledge that averaging can mask biases, but argue it is acceptable here because each attribute's shift is always positive and the attributes studied are generally non-protected (color, shape, texture).

### Experiments: Setup and Backbone Model Performance (p. 4–6)

**Datasets.** Two real image datasets: **CelebA** (more than 200,000 celebrity face images carrying 40 binary attribute annotations each, spanning fine details like earrings and pointy nose through broader features like hair color, gender, age) and **DeepFashion** (over 800,000 fashion images; a subset carrying 26 fine-grained attribute labels is used). Official train/validation/test splits are followed. Images are center-cropped and resized to 128×128; attributes that are not visually grounded after cropping (e.g., Wearing Necklace, Wearing Necktie) or that would hurt quality (Blurry) are excluded.

**Backbone models.** Following Dhariwal and Nichol's setup, unconditional ablated diffusion models (ADMs) are trained at varying sizes by adjusting U-Net bottleneck channels (32 for tiny, 64 for small, 256 for large). 10,000 images are generated per checkpoint with 100 inference steps (DDIM). Classifiers use ResNeXt-50 and Swin Transformer backbones (the main paper reports ResNeXt-50 results). A BigGAN model is trained with recommended settings for comparison.

**Evaluation metrics.** FID and KID measure generation quality; FLD (Feature Likelihood Divergence) together with the generalization gap gauges memorization, using DINOv2 as feature extractor. FID and KID fall to low values, FLD matches the conventional metrics in indicating no serious memorization, and generalization gaps that are positive or only mildly negative show no overfitting. Classifier accuracy on validation exceeds 80% for most attributes, averaging 91.7% (CelebA) and 90.5% (DeepFashion).

### Average Attribute Bias Shift Evaluation (p. 6–7)

ABS stays noticeable even once generation metrics are low, so the shifts cannot be ignored. Bias metrics do not track generation metrics: for CelebA, bias metrics fluctuate after 300K steps while generation metrics plateau; for DeepFashion, generation metrics keep improving while ABS holds steady with minimal increases after roughly 200K steps. This misalignment shows that superior generation metrics do not imply lower bias—bias must be evaluated separately from quality and diversity.

To confirm that sampling 10,000 images suffices, the authors compare ABS between 10K-image validation subsets and the entire validation set (CelebA): the generation set's ABS far exceeds the variance introduced by sampling, confirming the observed shifts are real.

Attributes are sorted into **spectrum-based** and **non-spectrum-based** categories. Non-spectrum-based attributes converge to small ABS: 0.71% (CelebA) versus 0.98% (DeepFashion). Spectrum-based attributes show markedly larger ABS, bottoming out at 3.25% (CelebA) and 4.73% (DeepFashion).

### Sensitivity of Attribute Bias Shifts to the Decision Boundary (p. 7–8)

The key explanatory factor is the density of the classifier's decision boundary. Plots of the classifier's pre-sigmoid logits distributions show that attributes with large shifts have boundaries sitting in **high-density** regions, while small-shift attributes have boundaries where density is low. An attribute is labeled spectrum-based if the validation distribution's density at the boundary exceeds 0.01, and non-spectrum-based otherwise. Attributes on a spectrum react more strongly to distribution changes: where density is low, moving probability mass across the boundary is difficult. Empirically, the distribution changes between training and generation carry only a small earth mover's distance (EMD)—substantial mode reweighting would produce a large EMD.

The categorization (Table 1): CelebA has 22 spectrum-based attributes (including Rosy Cheeks, Big Nose, No Beard, Arched Eyebrows, Black Hair, Brown Hair, Heavy Makeup, Attractive, Smiling, Wearing Lipstick, Young, Oval Face) and 14 non-spectrum-based ones (including 5-o-Clock Shadow, Bangs, Eyeglasses, Bald, Wearing Hat, Male, Blond Hair, Gray Hair, Mustache, Pale Skin, Goatee). DeepFashion has 19 spectrum-based attributes (including Floral, Graphic, Solid, Long sleeve, Sleeveless, Knit, Cotton, Maxi length, Crew neckline, V neckline, Loose, Tight, Conventional) and 7 non-spectrum-based ones (Striped, Pleated, Leather, Faux, Square neckline, Lattice, Denim).

### Attribute Bias Shift in Different Generative Models (p. 7–8)

Comparing model sizes and architectures: the tiny diffusion model fails to produce realistic images and is excluded from bias analysis. The small diffusion model has worse FLD than the large one yet much larger ABS for both attribute categories; its ABS even rises after 300K steps without FLD change. BigGAN achieves FLD comparable to the large diffusion model but considerably larger ABS—possibly due to GAN mode collapse. BigGAN's shifts end up similar to the small diffusion model's. Images from BigGAN and the small diffusion model look more "washed out," with fewer variations and less detail than the large diffusion model's.

### Per-Attribute Bias Shift (p. 9)

Probabilities of spectrum-based attributes in generated data generally diverge from classifier-predicted validation probabilities, producing the observed shifts. Spectrum-based attributes fluctuate more during training (e.g., Young on CelebA and Solid on DeepFashion still fluctuate after 300K steps), suggesting extra caution with such attributes. Training runs with three random seeds on CelebA show randomness across seeds but convergence to the same attribute probabilities, so single-seed results are reported elsewhere.

### Conclusion, Limitations, and Broader Impact (p. 9)

The paper investigates how the inductive biases of unconditional image generators shift attribute distributions, concluding: (1) bias shifts arise between the training and generated distributions; (2) picking the checkpoint that scores best on generation metrics offers no guarantee of minimal bias shifts; (3) BigGAN and small diffusion models show larger shifts than large diffusion models despite comparable generation metrics. The authors hope this analysis of unconditional models enables systematic study of further bias sources—conditioning, guidance, pretrained modules.

**Limitations**: the analysis relies on classifier-predicted labels in a controlled setting, which may not capture the ambiguity with which attributes are interpreted in the real world; binary annotations limit spectrum-based attributes; findings may not generalize beyond unconditional models trained on CelebA and DeepFashion.

**Broader impact**: the findings should not be taken to mean that algorithmic bias is a non-issue. CelebA offers a curated, stylized representation of faces under a binary gender schema, encoding a simplified "worldview" whose labels (e.g., what qualifies as "smiling" or "attractive") reflect specific cultural and aesthetic norms; the authors caution against reading its results as reflective of broader societal dynamics.

### Appendices: Proof, Datasets, Training Details, and Threshold Justification (p. 14–23)

- **Appendix A** proves mathematically that for a distribution translated by δ, bias shift is governed by the density of the distribution in the δ-neighborhood around the decision boundary: low-density boundaries yield small shift under translation.
- **Appendix B** details the datasets: CelebA covers 10,000 celebrities with 20 images each; the training set holds the first 8,000 identities (160K images), validation 1,000 identities (20K), test 1,000 identities (20K). DeepFashion's coarse 1,000-attribute annotations are noisy (most attributes under 1% positive samples); the 26-attribute fine-grained subset is used, with a random (not identity-based) split.
- **Appendix D** gives training details: ADMs trained on NVIDIA A100 40GB GPUs, batch size 16 per GPU across 8 GPUs, learning rate 1e-4, batch size 128, 256 channels, 2 res blocks, dropout 0.3, 1000 diffusion steps, DDIM 100 inference steps, FP16. DDIM yields fewer bias shifts than the improved diffusion sampler (which has slightly better FLD but worse FID/KID). Classifiers: ResNeXt fine-tunes the last 6 layers plus the classification layer; Swin Transformer fine-tunes the last 14 layers of stage-3 and all of stage-4; AdamW with learning rate 0.001; class imbalance handled by reweighting cross-entropy loss by the positive/negative sample ratio. Both classifier backbones yield similar bias shifts, showing the findings are not architecture-dependent. Under the Swin Transformer classifier, 5-o-Clock Shadow, Bangs, and Blond Hair move into the spectrum-based category—their ResNeXt densities sit near the 0.01 threshold. A zero-shot VQA evaluation using Llama 3.2-Vision 11B performs poorly (average accuracy 77.39%, dropping below 70% on several attributes, among them attractive, heavy makeup, and young).
- **Appendix E** justifies the 0.01 threshold via Wu et al.'s study of inter-human agreement in CelebA annotations (two annotators labeling 1,000 images; attributes grouped by agreement ≥95%, 85–95%, ≤85%). The categorizations align strongly; pale skin is the sole notable outlier, and no beard and mouth slightly open—despite high agreement—show large errors relative to ground truth, supporting their spectrum-based classification.
- **Appendices D.2, F, G** provide per-attribute classifier performance tables, per-attribute probability curves during training, and sample generated images for the large, small, and tiny diffusion models and BigGAN on CelebA, and the large diffusion model on DeepFashion.

## GLOSSAR

- **Bias (of an attribute)** — the gap between the probability of the attribute appearing in the observed distribution and the share it should have in an ideal reference distribution.
- **Bias shift** — the change in attribute bias between the training distribution and the generated distribution; equivalently, the change in attribute frequency from training data to generated data.
- **Attribute bias shift** — the per-attribute bias shift separating generation from training data, computed from classifier-predicted labels.
- **Average attribute bias shift (ABS)** — the mean of the per-attribute bias shifts over all attributes considered, expressed as a percentage; measures overall bias shift magnitude between training and generated distributions.
- **Spectrum-based attribute** — an attribute whose classifier decision boundary sits in a high-density region (density above 0.01) of the validation distribution; its values lie on a spectrum rather than being binary, and it shows larger, more sensitive bias shifts.
- **Non-spectrum-based attribute** — an attribute whose classifier decision boundary sits in a low-density region (density at most 0.01); it exhibits small bias shifts under distribution changes.
- **Decision boundary density** — the density of the classifier's pre-sigmoid logit distribution at the decision boundary; predicts shift sensitivity well.
- **Unconditional generative model** — a generative model trained without labels, text conditioning, or guidance, used to isolate the generator's own contribution to bias.
- **Ideal reference distribution** — the distribution against which bias is measured, whose attribute proportion P_ideal(C) cancels out in bias shift computation.
- **Classifier-predicted labels** — attribute labels assigned to training, validation, and generated images by the same fine-tuned classifier, keeping the classification protocol consistent across distributions.
